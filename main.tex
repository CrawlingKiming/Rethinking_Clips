\title{\bf When Should We Clip in RLVR?\\
Effective Sample Size as a Reliability Criterion}
\author{Dongkyu Derek Cho, Thomas Voice}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Clipping is widely treated as necessary for stable policy optimization, yet it
can also remove useful learning signal.  This tension is pronounced in
reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, where permissive updates often
learn quickly but may fail after the policy moves too far from its rollout data.
We ask when clipping becomes useful, rather than whether it should be used at
all.  Our answer is effective sample size, which measures how broadly the
current update is supported by the rollout batch.  We show that effective
sample size controls gradient estimation error, that this error weakens a
one-step policy-improvement guarantee, and that clipping is beneficial only
when its variance reduction offsets the bias it introduces.  This analysis
suggests a reliability-gated rule: retain the permissive update while support is
broad, and clip after support deteriorates.  In an exactly evaluable verifier
task embedded in an RLVR-style optimization loop, the proposed gate preserves
fast early learning and reaches a fixed reward target with fewer verifier
responses than either static choice.  These results support effective sample
size as a practical criterion for deciding when to clip.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Policy optimization on fixed rollout data faces a basic mismatch: the policy
being updated is no longer the policy that generated the data.  PPO addresses
this mismatch with a clipped surrogate that suppresses selected samples once
their likelihood ratios cross a prescribed boundary \citep{schulman2017ppo}.
The same mechanism is now standard in reinforcement learning with verifiable
rewards (RLVR), where an expensive rollout batch is followed by several policy
updates \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}.  Because the mismatch grows during these
updates, clipping is often treated as a necessary condition for stable
training.

RLVR practice reveals that this view is incomplete.  Clipping can remove
gradients from low-probability tokens that carry useful new behavior, slowing
early learning and contributing to entropy collapse
\citep{yu2025dapo,park2025clipping}.  More permissive objectives can avoid this
loss and improve reward faster, yet they may fail later in training; the
motivating CISPO trajectory in Figure~\ref{fig:llm-motivation} exhibits this
pattern \citep{minimax2025m1}.  These observations do not rank clipping and
permissive optimization globally.  They indicate that the preferable update
changes as a rollout batch becomes less representative of the current policy.
The central question is therefore not whether to clip, but when clipping
becomes necessary.

Common diagnostics do not locate this change of regime.  A clipping threshold
specifies how to modify an individual sample, an update count records how long
the batch has been optimized, and a divergence summarizes how far the policy
has moved \citep{qi2026dppo}.  None of these quantities directly measures
whether the fixed batch still supports an accurate gradient for the current
policy.  That question depends on the concentration of the sequence importance
weights across the entire batch.

Sequence effective sample size (ESS) measures this concentration.  ESS is high
when support is distributed across many responses and low when only a few
responses carry most of the importance weight.  Our central thesis is that the
absence of clipping is not inherently unstable.  A permissive update remains
statistically reliable while its rollout batch provides sufficient effective
support.  Clipping becomes useful after that support deteriorates, when the
variance it removes can justify the bias it introduces.

Establishing this thesis requires a bridge from coverage to optimization.  We
derive the exact mean-squared error of the permissive gradient and separate its
dependence on sequence ESS from the scale of the associated gradient
contributions.  We then show that gradient error enters a one-step
policy-improvement guarantee.  Finally, we differentiate the PPO surrogate to
show that clipping is an advantage-dependent gradient mask, not global ratio
truncation, and derive the exact bias--variance condition under which a modified
gradient is preferable.  These results motivate a simple decision rule: retain
the permissive gradient while ESS is high and activate a safeguard when ESS
falls.

We test every link in an exactly evaluable verifier problem before transferring
the rule to language-model training.  The controlled task follows the rollout
and minibatch order of RLVR while exposing the population gradient and reward.
Across 100 paired runs, ESS predicts both gradient error and the sign of the
resulting reward change.  A prespecified $0.1$ gate reaches a fixed reward target
with 25\% fewer verifier responses than always using the permissive gradient and
40\% fewer than always applying the PPO mask.  Section~\ref{sec:llm-experiments}
specifies the matched LLM experiment needed to test whether the same regime
change appears under PPO, Clip-Higher, and DPPO safeguards.

\subsection{Related perspectives}

\paragraph{Clipping in RLVR.}
PPO introduced clipping as a local policy-update mechanism
\citep{schulman2017ppo}, and GRPO carried this surrogate into RLVR
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}.  Recent methods expose the cost of applying the
same constraint throughout training.  DAPO relaxes the upper boundary,
Clip-Low changes entropy behavior, CISPO retains gradients beyond the PPO
boundary, and DPPO replaces ratio clipping with a divergence-based constraint
\citep{yu2025dapo,park2025clipping,minimax2025m1,qi2026dppo}.  These methods
change the intervention; our question is when an intervention is statistically
warranted.

\paragraph{Reliability under policy mismatch.}
Importance-sampling analyses use second moments and ESS to quantify coverage
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  Recent LLM methods likewise adapt to
weight concentration, ESS, or sequence-level mismatch
\citep{zheng2025m2po,shen2026vespo,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}.  Our analysis
adds the missing optimization link: it connects sequence ESS to gradient MSE,
then connects that MSE to policy improvement and the clipping crossover.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item We derive an exact connection between sequence ESS and the mean-squared
error of a fixed-rollout policy-gradient estimator.
\item We connect gradient error to certified policy improvement and characterize
clipping as a conditional bias--variance decision.
\item We test the complete mechanism in an RLVR-style verifier task and specify
a matched LLM evaluation across PPO, Clip-Higher, and DPPO safeguards.
\end{enumerate}

The next section states the fixed-rollout problem in which this reliability
question can be analyzed exactly.

\section{Preliminaries: fixed-rollout policy optimization}
\label{sec:setting}

RLVR alternates between data collection and optimization.  A rollout policy
generates a batch of complete responses, and a changing current policy is then
updated on that fixed batch.  We analyze one such optimization phase and keep
the rollout policy explicit throughout.

\subsection{Population target and sampled update}

Let $X\sim\nu$ be a prompt, let $Q(\cdot\mid X)$ be the rollout policy, and let
$R(X,Y)$ be the terminal verifier reward of a complete response.  During the
optimization phase, the policy of interest is $P_\theta$, with objective
$J(\theta):=\E_{P_\theta}[R]$.  Assuming $P_\theta\ll Q$, this target can be
evaluated from rollout data using the sequence likelihood ratio
\begin{equation}
  W_\theta(X,Y)
  :=\frac{P_\theta(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)},
  \qquad
  \E_Q[W_\theta\mid X]=1.
  \label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}

The policy gradient separates the response-level learning signal from the
distribution mismatch.  Let $b(X)$ be a detached prompt-only baseline, set
$A=R-b(X)$, and define the current-policy score and gradient contribution by
\begin{equation}
  S_\theta:=\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y\mid X),
  \qquad
  H_\theta:=A S_\theta.
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
Assuming that differentiation and expectation can be interchanged, the target
gradient is
\begin{align}
  g(\theta):=\nabla J(\theta)
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[R S_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-1}\\
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[(R-b(X))S_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-2}\\
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[H_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-3}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta].
  \label{eq:true-gradient}
\end{align}
The first line is the score-function identity.  The second uses
$\E_{P_\theta}[b(X)S_\theta\mid X]
=b(X)\nabla_\theta\sum_yP_\theta(y\mid X)=0$.  The third substitutes
\eqref{eq:score}, and the fourth changes measure from $P_\theta$ to $Q$.
Given $N$ independent prompt-response pairs from the rollout distribution, the
permissive importance-weighted estimator is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_W(\theta)
  :=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N W_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}.
  \label{eq:raw-estimator}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:true-gradient} identifies the desired update, whereas
Equation~\eqref{eq:raw-estimator} describes what the fixed batch can estimate.
The estimator is unbiased at fixed $\theta$, but its accuracy can deteriorate as
$P_\theta$ moves into responses that are poorly represented under $Q$.  We next
quantify this loss of coverage.

\subsection{Effective support of the rollout distribution}

Sequence ESS summarizes how much support the rollout distribution provides for
the current policy.  Assuming $\E_Q[W_\theta^2]<\infty$, define the normalized
population ESS by
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\theta:=\frac{1}{\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
  =\exp\{-D_2(P_\theta\|Q)\}.
  \label{eq:population-ess}
\end{equation}
Because $\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$, its relation to ratio dispersion is exact:
\begin{align}
  \E_Q[(W_\theta-1)^2]
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta^2]-2\E_Q[W_\theta]+1
    \label{eq:weight-variance-proof-1}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta^2]-1
    \label{eq:weight-variance-proof-2}\\
  &=\rho_\theta^{-1}-1.
    \label{eq:weight-variance}
\end{align}
The first line expands the square, the second uses $\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$, and the
third uses Equation~\eqref{eq:population-ess}.  Hence $N\rho_\theta$ is the
population effective sequence count, consistent with the second-moment
coverage principle used in importance-sampling policy optimization
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  When $\rho_\theta$ is near one, the batch
retains nearly all of its nominal support.  When it approaches zero, a batch of
$N$ responses behaves as if it contained far fewer effective observations.

ESS measures ratio concentration, not the size of the gradient contributions
attached to those ratios.  The theoretical analysis begins by separating these
two quantities and then asks whether their joint effect is large enough to
change the preferred policy update.

\section{Theoretical analysis}
\label{sec:theory}

Section~\ref{sec:setting} reduced fixed-rollout optimization to a sampled
importance-weighted gradient and a measure of its effective support.  This
section determines when that gradient is reliable, why its error affects policy
improvement, and when clipping can reduce that error.  The argument follows one
causal chain: poor coverage raises gradient error, gradient error weakens the
policy update, and clipping becomes preferable only when its variance reduction
repays its bias.  This chain gives ESS an operational role rather than treating
it as another diagnostic of policy drift.  All proofs are given line by line in
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} so that the main argument remains visible.

\subsection{When the permissive gradient is reliable}
\label{sec:raw}

The permissive estimator targets the desired on-policy gradient without bias,
but unbiasedness alone does not make a fixed batch reliable.  A small number of
large ratios can dominate the average.  ESS measures this loss of coverage, but
the resulting error also depends on the size of the associated gradient
contributions.  We isolate that second factor through
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}
  :=\frac{\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]}
          {\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}.
  \label{eq:gradient-scale}
\end{equation}
The quantity $G_{2,\theta}$ is the squared gradient scale under the part of the
rollout distribution emphasized by large ratios.  Separating this scale from
coverage yields the paper's reliability bridge.

\begin{theorem}[ESS reliability bridge]
\label{thm:raw-mse}
Assume $\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]<\infty$.  For fixed $\theta$,
$\widehat g_W$ is unbiased and
\begin{align}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  &=\frac1N\left\{
    \E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right\}
  \label{eq:raw-mse}\\
  &=\frac1N\left(
    \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{\rho_\theta}-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right)
  \label{eq:ess-factorization}\\
  &\le \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}.
  \label{eq:ess-bridge}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

The factorization has a direct interpretation.  At fixed $G_{2,\theta}$, the permissive-gradient
error scales inversely with the effective sequence count $N\rho_\theta$.
Neither the number of optimization steps nor parameter distance is fundamental
in this statement.  They matter when they concentrate the ratios or enlarge the
weighted gradient scale.  The next question is whether this statistical error
has an optimization consequence.

\subsection{Why gradient error matters for policy improvement}
\label{sec:mse-improvement}

Smoothness turns gradient reliability into a policy-improvement statement.
Theorem~7 of
\citet{papini2022smoothing} shows that an $L$-smooth policy objective satisfies
the following lower bound for any feasible parameter step $d$:
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta+d)-J(\theta)
  \ge g(\theta)^\top d-\frac{L}{2}\|d\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:smooth-policy-improvement}
\end{equation}
The linear term rewards alignment with the true gradient, while the quadratic
term penalizes an inaccurate or excessively large step.  The following result
combines both effects into gradient MSE.  It therefore compares permissive,
clipped, and truncated estimators on the same optimization scale.  Related
analyses use gradient second moments for policy-gradient convergence
\citep{yuan2022general}, gradient MSE for off-policy efficiency
\citep{kallus2020efficient}, and joint bias and variance control for biased
gradients \citep{driggs2022biased}.

\begin{theorem}[Gradient MSE controls certified policy improvement]
\label{thm:mse-improvement}
Fix $\theta$ and let $g=g(\theta)$.  Let $\widehat g$ be any, possibly biased,
gradient estimator with finite second moment.  Suppose $J$ is $L$-smooth on
every line segment from $\theta$ to $\theta+\eta\widehat g$, and that these
updates are feasible.  For $0<\eta\le 1/L$,
\begin{equation}
  \E\!\left[J(\theta+\eta\widehat g)-J(\theta)\right]
  \ge \frac{\eta}{2}
  \left\{\|g\|_2^2-\E\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2\right\}.
  \label{eq:mse-improvement}
\end{equation}
Consequently, $\E\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2<\|g\|_2^2$ is sufficient for positive
expected policy improvement.
\end{theorem}

For two estimators used with the same learning rate, an MSE reduction of
$\delta$ improves the
certified lower bound by $\eta\delta/2$.  The statement compares guarantees,
not every realized step, because the smoothness inequality need not be tight.
Substituting the ESS factorization yields the promised consequence of effective
coverage.

\begin{corollary}[ESS certificate for an unclipped update]
\label{cor:ess-improvement}
Under Theorems~\ref{thm:raw-mse} and \ref{thm:mse-improvement},
\begin{equation}
  \E\!\left[J(\theta+\eta\widehat g_W)-J(\theta)\right]
  \ge \frac{\eta}{2}
  \left\{
    \left(1+\frac1N\right)\|g\|_2^2
    -\frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:ess-improvement-certificate}
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}<(N+1)\rho_\theta\|g\|_2^2
  \label{eq:positive-ess-update}
\end{equation}
is sufficient for positive expected policy improvement.
\end{corollary}

The corollary supplies the missing optimization meaning of ESS: at fixed signal
and weighted gradient scale,
higher ESS gives a stronger one-step improvement certificate.  It does not
produce a universal numerical threshold, because reliability also depends on
$G_{2,\theta}$ and the gradient signal.  To understand when ESS can nevertheless
be informative in RLVR, we next examine the remaining scale term.

\subsection{Why RLVR makes the scale term manageable}

The weighted gradient scale combines two factors: the advantage and the policy
score.  RLVR naturally controls the first factor because verifier rewards are
bounded and group-normalized advantages have finite range.  A score bound or an
empirical scale estimate is still required for the second factor.

\begin{proposition}[A sufficient bound on $G_{2,\theta}$]
\label{prop:g2-bound}
If $|A|\le B_A$ and $\|S_\theta\|_2\le B_S$ almost surely under $Q$, then
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}\le B_A^2B_S^2.
  \label{eq:g2-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

Common RLVR constructions make the advantage factor small.  If the verifier
reward is binary and a prompt-only baseline satisfies $b(X)\in[0,1]$, then
$|A|=|R-b(X)|\le1$.  Outcome-level GRPO instead standardizes rewards within a
group of $K$ responses \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}.  Its standardized
advantages also have a deterministic finite-group bound whenever their sample
variance is nonzero.

\begin{proposition}[Standardized group-advantage bound]
\label{prop:grpo-bound}
Let $d_i=r_i-\bar r$, let $v=K^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^K d_j^2>0$, and let
$A_i=d_i/\sqrt v$.  Then $|A_i|\le\sqrt{K-1}$ for every $i$.
\end{proposition}

These results clarify both the value and the limit of advantage normalization.
It controls one factor in $G_{2,\theta}$, while the score still requires either a
bound or an empirical estimate.  Group normalization also couples the $K$
responses, so Theorem~\ref{thm:raw-mse} cannot be applied response by response
without a group-level sampling argument.  The binary-reward,
prompt-baseline case satisfies the theorem directly; the GRPO result controls
scale but does not supply an independence justification.

\begin{remark}[Pointwise scope]
The expectation is over a fresh batch at fixed $\theta$.  During same-batch
optimization, $\theta$ depends on the fixed rollout data.  Extending
\eqref{eq:ess-bridge} uniformly over that adaptive path requires additional
stability or complexity control.
\end{remark}

The analysis has now linked effective coverage to gradient error and gradient
error to policy improvement.  This link explains why a permissive update can
work while support remains broad.  It does not yet show that clipping is the
right response when support deteriorates.  That comparison requires the exact
gradient induced by the clipped objective.

\subsection{What clipping changes}
\label{sec:clipping}

Low ESS indicates that an intervention may be useful, but it does not establish
that a particular intervention is better.  PPO clipping can remove unstable
sample contributions, yet it changes the target gradient at the same time.  We
derive that change explicitly because clipping is often confused with globally
truncating the importance ratio.

\subsubsection{PPO clipping as an advantage-sign gradient mask}

For a detached advantage $A$, the per-sample PPO surrogate is
\begin{equation}
  \ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
  :=\min\left\{
    W_\theta A,
    \operatorname{clip}(W_\theta,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:ppo-objective}
\end{equation}
Clipping this scalar objective does not cap the gradient coefficient.  Its
derivative instead masks samples according to advantage sign.

\begin{proposition}[PPO clipping is advantage-sign gradient masking]
\label{prop:ppo-mask}
Away from the two clipping boundaries, define
\begin{equation}
  M_\epsilon
  :=\mathbf{1}\{A\ge0, W_\theta\le1+\epsilon\}
   +\mathbf{1}\{A<0, W_\theta\ge1-\epsilon\}.
  \label{eq:ppo-mask}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_\theta\ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
  =W_\theta M_\epsilon H_\theta.
  \label{eq:ppo-gradient}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

PPO therefore removes an upper-ratio gradient only for nonnegative advantages
and a lower-ratio gradient only for negative advantages.  In particular, a
large ratio with negative advantage remains active.  PPO clipping is a
directional mask, not a global bound on importance coefficients.

This distinction reveals the relevant trade-off.  The mask is useful only when
the variance it removes is worth the bias it introduces.  To express that
comparison once for PPO and other interventions, let
$C_\theta$ be any scalar coefficient for which $C_\theta H_\theta$ has a finite
second moment.  Define
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_C:=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N C_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i},
  \qquad
  b_C:=\E_Q[(C_\theta-W_\theta)H_\theta],
  \label{eq:modified-estimator}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma_W:=\Cov_Q(W_\theta H_\theta),
  \qquad
  \Sigma_C:=\Cov_Q(C_\theta H_\theta).
  \label{eq:bias-covariance}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{The estimator crossover}

The next result gives the exact risk of the modified coefficient $C_\theta$
relative to the permissive coefficient $W_\theta$.

\begin{theorem}[Exact estimator crossover]
\label{thm:crossover}
For fixed $\theta$ and finite second moments,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  =\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  =\|b_C\|_2^2+\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C).
  \label{eq:two-risks}
\end{equation}
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  <\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  \iff
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C)
  >N\|b_C\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The crossover condition states the entire reason to clip: the removed
covariance must exceed the squared bias of
the modified gradient, scaled by batch size.  For PPO, the coefficient is
$C_\theta^{\mathrm{PPO}}=W_\theta M_\epsilon$.
Its exact bias is
\begin{align}
  b_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}
  &=\E_Q[(W_\theta M_\epsilon-W_\theta)H_\theta]
    \label{eq:ppo-bias-1}\\
  &=-\E_Q[W_\theta(1-M_\epsilon)H_\theta].
    \label{eq:ppo-bias-2}
\end{align}
Theorem~\ref{thm:crossover} therefore compares the covariance removed by PPO's
sign-specific mask with the squared bias of the masked gradient.  Combining the theorem with
Theorem~\ref{thm:mse-improvement}, the difference between the modified and raw
MSE-based improvement certificates is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\eta}{2}\left\{
    \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
    -\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:improvement-crossover}
\end{equation}
Thus Equation~\eqref{eq:crossover} determines which estimator has the stronger
one-step certificate, not merely which estimator has lower gradient risk.

\subsubsection{Ratio truncation bias trade-off}

Ratio truncation changes the importance coefficient after the gradient has been
specified, so it is distinct from the derivative of the PPO objective.  For
$c>0$, define
\begin{equation}
  C_c^{\mathrm{tr}}:=\min(W_\theta,c),
  \qquad
  T_c:=(W_\theta-c)_+,
  \qquad
  \tau_c:=\E_Q[T_c].
  \label{eq:upper-truncation}
\end{equation}
The contribution above $c$ is then $cH_\theta$, not zero.  This is a detached
importance-weight estimator, not the derivative of the PPO surrogate.

\begin{corollary}[Bias and MSE of upper-ratio truncation]
\label{cor:upper-truncation}
Suppose $\|H_\theta\|_2\le H_{\max}$ almost surely.  The detached estimator
$\widehat g_c=N^{-1}\sum_i C_{c,i}^{\mathrm{tr}}H_{\theta,i}$ satisfies
\begin{align}
  b_c&=-\E_Q[T_cH_\theta],
  &\|b_c\|_2&\le H_{\max}\tau_c,
  \label{eq:cap-bias}\\
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_c)
  &\le H_{\max}^2\left(\tau_c^2+\frac{c}{N}\right).
  \label{eq:cap-mse-bound}
\end{align}
\end{corollary}

The result isolates the appeal of upper truncation.  It replaces the raw
second-moment term by $c/N$, while the introduced bias remains small when the
tail excess $\tau_c$ is small.  The cap is therefore a tunable estimator
parameter, not part of the paper's central claim.  Truncated importance ratios
also appear in ACER with
bias correction \citep{wang2017acer} and in the V-trace correction used by
IMPALA \citep{espeholt2018impala}; recent LLM methods likewise control extreme
weight moments \citep{zheng2025m2po,fakoor2026p3o}.  These precedents motivate
truncation as a separate importance-sampling device.  They do not make it
equivalent to PPO clipping.

The theory now yields a complete decision logic.  When $G_{2,\theta}$ is
controlled, high ESS certifies a reliable permissive gradient and a stronger
policy-improvement bound.  As ESS falls, permissive-gradient error grows.
Clipping becomes preferable only after the variance it removes exceeds the bias
it introduces.  The final step is to turn this population comparison into an
observable training rule.

\subsection{The ESS-guided decision rule}
\label{sec:practice}

Population ESS is unavailable during training, but its plug-in estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho
  :=\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^N W_i)^2}{N\sum_{i=1}^N W_i^2}.
  \label{eq:sample-ess}
\end{equation}
The estimate is computed from raw sequence ratios before any clipping or
truncation.  The proposed gate uses the permissive gradient when support remains
adequate and the PPO-masked gradient when support falls below a threshold.

The population theory motivates this rule but does not determine its threshold.
Sample ESS can miss unobserved tail mass, the same batch usually determines both
the gate and the gradient, and a token-local LLM surrogate is not algebraically
identical to Equation~\eqref{eq:raw-estimator}.  The boundary also depends on
gradient scale and signal.  We therefore treat the threshold as a testable
application-level choice rather than a theorem.  Recent LLM RL methods
already adapt updates using ESS, weight moments, or sequence reshaping
\cite{zheng2025m2po,shen2026vespo,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}; our question is
whether an ESS boundary locates the gradient-MSE crossover that makes such an
intervention useful.  In the controlled study below, we fix the threshold at
$0.1$ before observing the results.  Section~\ref{sec:simulation} then tests the
two empirical implications of the theory: low ESS should predict gradient
failure, and intervening only in that regime should improve the optimization
trajectory.

\section{Controlled simulation: ESS in an RLVR optimization loop}
\label{sec:simulation}

The theory predicts a specific failure sequence.  As a rollout batch becomes
less representative of the current policy, ESS should fall, the permissive
gradient should become inaccurate, and its policy update should become less
effective.  An LLM experiment cannot test this mechanism directly because its
population gradient is unknown.  We therefore construct a finite verifier task
that preserves the data flow of RLVR while making both the gradient and the
population reward exactly computable.

\subsection{Experimental design}

We convert Optdigits classification into a sequence-level verifier problem
\citep{alpaydin1998optdigits,wang2017adaptive}.  Each digit image serves as a
prompt.  The policy answers with a 16-token binary encoding of the digit, and
the verifier returns one only when the complete code is correct.  This task is
not intended to reproduce language generation.  Its purpose is to retain the
sequence-level importance ratio used in RLVR while providing an exact oracle
over the finite prompt population.

Each run then follows an RLVR training loop
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath,yu2025dapo}.  A rollout contains 128 prompts and 16
responses per prompt, from which we compute leave-one-out group advantages.
The prompt groups are partitioned into 16 sequential minibatches.  Before each
update, we recompute the sequence ratios and ESS under the current policy.  The
permissive (raw) method always uses the unmasked gradient, PPO always applies its
advantage-sign mask with $\epsilon=0.2$, and the ESS gate applies that mask only
below $0.1$.  After the 16 updates, the new policy generates the next rollout.
We repeat this cycle for eight rollouts and compare 100 paired replications.
Thus ESS deteriorates endogenously as optimization proceeds on fixed data; it
is not imposed by an artificial ratio distribution.  Appendix~\ref{app:bandit}
specifies the task, oracle, algorithm, and statistical protocol in full.

Figure~\ref{fig:ess-validation} reads from mechanism to consequence.  Panel (a)
tests whether ESS predicts gradient error.  Panel (b) tests whether that error
changes the value of a policy step.  Panel (c) tests whether the same statistic
can choose between permissive and masked updates during training.

\subsection{Results}

ESS separates reliable from unreliable permissive updates.  The gradient MSE
is $1.6\times10^{-2}$ above ESS $0.8$, increases to $1.31\times10^{-1}$ between
$0.03$ and $0.1$, and reaches $4.77\times10^{-1}$ below $0.03$.  The policy
consequence follows the same boundary.  Permissive steps reduce reward by
$4.0\%$ in the $[0.03,0.1)$ bin and by $13.3\%$ below $0.03$, while the
corresponding oracle-gradient steps improve reward in every bin.  Low ESS
therefore identifies gradient estimation failure rather than an inherently
unfavorable local objective.

This local diagnosis translates into faster optimization.  The ESS-gated method
doubles the initial reward after $6.1$K verifier responses, compared with
$8.2$K for the always-permissive method and $10.2$K for always applying the PPO
mask.  The gate therefore reduces the response budget to this target by 25\%
and 40\%, respectively.  Only 5.17 of 128 updates are masked on average, so the
gate follows the permissive trajectory while the batch remains reliable and
intervenes only in the low-ESS regime.  At the final checkpoint, the gate and
always-masked method reach nearly identical relative improvements of $208.5\%$
and $208.3\%$, compared with $195.1\%$ for the permissive method; uncertainty
does not resolve a final-reward difference between the gate and always masking.
The supported conclusion is therefore sharper than universal dominance: ESS
locates the updates that require protection and converts that diagnosis into a
sample-efficiency gain in this testbed.

\section{Main experiments: ESS-gated RLVR}
\label{sec:llm-experiments}

The controlled study establishes the mechanism under an exact oracle.  The main
experiment tests whether the same ESS rule improves language-model training,
where neither the population gradient nor its MSE is observable.  Evidence must
therefore come from matched training trajectories: the gate should preserve the
early efficiency of a permissive update while improving its late stability.

\subsection{Decision rule and comparison set}

At every policy update, we compute sequence ESS from the raw ratios before any
masking or truncation.  When $\widehat\rho\ge0.1$, the method retains the
available learning signal using either the fully permissive estimator or the
CISPO-style weight-capped estimator \citep{minimax2025m1}.  When
$\widehat\rho<0.1$, it activates one of three safeguards: conventional PPO
clipping, Clip-Higher \citep{yu2025dapo}, or the divergence constraint of DPPO
\citep{qi2026dppo}.

\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{LLM}}
  =\begin{cases}
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{permissive}},&\widehat\rho\ge0.1,\\
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{safeguard}},&\widehat\rho<0.1.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:llm-gate}
\end{equation}
Each gated method is compared with its always-permissive and always-protected
counterparts under identical rollout, optimizer, token-budget, and evaluation
settings.  This matching separates the value of the ESS decision from the value
of the safeguard itself.  The threshold is fixed before the reported runs and
is not retuned by safeguard, model, or benchmark.

\label{app:bandit}

This appendix defines every quantity used in
Figure~\ref{fig:ess-validation} and gives the complete simulation protocol.  We
first map the finite verifier task to an RLVR update, then derive the population

\subsection{Task and correspondence to RLVR}

The simulator turns supervised classification into a verifier task using the
standard contextual-bandit reduction of \citet{wang2017adaptive}: an input is
presented as the context, a policy samples an answer, and reward is one only for
the correct label.  We replace the single label action with a token sequence and
embed this task in the grouped rollout and minibatch structure used by RLVR
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath,yu2025dapo}.  Table
\ref{tab:rlvr-correspondence} records the resulting correspondence.

The simulator is a sequence policy, not a language model.  Its token heads are
conditionally independent so that the population gradient remains exact, but
its response ratio is the same product of token ratios that appears in RLVR.
During training, the learner observes only sampled responses, rollout log
probabilities, and verifier rewards.  Labels are used only by the oracle that
evaluates the finite-population reward and gradient.  This separation preserves
the RLVR feedback structure while exposing the quantities needed to test the
theory.

\subsection{Finite population and initial policy}

Optdigits is a handwritten-digit classification benchmark with 5,620 labeled
examples
\citep{alpaydin1998optdigits}.  Each original $32\times32$ binary image is
partitioned into $4\times4$ blocks; foreground counts produce 64 integer
features in $\{0,\ldots,16\}$.  We concatenate the supplied 3,823 training and
1,797 test examples, divide each feature by 16, and append an intercept.  This
gives a finite population $\{(x_j,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{M}$ with $M=5{,}620$ and
$x_j\in\mathbb{R}^{65}$.  The original split is not used because the estimand
is finite-population policy optimization, not classification generalization.

Each label $y\in\{0,\ldots,9\}$ has a four-bit binary representation.  Let
$c(y)\in\{0,1\}^{16}$ repeat this code four times.  The repetition changes only
the response length, not which answer is correct, and creates a sequence ratio
that compounds over 16 token probabilities.  The policy has one Bernoulli head
per token position,
\begin{align}
  p_{\theta,\ell}(x)&=\sigma(\theta_\ell^\top x),\nonumber\\
  P_\theta(a_{1:16}\mid x)
  &=\prod_{\ell=1}^{16}
    p_{\theta,\ell}(x)^{a_\ell}
    \{1-p_{\theta,\ell}(x)\}^{1-a_\ell},
  \qquad \theta\in\mathbb{R}^{16\times65}.
  \label{eq:optdigits-policy}
\end{align}
The verifier reward is
$R(x_j,a_{1:16})=\mathbf{1}\{a_{1:16}=c(y_j)\}$.

Initialization is deterministic and uses no simulation outcomes.  Starting
from zero, we fit all token heads for 500 full-population logistic-regression
steps with step size $0.8$ and no regularization.  We then multiply the fitted
weights by $0.55$ to keep the initial policy stochastic, which gives exact
initial reward $V(\theta_0)=0.095766$.  This procedure uses no data split,
minibatching, early stopping, or model selection.

For compactness, define
\begin{equation}
  q_j(\theta)
  :=P_\theta(c(y_j)\mid x_j)
  =\prod_{\ell=1}^{16}
  \left[c_{j\ell}p_{j\ell}
  +(1-c_{j\ell})(1-p_{j\ell})\right],
  \label{eq:correct-sequence-probability}
\end{equation}
where $c_{j\ell}=c(y_j)_\ell$ and
$p_{j\ell}=p_{\theta,\ell}(x_j)$.  The finite-population objective is
\begin{equation}
  V(\theta)=\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^{M}q_j(\theta).
  \label{eq:optdigits-value}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Exact gradient and group-relative advantage}

The finite population makes the target gradient available in closed form.  For
token position $\ell$, define the probability assigned to its correct bit as
$h_{j\ell}=c_{j\ell}p_{j\ell}+(1-c_{j\ell})(1-p_{j\ell})$.  The Bernoulli score
identity gives
\begin{align}
  \nabla_{\theta_\ell}q_j(\theta)
  &=q_j(\theta)\nabla_{\theta_\ell}\log q_j(\theta)
    \label{eq:optdigits-gradient-proof-1}\\
  &=q_j(\theta)\nabla_{\theta_\ell}\log h_{j\ell}
    \label{eq:optdigits-gradient-proof-2}\\
  &=q_j(\theta)\{c_{j\ell}-p_{j\ell}\}x_j.
    \label{eq:optdigits-gradient-proof-3}
\end{align}
The second line holds because $\theta_\ell$ appears only in token head $\ell$.
For $c_{j\ell}=1$, the last derivative is
$\nabla\log p_{j\ell}=(1-p_{j\ell})x_j$; for $c_{j\ell}=0$, it is
$\nabla\log(1-p_{j\ell})=-p_{j\ell}x_j$.  Both cases equal
$(c_{j\ell}-p_{j\ell})x_j$.  Averaging
Equation~\eqref{eq:optdigits-gradient-proof-3} over all prompts yields
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_{\theta_\ell}V(\theta)
  =\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^{M}
    q_j(\theta)\{c_{j\ell}-p_{j\ell}\}x_j.
  \label{eq:optdigits-gradient}
\end{equation}
Thus the oracle is a deterministic sum over all 5,620 prompts at the current
policy parameters, not a Monte Carlo estimate from a larger rollout.  For every
recorded minibatch update, we compare the sampled gradient and this oracle at
the same pre-update parameter value.  Their squared Frobenius error is
\begin{equation}
  E_t=\|\widehat g_t-\nabla V(\theta_t)\|_F^2,
  \label{eq:optdigits-gradient-error}
\end{equation}
which sums squared coordinate error across all $16\times65=1{,}040$ policy
parameters.  This quantity is the gradient MSE reported in the figure after
averaging $E_t$ within each fixed ESS bin.

For prompt $i$, let $Y_{ig}$ be complete response
$g\in\{1,\ldots,G\}$ and let $R_{ig}=R(x_i,Y_{ig})$ be its verifier reward.  We
use the leave-one-out group advantage
\begin{equation}
  A_{ig}=R_{ig}-\frac1{G-1}\sum_{h\ne g}R_{ih}
  =\frac{G}{G-1}(R_{ig}-\bar R_i).
  \label{eq:loo-group-advantage}
\end{equation}
It has the same sign as the group-centered advantage used in GRPO
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}.  We omit group standard-deviation scaling so that
all minibatches target the same population gradient.  To verify that the
leave-one-out baseline preserves this target, let
$B_{i,-g}=(G-1)^{-1}\sum_{h\ne g}R_{ih}$ and define
\begin{equation}
  W_{ig}=\frac{P_\theta(Y_{ig}\mid x_i)}{Q(Y_{ig}\mid x_i)},
  \qquad
  S_{ig}=\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y_{ig}\mid x_i).
  \label{eq:loo-weight-score}
\end{equation}
Conditional on the prompt, $B_{i,-g}$ is independent of the focal response.
Therefore,
\begin{align}
  &\E_Q[W_{ig}A_{ig}S_{ig}\mid x_i]
    \label{eq:loo-unbiased-proof-1}\\
  &\quad=\E_Q[W_{ig}R_{ig}S_{ig}\mid x_i]
  -\E_Q[B_{i,-g}\mid x_i]\E_Q[W_{ig}S_{ig}\mid x_i]
    \label{eq:loo-unbiased-proof-2}\\
  &\quad=\E_{P_\theta}[R_{ig}S_{ig}\mid x_i]
  -\E_Q[B_{i,-g}\mid x_i]\E_{P_\theta}[S_{ig}\mid x_i]
    \label{eq:loo-unbiased-proof-3}\\
  &\quad=\nabla_\theta\E_{P_\theta}[R_{ig}\mid x_i].
  \label{eq:loo-unbiased-proof}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:loo-unbiased-proof-2} uses independence of the focal response
and leave-one-out baseline.  Equation~\eqref{eq:loo-unbiased-proof-3} changes
measure from $Q$ to $P_\theta$.  The final line uses the zero-mean score identity
for the baseline term and the score-function identity for the reward term.  If
every response in a group has the same reward, all group advantages are zero.

\subsection{Rollout and minibatch procedure}

Each replication begins from the same $\theta_0$ and processes eight newly
generated rollout batches.  For rollout $r$, the simulator executes the
following operations in order.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Set the rollout policy $Q_r=P_{\theta_{r,0}}$ and sample 128 distinct
  prompts uniformly from the finite population.
  \item For each prompt, sample $G=16$ complete responses from $Q_r$, compute
  the binary verifier rewards, and compute Equation
  \eqref{eq:loo-group-advantage} once.
  \item Randomly permute the 128 prompt groups and partition them into 16
  minibatches.  Each minibatch contains eight intact groups, hence $N=128$
  complete responses.
  \item For minibatch $t=1,\ldots,16$, compute the current sequence ratio
  \begin{equation}
    W_{ig,t}
    =\frac{P_{\theta_{r,t-1}}(Y_{ig}\mid x_i)}
    {Q_r(Y_{ig}\mid x_i)}
    =\prod_{\ell=1}^{16}
    \frac{P_{\theta_{r,t-1}}(Y_{ig\ell}\mid x_i)}
    {Q_r(Y_{ig\ell}\mid x_i)},
    \label{eq:simulation-sequence-ratio}
  \end{equation}
  then compute raw ESS, choose the method-specific gradient, and update
  $\theta_{r,t}=\theta_{r,t-1}+3\widehat g_{r,t}$.
  \item After the sixteenth minibatch, set
  $\theta_{r+1,0}=\theta_{r,16}$ and generate a new rollout batch from that
  updated policy.
\end{enumerate}
Every sampled response enters exactly one update.  Within a rollout, verifier
rewards, advantages, and rollout log probabilities remain fixed.  Current
probabilities, ratios, scores, ESS, and the gate decision are recomputed before
each minibatch update.  This order matches grouped RLVR: generate responses from
the old policy, optimize sequential minibatches, and only then collect a new
rollout from the updated policy.

Sixteen minibatches are needed to expose the failure regime rather than to
increase the apparent gain.  Across the diagnostic trajectories, no update in
positions 1 through 4 has ESS below $0.1$.  The low-ESS frequency is at most 5\%
in positions 5 through 8 and reaches 10\% at position 16.  A four- or
eight-minibatch design would therefore make the gate nearly identical to the
permissive method and would not test the regime it is designed to detect.

\subsection{Estimators, gate, and diagnostics}

Let $S_{ig,t}=\nabla_\theta\log
P_{\theta_{r,t-1}}(Y_{ig}\mid x_i)$.  Each minibatch contains eight prompt groups
and 16 responses per group, so $N=128$.  Summing over these 128 complete
responses, the permissive and PPO-masked gradients are
\begin{align}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{raw},t}
  &=\frac1N\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t}A_{ig}S_{ig,t},
    \label{eq:simulation-raw-gradient}\\
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{PPO},t}
  &=\frac1N\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t}M_{ig,t}A_{ig}S_{ig,t},
    \label{eq:simulation-ppo-gradient}
\end{align}
where $M_{ig,t}$ is the advantage-sign mask in
Proposition~\ref{prop:ppo-mask} with $\epsilon=0.2$.  This is gradient masking,
not numerical ratio truncation.  The third method uses
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{gate},t}
  =\begin{cases}
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{raw},t},&\widehat\rho_t\ge0.1,\\
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{PPO},t},&\widehat\rho_t<0.1,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad
  \widehat\rho_t
  =\frac{(\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t})^2}
  {N\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t}^2}.
  \label{eq:simulation-gate}
\end{equation}
The threshold is fixed before the runs and is not selected from any simulation
outcome.

All three methods use identical initial weights and are paired on prompt draws,
response-sampling uniforms, and group permutations.  Their policies diverge
after the first update, but applying a shared uniform draw to each method's own
token probability remains a valid coupling and reduces comparison noise.  The
master seed is 73.  We run 100 independent replications, each with
$8\times16=128$ updates.

Panels (a) and (b) use every minibatch from the first 20 ESS-gated
replications, yielding $20\times8\times16=2{,}560$ diagnostic observations.  At
each pre-update policy, we compute Equation~\eqref{eq:optdigits-gradient} and
record $\|\widehat g_{\mathrm{raw},t}-\nabla V(\theta_t)\|_F^2$.  We then evaluate
two counterfactual one-step policies, one updated by the sampled permissive
gradient and the other by the oracle gradient, without changing the actual
training trajectory.  Their outcomes isolate whether a harmful sampled step is
caused by estimation error or by an unfavorable population gradient.

Before running the simulation, we fixed the ESS intervals $[0,0.03)$,
$[0.03,0.1)$, $[0.1,0.2)$, $[0.2,0.4)$, $[0.4,0.6)$, $[0.6,0.8)$, and
$[0.8,1]$.  Panels (a) and (b) report the within-bin mean and its standard error.
Panel (c) uses all 100 replications and reports
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_r^{\mathrm{rel}}
  =100\frac{V(\theta_{r,0})-V(\theta_{0,0})}
  {V(\theta_{0,0})}.
  \label{eq:relative-improvement}
\end{equation}
after each rollout.  The plotted line is the replication mean and the band is
the mean plus or minus $1.96$ standard errors.  Response counts include every
complete response sent to the verifier; one rollout therefore costs
$128\times16=2{,}048$ verifier responses.  A method reaches the doubling target
at the first post-rollout checkpoint for which its mean relative improvement
exceeds 100\%.
