\title{\bf Rethinking Clipping in RLVR:\\
Sequence ESS Governs the Reliability of Unclipped Updates}
\author{Anonymous authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards often takes several policy
updates on fixed rollout data.  PPO clipping is commonly treated as necessary
once the policy moves away from those data, yet elapsed updates do not determine
whether an unclipped update is reliable.  We show that reliability depends on
both policy coverage and the influence of the samples that have poor coverage.
Effective sample size connects these quantities to gradient-estimation error,
and we prove that lower error gives a stronger guarantee on the next policy
improvement.  We also show that PPO clipping is advantage-sign gradient
masking, not a cap on all large importance ratios.  This distinction yields an
ESS-guided rule: use the unbiased gradient while coverage is sufficient and
apply PPO masking after coverage deteriorates.  In a controlled contextual
bandit, high ESS predicts low gradient error, low error predicts better policy
updates, and the gated method avoids the loss caused by clipping throughout
optimization.
Validation in language-model training remains open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

RLVR improves data efficiency by taking several policy updates on fixed rollout
data.  As the policy changes, those responses become less representative of its
current behavior.  The central question is therefore not how many updates have
elapsed, but whether the rollout data still support an accurate gradient.

PPO addresses policy change with a clipped surrogate
\citep{schulman2017ppo}.  This mechanism is often discussed as if it established
that an unclipped update becomes statistically unstable after enough reuse.
That conclusion does not follow from the update count or from clipping itself.
Recent work has likewise questioned whether a fixed ratio interval identifies
harmful policy change \citep{qi2026dppo}, while adaptive methods instead monitor
the concentration of off-policy weights
\citep{zheng2025m2po,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}.  These observations leave a
prior question unanswered: when is the unclipped estimator actually unreliable?

The answer begins with coverage.  Two rollout datasets at the same optimization
step can differ sharply in how well they represent the current policy.  One may
remain broadly informative, while a few responses may dominate the other.
Sequence effective sample size (ESS) measures this distinction and therefore
replaces update count with a statistical quantity.

Our main result connects ESS to the mean-squared error of the unclipped
gradient.  The connection also exposes the required qualification: coverage
must be considered together with the gradient scale carried by poorly covered
samples.  Thus stale data can remain reliable, and recent data can already be
unreliable.  We then prove why this estimation result matters for optimization:
lower gradient error raises a certified lower bound on the expected reward of
the next policy step.  ESS is therefore the bridge from policy coverage to an
optimization consequence, not a complete decision rule by itself.

This bridge clarifies what should happen next.  PPO clipping and ratio
truncation are not the same intervention.  Differentiating the PPO surrogate
masks a sample according to the sign of its advantage and the side on which its
ratio crosses the clipping interval.  Explicit ratio truncation instead caps
every large importance coefficient.  We derive the bias and covariance of each
estimator separately.  This distinction rules out both a universal ESS
threshold and a privileged truncation cap.

We validate this chain before making any claim about language-model training.
Following the classification-to-contextual-bandit protocol of
\citet{wang2017adaptive}, we use Optdigits images as contexts, labels as actions,
and correctness as a bounded reward \citep{alpaydin1998optdigits}.  The finite
population lets us calculate the true gradient and all theoretical moments.
Across 100 batches per condition, the experiment tests whether high ESS predicts
low raw-gradient error, whether lower error improves the next policy update,
and whether an ESS gate improves fixed-rollout optimization over always clipping.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item We identify sequence ESS as the bridge from policy coverage to gradient
error, then prove how that error controls a one-step policy-improvement
guarantee.
\item We show that PPO clipping is an advantage-sign gradient mask, distinguish
it from ratio truncation, and derive the exact risk comparison for each.
\item We validate the chain from ESS to gradient error, one-step policy
improvement, and ESS-gated fixed-rollout optimization on an established
contextual-bandit benchmark.
\end{enumerate}

The next section makes the setting precise and separates population correctness
from finite-sample reliability.

\section{Problem formulation and sequence coverage}
\label{sec:setting}

We first separate the target of an update from the accuracy with which it is
estimated.  Let $X\sim\nu$ be a prompt, let $Y$ be a complete response drawn
from the rollout policy $Q$, and let $R=R(X,Y)$ be a terminal verifier reward.
The current policy is $P_\theta$, with $P_\theta\ll Q$.  Their sequence
likelihood ratio is
\begin{equation}
  W_\theta(X,Y)
  :=\frac{P_\theta(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)},
  \qquad
  \E_Q[W_\theta\mid X]=1.
  \label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}

Let $b(X)$ be a detached prompt-only baseline and set $A=R-b(X)$.  Define the
score and the corresponding sequence contribution by
\begin{equation}
  S_\theta:=\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y\mid X),
  \qquad
  H_\theta:=A S_\theta.
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
For $J(\theta):=\E_{P_\theta}[R]$, assume that differentiation and expectation
can be interchanged.  The population gradient follows one identity per line:
\begin{align}
  g(\theta):=\nabla J(\theta)
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[R S_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-1}\\
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[(R-b(X))S_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-2}\\
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[H_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-3}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta].
  \label{eq:true-gradient}
\end{align}
The first line is the score-function identity.  The second uses
$\E_{P_\theta}[b(X)S_\theta\mid X]
=b(X)\nabla_\theta\sum_yP_\theta(y\mid X)=0$.  The third substitutes
\eqref{eq:score}, and the fourth changes measure from $P_\theta$ to $Q$.
Given $N$ independent responses from $Q$, the unclipped estimator is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_W(\theta)
  :=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N W_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}.
  \label{eq:raw-estimator}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:true-gradient} establishes the correct population target.
It does not establish that a finite rollout sample estimates that target
reliably, which is the question addressed next.

\subsection{Sequence coverage}

The second moment of the likelihood ratio measures how well $Q$ covers
$P_\theta$.  Assuming $\E_Q[W_\theta^2]<\infty$, define normalized sequence ESS
by
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\theta:=\frac{1}{\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
  =\exp\{-D_2(P_\theta\|Q)\}.
  \label{eq:population-ess}
\end{equation}
Because $\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$, its relation to ratio dispersion is exact:
\begin{align}
  \E_Q[(W_\theta-1)^2]
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta^2]-2\E_Q[W_\theta]+1
    \label{eq:weight-variance-proof-1}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta^2]-1
    \label{eq:weight-variance-proof-2}\\
  &=\rho_\theta^{-1}-1.
    \label{eq:weight-variance}
\end{align}
The first line expands the square, the second uses $\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$, and the
third uses \eqref{eq:population-ess}.  Thus $N\rho_\theta$ is the population
effective sequence count, consistent with the second-moment coverage principle
used in importance-sampling policy optimization
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  Coverage is scalar, whereas the update
is a vector, so the next section establishes the exact bridge between them.

\section{Main result: ESS governs finite-sample reliability}
\label{sec:raw}

Population correctness does not prevent a small number of responses from
dominating a finite-sample update.  To separate ratio concentration from the
scale of the associated gradient contributions, define
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}
  :=\frac{\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]}
          {\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}.
  \label{eq:gradient-scale}
\end{equation}
The quantity $G_{2,\theta}$ is a weighted gradient scale.  It records whether
large likelihood ratios are attached to large gradient contributions.  With
this qualifier explicit, the main theorem states the reliability bridge in one
step.

\begin{theorem}[ESS reliability bridge]
\label{thm:raw-mse}
Assume $\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]<\infty$.  For fixed $\theta$,
$\widehat g_W$ is unbiased and
\begin{align}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  &=\frac1N\left\{
    \E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right\}
  \label{eq:raw-mse}\\
  &=\frac1N\left(
    \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{\rho_\theta}-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right)
  \label{eq:ess-factorization}\\
  &\le \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}.
  \label{eq:ess-bridge}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $Z_i=W_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}$ and let $g=g(\theta)$.  Unbiasedness follows
line by line from identical sampling:
\begin{align}
  \E[\widehat g_W]
  &=\E\left[\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N Z_i\right]
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-1}\\
  &=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\E[Z_i]
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-2}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta]
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-3}\\
  &=g.
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-4}
\end{align}
For the MSE, center each contribution before expanding the squared norm:
\begin{align}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  &=\E\left\|
    \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N(Z_i-g)
    \right\|_2^2
    \label{eq:raw-proof-1}\\
  &=\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i=1}^N\E\|Z_i-g\|_2^2
    +\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i\ne j}
      \E[(Z_i-g)^\top(Z_j-g)]
    \label{eq:raw-proof-2}\\
  &=\frac1N\E\|Z_1-g\|_2^2
    \label{eq:raw-proof-3}\\
  &=\frac1N\left\{
    \E\|Z_1\|_2^2-2g^\top\E[Z_1]+\|g\|_2^2
    \right\}
    \label{eq:raw-proof-4}\\
  &=\frac1N\left\{
    \E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]-\|g\|_2^2
    \right\}
    \label{eq:raw-proof-5}\\
  &=\frac1N\left(
    \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{\rho_\theta}-\|g\|_2^2
    \right)
    \label{eq:raw-proof-6}\\
  &\le\frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}.
    \label{eq:raw-proof-7}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:unbiased-proof-1} substitutes the estimator definition.
Equation~\eqref{eq:unbiased-proof-2} uses linearity of expectation, and
\eqref{eq:unbiased-proof-3} uses identical sampling.  The last line substitutes
\eqref{eq:true-gradient}.  For the MSE, \eqref{eq:raw-proof-1} uses
$\widehat g_W-g=N^{-1}\sum_i(Z_i-g)$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:raw-proof-2} expands
the squared norm.  Independence and $\E[Z_i-g]=0$ make every cross term vanish;
identical sampling then gives \eqref{eq:raw-proof-3}.  Equation
\eqref{eq:raw-proof-4} expands the remaining squared norm.  Finally,
\eqref{eq:raw-proof-5} substitutes $\E[Z_1]=g$ and
$\|Z_1\|_2^2=W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:raw-proof-6} uses \eqref{eq:gradient-scale} and
\eqref{eq:population-ess}.  Equation~\eqref{eq:raw-proof-7} drops the
nonpositive term $-\|g\|_2^2/N$.
\end{proof}

Equation~\eqref{eq:ess-bridge} is the main theoretical bridge.  Sequence ESS
governs the error contributed by ratio concentration, while $G_{2,\theta}$
records whether large ratios coincide with large gradient contributions.  When
$G_{2,\theta}$ is controlled and $N\rho_\theta$ is large, the upper bound remains
small.  Policy drift or update count alone therefore does not imply estimator
failure.  Gradient error would still be only a statistical diagnostic, however,
unless it implied a concrete optimization consequence.  The next result makes
that implication explicit.

\subsection{Why gradient MSE controls policy improvement}
\label{sec:mse-improvement}

Policy-gradient theory supplies the missing optimization link.  Theorem~7 of
\citet{papini2022smoothing} states that an $L$-smooth policy objective satisfies
the following lower bound for any feasible parameter step $d$:
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta+d)-J(\theta)
  \ge g(\theta)^\top d-\frac{L}{2}\|d\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:smooth-policy-improvement}
\end{equation}
That work uses the bound to construct safe policy-gradient schedules.  Broader
analyses establish convergence from smoothness and gradient second moments
\citep{yuan2022general}.  Off-policy work also uses gradient MSE to assess
efficiency \citep{kallus2020efficient}, while biased-gradient theory shows why
bias and variance must be assessed jointly \citep{driggs2022biased}.  The result below applies
\eqref{eq:smooth-policy-improvement} directly to the possibly biased update
directions created by clipping or truncation.

\begin{theorem}[Gradient MSE controls certified policy improvement]
\label{thm:mse-improvement}
Fix $\theta$ and let $g=g(\theta)$.  Let $\widehat g$ be any, possibly biased,
gradient estimator with finite second moment.  Suppose $J$ is $L$-smooth on
every line segment from $\theta$ to $\theta+\eta\widehat g$, and that these
updates are feasible.  For $0<\eta\le 1/L$,
\begin{equation}
  \E\!\left[J(\theta+\eta\widehat g)-J(\theta)\right]
  \ge \frac{\eta}{2}
  \left\{\|g\|_2^2-\E\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2\right\}.
  \label{eq:mse-improvement}
\end{equation}
Consequently, $\E\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2<\|g\|_2^2$ is sufficient for positive
expected policy improvement.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply \eqref{eq:smooth-policy-improvement} with
$d=\eta\widehat g$ and take expectation:
\begin{align}
  &\E\!\left[J(\theta+\eta\widehat g)-J(\theta)\right]
    \label{eq:mse-improvement-proof-1}\\
  &\quad\ge
    \eta\E[g^\top\widehat g]
    -\frac{L\eta^2}{2}\E\|\widehat g\|_2^2
    \label{eq:mse-improvement-proof-2}\\
  &\quad\ge
    \eta\E[g^\top\widehat g]
    -\frac{\eta}{2}\E\|\widehat g\|_2^2
    \label{eq:mse-improvement-proof-3}\\
  &\quad=
    \frac{\eta}{2}\E\!\left[
      2g^\top\widehat g-\|\widehat g\|_2^2
    \right]
    \label{eq:mse-improvement-proof-4}\\
  &\quad=
    \frac{\eta}{2}\E\!\left[
      \|g\|_2^2-\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2
    \right]
    \label{eq:mse-improvement-proof-5}\\
  &\quad=
    \frac{\eta}{2}
    \left\{\|g\|_2^2-\E\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2\right\}.
    \label{eq:mse-improvement-proof-6}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:mse-improvement-proof-2} uses the cited smoothness bound.
Equation~\eqref{eq:mse-improvement-proof-3} uses $L\eta\le1$ and the
nonnegativity of $\|\widehat g\|_2^2$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:mse-improvement-proof-4} collects the two terms under one
expectation.  Equation~\eqref{eq:mse-improvement-proof-5} uses
$2g^\top\widehat g-\|\widehat g\|_2^2
=\|g\|_2^2-\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2$.  The final line moves the deterministic
quantity $\|g\|_2^2$ outside the expectation.  If the MSE is below
$\|g\|_2^2$, the right side is positive.
\end{proof}

This theorem provides a direct, nonasymptotic meaning for low gradient MSE.
For two estimators used with the same learning rate, reducing MSE by $\delta$
raises the certified improvement lower bound by $\eta\delta/2$.  This statement
orders their guarantees, not necessarily their realized improvements, because
the smoothness lower bound need not be tight.

\begin{corollary}[ESS certificate for an unclipped update]
\label{cor:ess-improvement}
Under Theorems~\ref{thm:raw-mse} and \ref{thm:mse-improvement},
\begin{equation}
  \E\!\left[J(\theta+\eta\widehat g_W)-J(\theta)\right]
  \ge \frac{\eta}{2}
  \left\{
    \left(1+\frac1N\right)\|g\|_2^2
    -\frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:ess-improvement-certificate}
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}<(N+1)\rho_\theta\|g\|_2^2
  \label{eq:positive-ess-update}
\end{equation}
is sufficient for positive expected policy improvement.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Substitute the exact MSE from \eqref{eq:ess-factorization} into
\eqref{eq:mse-improvement}:
\begin{align}
  &\E\!\left[J(\theta+\eta\widehat g_W)-J(\theta)\right]
    \label{eq:ess-improvement-proof-1}\\
  &\quad\ge\frac{\eta}{2}\left[
    \|g\|_2^2
    -\frac1N\left(
      \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{\rho_\theta}-\|g\|_2^2
    \right)
  \right]
    \label{eq:ess-improvement-proof-2}\\
  &\quad=\frac{\eta}{2}\left\{
    \left(1+\frac1N\right)\|g\|_2^2
    -\frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}
  \right\}.
    \label{eq:ess-improvement-proof-3}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ess-improvement-proof-2} applies
Theorem~\ref{thm:mse-improvement}; Equation
\eqref{eq:ess-improvement-proof-3} collects the two gradient-norm terms.  Its
right side is positive exactly when
$G_{2,\theta}<(N+1)\rho_\theta\|g\|_2^2$.
\end{proof}

Corollary~\ref{cor:ess-improvement} closes the theoretical chain.  At fixed
gradient signal and weighted gradient scale, higher ESS strengthens the
one-step improvement certificate.  The certificate does not create a universal
ESS threshold because its boundary also depends on the gradient signal and
$G_{2,\theta}$.  The remaining task is therefore to show when the latter scale
is controlled in RLVR.

\subsection{Controlling the weighted gradient scale in RLVR}

The main theorem is informative only when its remaining factor is controlled.
Advantage bounds are
useful here, but they cannot control $G_{2,\theta}$ without a corresponding
condition on the score.

\begin{proposition}[A sufficient bound on $G_{2,\theta}$]
\label{prop:g2-bound}
If $|A|\le B_A$ and $\|S_\theta\|_2\le B_S$ almost surely under $Q$, then
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}\le B_A^2B_S^2.
  \label{eq:g2-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $H_\theta=A S_\theta$, substitute its squared norm into
\eqref{eq:gradient-scale} and apply one bound per line:
\begin{align}
  G_{2,\theta}
  &=\frac{\E_Q[W_\theta^2 A^2\|S_\theta\|_2^2]}
          {\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
    \label{eq:g2-proof-1}\\
  &\le B_A^2
    \frac{\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|S_\theta\|_2^2]}
         {\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
    \label{eq:g2-proof-2}\\
  &\le B_A^2B_S^2.
    \label{eq:g2-proof-3}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:g2-proof-2} uses $A^2\le B_A^2$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:g2-proof-3} uses $\|S_\theta\|_2^2\le B_S^2$ under the same
nonnegative weight $W_\theta^2$, then cancels $\E_Q[W_\theta^2]$.
\end{proof}

The advantage side is favorable in common RLVR constructions.  If the verifier
reward is binary and a prompt-only baseline satisfies $b(X)\in[0,1]$, then
$|A|=|R-b(X)|\le1$.  Outcome-level GRPO instead standardizes rewards within a
group of $K$ responses \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}.  Its standardized
advantages also have a deterministic finite-group bound whenever their sample
variance is nonzero.

\begin{proposition}[Standardized group-advantage bound]
\label{prop:grpo-bound}
Let $d_i=r_i-\bar r$, let $v=K^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^K d_j^2>0$, and let
$A_i=d_i/\sqrt v$.  Then $|A_i|\le\sqrt{K-1}$ for every $i$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The centered deviations satisfy $d_i=-\sum_{j\ne i}d_j$.  Therefore,
\begin{align}
  d_i^2
  &=\left(\sum_{j\ne i}d_j\right)^2
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-1}\\
  &\le (K-1)\sum_{j\ne i}d_j^2
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-2}\\
  &=(K-1)\left(\sum_{j=1}^K d_j^2-d_i^2\right).
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-3}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:grpo-proof-1} uses the zero-sum identity for centered
deviations.  Equation~\eqref{eq:grpo-proof-2} is Cauchy--Schwarz, and
\eqref{eq:grpo-proof-3} restores the omitted term.  Rearranging gives
\begin{align}
  Kd_i^2
  &\le (K-1)\sum_{j=1}^K d_j^2
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-4}\\
  \frac{d_i^2}{v}
  &\le K-1,
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-5}
\end{align}
where the second line uses $Kv=\sum_jd_j^2$.  Taking square roots proves the
claim.  Adding a positive stabilizer to the denominator can only decrease the
magnitude.
\end{proof}

These bounds isolate what advantage normalization can and cannot establish.
They control the advantage factor, while a bound or empirical estimate of the
score factor remains necessary.  Moreover, group normalization couples the $K$
responses, so Theorem~\ref{thm:raw-mse} does not apply response by response without a
group-level sampling analysis.  The binary-reward, prompt-baseline case obeys
the theorem directly; the GRPO bound supplies scale information rather than an
i.i.d. justification.

\begin{remark}[Pointwise scope]
The expectation is over a fresh batch at fixed $\theta$.  During same-batch
optimization, $\theta$ depends on the fixed rollout data.  Extending
\eqref{eq:ess-bridge} uniformly over that adaptive path requires additional
stability or complexity control.
\end{remark}

The raw risk is now explicit.  The next question is what PPO clipping actually
does to this gradient and how that operation differs from ratio truncation.

\section{PPO clipping and ratio truncation are different estimators}
\label{sec:clipping}

For a detached advantage $A$, the per-sample PPO surrogate is
\begin{equation}
  \ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
  :=\min\left\{
    W_\theta A,
    \operatorname{clip}(W_\theta,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:ppo-objective}
\end{equation}
Clipping this scalar objective does not cap the gradient coefficient.  Its
derivative instead masks samples according to advantage sign.

\begin{proposition}[PPO clipping is advantage-sign gradient masking]
\label{prop:ppo-mask}
Away from the two clipping boundaries, define
\begin{equation}
  M_\epsilon
  :=\mathbb{1}\{A\ge0, W_\theta\le1+\epsilon\}
   +\mathbb{1}\{A<0, W_\theta\ge1-\epsilon\}.
  \label{eq:ppo-mask}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_\theta\ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
  =W_\theta M_\epsilon H_\theta.
  \label{eq:ppo-gradient}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
When $A\ge0$, multiplication by $A$ preserves order.  Hence
\begin{align}
  \ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}
  &=A\min\{W_\theta,1+\epsilon\}
    \label{eq:ppo-positive-proof-1}\\
  \nabla_\theta\ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}
  &=A\nabla_\theta W_\theta
    \mathbb{1}\{W_\theta<1+\epsilon\}
    \label{eq:ppo-positive-proof-2}\\
  &=AW_\theta S_\theta
    \mathbb{1}\{W_\theta<1+\epsilon\}.
    \label{eq:ppo-positive-proof-3}
\end{align}
The second line differentiates the active branch.  The third uses
$\nabla_\theta W_\theta=W_\theta S_\theta$.

When $A<0$, multiplication reverses order.  Therefore
\begin{align}
  \ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}
  &=A\max\{W_\theta,1-\epsilon\}
    \label{eq:ppo-negative-proof-1}\\
  \nabla_\theta\ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}
  &=A\nabla_\theta W_\theta
    \mathbb{1}\{W_\theta>1-\epsilon\}
    \label{eq:ppo-negative-proof-2}\\
  &=AW_\theta S_\theta
    \mathbb{1}\{W_\theta>1-\epsilon\}.
    \label{eq:ppo-negative-proof-3}
\end{align}
Combining the two cases and substituting $H_\theta=AS_\theta$ gives
\eqref{eq:ppo-gradient}.  When $A=0$, the gradient is zero.  At the two
boundaries, any fixed valid subgradient convention preserves the distinction.
\end{proof}

PPO therefore removes an upper-ratio gradient only for nonnegative advantages
and a lower-ratio gradient only for negative advantages.  In particular, a
large ratio with negative advantage remains active.  PPO clipping is a
directional mask, not a global bound on importance coefficients.

To compare this mask with other estimators, let $C_\theta$ be any scalar
coefficient for which $C_\theta H_\theta$ has a finite second moment.  Define
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_C:=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N C_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i},
  \qquad
  b_C:=\E_Q[(C_\theta-W_\theta)H_\theta],
  \label{eq:modified-estimator}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma_W:=\Cov_Q(W_\theta H_\theta),
  \qquad
  \Sigma_C:=\Cov_Q(C_\theta H_\theta).
  \label{eq:bias-covariance}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Exact estimator crossover]
\label{thm:crossover}
For fixed $\theta$ and finite second moments,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  =\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  =\|b_C\|_2^2+\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C).
  \label{eq:two-risks}
\end{equation}
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  <\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  \iff
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C)
  >N\|b_C\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $\mu_C:=\E[\widehat g_C]$.  Its displacement from the target is
\begin{align}
  \mu_C-g
  &=\E_Q[C_\theta H_\theta]-\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta]
    \label{eq:modified-bias-proof-1}\\
  &=\E_Q[(C_\theta-W_\theta)H_\theta]
    \label{eq:modified-bias-proof-2}\\
  &=b_C.
    \label{eq:modified-bias-proof-3}
\end{align}
Centering the modified estimator at $\mu_C$ now yields
\begin{align}
  \E\|\widehat g_C-g\|_2^2
  &=\E\|\widehat g_C-\mu_C+b_C\|_2^2
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-1}\\
  &=\E\|\widehat g_C-\mu_C\|_2^2
    +2b_C^\top\E[\widehat g_C-\mu_C]
    +\|b_C\|_2^2
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-2}\\
  &=\E\|\widehat g_C-\mu_C\|_2^2+\|b_C\|_2^2
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-3}\\
  &=\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C)+\|b_C\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-4}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:modified-bias-proof-1}--\eqref{eq:modified-bias-proof-3}
substitute $g=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta]$, use linearity, and invoke the definition
of $b_C$, respectively.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-1} adds and
subtracts $\mu_C$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-2} expands the
squared norm.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-3} uses
$\E[\widehat g_C-\mu_C]=0$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-4} uses
independence of the $N$ sampled responses, exactly as in
\eqref{eq:raw-proof-2}--\eqref{eq:raw-proof-3}.  The raw estimator is unbiased by
Theorem~\ref{thm:raw-mse}, so the same covariance calculation gives
\begin{equation}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  =\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W).
  \label{eq:raw-risk-crossover-proof}
\end{equation}
Subtracting the two risks gives
\begin{align}
  &\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
    -\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
    \label{eq:crossover-proof-1}\\
  &\quad=\|b_C\|_2^2
    -\frac1N\left\{
      \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C)
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:crossover-proof-2}
\end{align}
The left side is negative exactly when the covariance reduction exceeds
$N\|b_C\|_2^2$, which is \eqref{eq:crossover}.
\end{proof}

For PPO, the coefficient is $C_\theta^{\mathrm{PPO}}=W_\theta M_\epsilon$.
Its exact bias is
\begin{align}
  b_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}
  &=\E_Q[(W_\theta M_\epsilon-W_\theta)H_\theta]
    \label{eq:ppo-bias-1}\\
  &=-\E_Q[W_\theta(1-M_\epsilon)H_\theta].
    \label{eq:ppo-bias-2}
\end{align}
Theorem~\ref{thm:crossover} compares the covariance removed by this
sign-specific mask with its squared bias.  Combining the theorem with
Theorem~\ref{thm:mse-improvement}, the difference between the modified and raw
MSE-based improvement certificates is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\eta}{2}\left\{
    \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
    -\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:improvement-crossover}
\end{equation}
Thus \eqref{eq:crossover} identifies when masking or truncation has the larger
one-step certificate, not only when it has lower estimator risk.

Ratio truncation is different.  It changes the importance coefficient after
the gradient form has been specified.  For $c>0$, define
\begin{equation}
  C_c^{\mathrm{tr}}:=\min(W_\theta,c),
  \qquad
  T_c:=(W_\theta-c)_+,
  \qquad
  \tau_c:=\E_Q[T_c].
  \label{eq:upper-truncation}
\end{equation}
The contribution above $c$ is then $cH_\theta$, not zero.  This is a detached
importance-weight estimator, not the derivative of the PPO surrogate.

\begin{corollary}[Bias and MSE of upper-ratio truncation]
\label{cor:upper-truncation}
Suppose $\|H_\theta\|_2\le H_{\max}$ almost surely.  The detached estimator
$\widehat g_c=N^{-1}\sum_i C_{c,i}^{\mathrm{tr}}H_{\theta,i}$ satisfies
\begin{align}
  b_c&=-\E_Q[T_cH_\theta],
  &\|b_c\|_2&\le H_{\max}\tau_c,
  \label{eq:cap-bias}\\
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_c)
  &\le H_{\max}^2\left(\tau_c^2+\frac{c}{N}\right).
  \label{eq:cap-mse-bound}
\end{align}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The coefficient difference is $C_c^{\mathrm{tr}}-W_\theta=-T_c$.  Substitution into
\eqref{eq:bias-covariance} gives
\begin{align}
  b_c
  &=\E_Q[(C_c^{\mathrm{tr}}-W_\theta)H_\theta]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-1}\\
  &=-\E_Q[T_cH_\theta].
    \label{eq:cap-proof-2}
\end{align}
The triangle inequality and the bound on $H_\theta$ then give
\begin{align}
  \|b_c\|_2
  &\le\E_Q[T_c\|H_\theta\|_2]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-3}\\
  &\le H_{\max}\E_Q[T_c]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-4}\\
  &=H_{\max}\tau_c.
    \label{eq:cap-proof-5}
\end{align}
For the covariance term, use the trace identity and remove its nonnegative
mean-squared term:
\begin{align}
  \operatorname{tr}\{\Cov_Q(C_c^{\mathrm{tr}}H_\theta)\}
  &=\E_Q[(C_c^{\mathrm{tr}})^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]
    -\|\E_Q[C_c^{\mathrm{tr}}H_\theta]\|_2^2
    \label{eq:cap-proof-6}\\
  &\le H_{\max}^2\E_Q[(C_c^{\mathrm{tr}})^2]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-7}\\
  &\le cH_{\max}^2\E_Q[W_\theta]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-8}\\
  &=cH_{\max}^2.
    \label{eq:cap-proof-9}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cap-proof-7} uses the bound on $H_\theta$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:cap-proof-8} uses $(C_c^{\mathrm{tr}})^2\le cW_\theta$,
which holds both
when $W_\theta\le c$ and when $W_\theta>c$.  The final line uses
$\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$.  Combining \eqref{eq:two-risks},
\eqref{eq:cap-proof-5}, and \eqref{eq:cap-proof-9} proves
\eqref{eq:cap-mse-bound}.
\end{proof}

The bound explains when upper truncation can be useful.  It replaces the raw
second-moment dependence by $c/N$, while its bias is small when the tail excess
$\tau_c$ is small.  The cap is therefore an estimator parameter to calibrate,
not a scientific claim.  Truncated importance ratios also appear in ACER with
bias correction \citep{wang2017acer} and in the V-trace correction used by
IMPALA \citep{espeholt2018impala}; recent LLM methods likewise control extreme
weight moments \citep{zheng2025m2po,fakoor2026p3o}.  These precedents motivate
truncation as a separate importance-sampling device.  They do not make it
equivalent to PPO clipping.

The ESS identity and crossover now give the intended decision logic.  When
$G_{2,\theta}$ is controlled, high ESS certifies a small raw-gradient MSE and
therefore a stronger policy-improvement lower bound.  As ESS falls, raw-gradient
MSE grows and an intervention becomes valuable exactly when its variance
reduction repays its squared bias.  The threshold must still be calibrated
because PPO's benefit depends on this sign-specific crossover.

\section{From theory to an ESS-guided estimator rule}
\label{sec:practice}

Population ESS is unavailable during training.  Its plug-in estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho
  :=\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^N W_i)^2}{N\sum_{i=1}^N W_i^2}.
  \label{eq:sample-ess}
\end{equation}
It should be computed from raw, pre-clipping sequence ratios.  The ESS gate uses
the raw gradient while $\widehat\rho$ is high and the PPO masked gradient once
$\widehat\rho$ falls below a calibrated threshold.

This rule is a hypothesis, not a consequence of
Theorem~\ref{thm:crossover}.  First, sample ESS can miss unobserved tail mass.  Second,
the same batch usually determines both the gate and the gradient.  Third,
a token-local surrogate is not algebraically equal to
\eqref{eq:raw-estimator}.  Recent LLM RL methods
already adapt updates using ESS, weight moments, or sequence reshaping
\cite{zheng2025m2po,shen2026vespo,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}; our distinct
empirical question is whether their relevant regime change coincides with the
gradient-MSE crossover in \eqref{eq:crossover}.

The rule is simple.  Compute raw sequence ESS, use the unmasked update above a
threshold, and use PPO masking below it.  Select the threshold on validation
runs and freeze it before evaluation.  The controlled study below tests the
full chain from ESS to gradient error, policy improvement, and the gated
optimization trajectory.

\section{Empirical validation of the theory}
\label{sec:experiments}

Theorems~\ref{thm:raw-mse} and \ref{thm:mse-improvement} establish the first two
links in the population guarantee: ESS controls gradient MSE when the gradient
scale is controlled, and that MSE controls a one-step improvement certificate.
The practical claim also relies on sample ESS and realized masked updates, so
the experiment tests the full chain.  Does higher ESS imply lower raw-gradient
MSE?  Does the resulting MSE ordering appear in actual population reward after
one update?  Finally, can ESS decide when to use the raw gradient and when to
apply the actual PPO mask during fixed-rollout optimization?

We use the classification-to-contextual-bandit protocol of
\citet{wang2017adaptive}.  Each Optdigits image is a context, the ten labels are
actions, and a correct action yields unit reward \citep{alpaydin1998optdigits}.
This established testbed is necessary because its finite labeled population
makes the target gradient and policy reward exactly computable.  We can thus
measure gradient MSE and the policy consequence of each estimated update rather
than infer either from a noisy training curve.

The first study fixes one evaluation policy and perturbs only its logger.  The
perturbation is independent of the gradient contributions, so it varies ESS
while keeping the weighted gradient scale in a narrow range.  We draw 100
batches of 256 interactions at each ESS level, compute the raw-gradient MSE,
and apply one estimated-gradient step before evaluating the exact population
reward.  The second study draws one fixed rollout batch per run and takes 35
updates with the raw gradient, the PPO mask in
Proposition~\ref{prop:ppo-mask}, or an ESS gate between them.  Forty runs select
the ESS threshold; the remaining 60 runs report policy reward.  Full details
appear in Appendix~\ref{app:bandit}.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/ess_policy_validation.pdf}
  \caption{Validation of the ESS decision chain.  (a) Raw-gradient MSE rises as
  normalized ESS falls; the dashed curve is the exact finite-population MSE.
  (b) The same loss of reliability reduces one-step population improvement and
  eventually makes the average update harmful; the dashed line uses the exact
  gradient.  (c) Over 35 updates on one fixed rollout batch, an ESS gate retains
  the benefit of raw updates at high coverage and applies PPO masking after
  coverage falls.  Curves and intervals use 60 held-out runs after threshold
  selection on 40 validation runs.}
  \label{fig:ess-validation}
\end{figure}

The three panels support the intended chain.  As ESS falls from $1.0$ to
$0.0025$, raw-gradient MSE increases from $0.0105$ to $0.918$.  Over the same
sweep, mean one-step population gain falls from $0.00939$ to $-0.00570$.
Thus the MSE deterioration predicted by the theorem corresponds to a worse
policy update in this controlled environment.  In fixed-rollout optimization,
the validation-selected ESS gate reaches test reward $0.7891\pm0.0019$, compared
with $0.7707\pm0.0012$ for always-clipped PPO and $0.7903\pm0.0018$ for the
unclipped estimator.  The gate removes most of the bias from clipping too early
while preserving the raw estimator's performance in this testbed.  It does not
significantly outperform the unclipped baseline here, so the result validates
ESS as a useful criterion rather than claiming universal dominance.

\section{Optdigits contextual-bandit protocol}
\label{app:bandit}

\subsection{Established testbed and data}

The protocol follows the classification reduction used by
\citet{dudik2011doubly,wang2017adaptive}.  A labeled example is a context, the
class labels are actions, and the reward equals one exactly when the selected
action is the true label.  These studies use the construction because logged
bandit feedback can be sampled while the full labeled data still reveal the
target value.  We use the same advantage for gradient evaluation: the full
empirical distribution also reveals the target gradient and all
importance-weight moments.  The 2011 study included Optdigits in its UCI
classification benchmark and used 30 repeated policy-learning runs; it also
used 100 repetitions in a separate covariate-shift study
\citep{dudik2011doubly}.  Our 100-repetition limit therefore remains consistent
with established contextual-bandit practice.

The Optdigits data contain $M=5{,}620$ examples, 64 pixel-aggregation features,
and ten digit classes \citep{alpaydin1998optdigits}.  Features are divided by 16
and augmented with an intercept.  We combine the original training and test
files and place uniform mass on the resulting empirical population.  This step
does not estimate predictive performance.  It defines a finite contextual
bandit on which population quantities are exactly computable.

\subsection{Policy class and controlled ESS sweep}

We fit a ten-class linear softmax classifier to the finite population and scale
its weights by $0.3$ to obtain a stochastic evaluation policy.  For feature
vector $x$ and action $a$, its score is
\begin{equation}
  S_\theta(x,a)=\{e_a-P_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\}\otimes x.
  \label{eq:optdigits-score}
\end{equation}
The reward is $R(x,a)=\mathbb{1}\{a=y(x)\}$, and the prompt-only baseline is the
target policy's probability of the correct label.  Hence $|A|\le1$, matching the
bounded-reward regime discussed in Proposition~\ref{prop:g2-bound}.

To isolate the coverage effect, we hold this evaluation policy and its gradient
contributions fixed.  We draw one zero-mean Gaussian perturbation of the
action logits for every context and define six logging policies by multiplying
that perturbation by
\begin{equation}
  \alpha\in\{0,0.5,1,1.5,2,2.5\}.
  \label{eq:logger-perturbation}
\end{equation}
Because this perturbation is sampled independently of $H_\theta$, it varies
coverage without deliberately aligning low coverage with large gradients.  In
the realized sweep, $G_{2,\theta}$ remains between $2.65$ and $3.12$, while
$\rho_\theta$ ranges from $1$ to $0.0025$.  This controlled design makes ESS the
main varying quantity rather than a post hoc correlate.

\subsection{Population quantities and sampled estimators}

For each logger $Q$, contexts are uniform over the $M$ examples and actions are
drawn from $Q(\cdot\mid x)$.  We enumerate all $M\times10$ context-action pairs
to calculate
\begin{align}
  g
  &=\frac1M\sum_{x=1}^M\sum_{a=1}^{10}
    P_\theta(a\mid x)H_\theta(x,a),
    \label{eq:optdigits-gradient}\\
  \rho_\theta^{-1}
  &=\frac1M\sum_{x=1}^M\sum_{a=1}^{10}
    Q(a\mid x)W_\theta(x,a)^2,
    \label{eq:optdigits-rho}\\
  G_{2,\theta}
  &=\rho_\theta\frac1M\sum_{x=1}^M\sum_{a=1}^{10}
    Q(a\mid x)W_\theta(x,a)^2\|H_\theta(x,a)\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:optdigits-g2}
\end{align}
Substitution into \eqref{eq:ess-factorization} gives the predicted raw MSE
without Monte Carlo approximation.

For each logger, we draw 100 independent batches with $N=256$.  We compute the
raw-gradient squared error and update once with learning rate $0.8$.  The full
population then gives the exact reward before and after each estimated update.
The same step using the population gradient supplies a reference gain.

\subsection{Fixed-rollout optimization and ESS gate}

The optimization study starts from the same policy.  Its logging policy mixes
the initial policy with ten percent uniform exploration and remains fixed for
the entire run.  Each of 100 runs draws one batch of 256 interactions and takes
35 updates on that batch with learning rate $5$.  The three methods are
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_W,
  \qquad
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{PPO}}
  =\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N W_iM_{\epsilon,i}H_i,
  \qquad
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{gate}}
  =\begin{cases}
    \widehat g_W,&\widehat\rho\ge\lambda,\\
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{PPO}},&\widehat\rho<\lambda,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:experimental-estimators}
\end{equation}
with $\epsilon=1$.  The threshold grid is fixed in advance.  The first 40 runs
select $\lambda$ by final population reward, and the remaining 60 evaluate the
frozen threshold.  The selected value is $\lambda=0.45$.

becomes negative in the lowest-ESS regime.  This is the empirical bridge needed
by the decision rule: ESS predicts when the raw update ceases to be reliable,
and that unreliability has a measurable policy consequence.
