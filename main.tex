\title{\bf When Does Clipping Help?\\
Effective Sample Size and Policy-Gradient Reliability}
\author{Dongkyu Derek Cho, Thomas Voice}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Policy optimization in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards repeatedly updates a
learner using data generated by a fixed rollout policy.  As the learner moves away from that
policy, the nominal batch size can substantially overstate the amount of information available
for the next update.  We study this problem at the level of the policy-gradient estimator
itself.  Viewing the gradient as a vector-valued importance-sampling estimator, we show that
its mean-squared error scales inversely with sequence effective sample size (ESS), where
$\mathrm{ESS}=N\exp\{-D_2(P_\theta\|Q)\}$.  Combining this reliability bound with a smoothness
argument yields a high-probability lower bound on the population improvement of an adaptive
gradient step.  The result makes the statistical mechanism explicit: larger ESS shrinks the
gradient-error radius, permits a larger certified step, and increases the guaranteed
improvement.  A one-pass disjoint-minibatch construction supplies the conditional freshness
needed by the theory, with an extension to intact prompt groups.  An exactly evaluable
contextual bandit verifies the predicted relation among ESS, gradient error, and harmful
updates, while language-model experiments show that sequence ESS remains an informative
operational diagnostic at scale.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) alternates between collecting a batch
of responses and optimizing a policy on that fixed batch.  Reusing the rollout is statistically
efficient, but it creates a moving-target problem: after several updates, the current policy no
longer matches the policy that generated the data.  The practical consequence is familiar.
Permissive updates can produce rapid early improvement, yet the same training run may become
unstable only after substantial reuse of the rollout.  Figure~\ref{fig:llm-motivation}
illustrates this delayed transition in Qwen3-30B-A3B training.

  \centering

  \caption{Empirical motivation in Qwen3-30B-A3B RLVR.  Two permissive
  coefficient-capped updates improve rapidly and fail only after normalized
  sequence ESS nearly vanishes.  The dotted line marks the prespecified $0.1$
  diagnostic studied later.}
  \label{fig:llm-motivation}
\end{figure}

The central question is therefore statistical: \emph{when does a fixed rollout still support
a reliable policy-gradient update for the current learner?}  A ratio threshold describes an
individual sample, and an update count records how long the rollout has been reused, but neither
directly measures the amount of effective information retained by the batch.  Sequence effective
sample size (ESS) does.  If $W=dP_\theta/dQ$ is the complete-response likelihood ratio, then
$\mathrm{ESS}=N/\mathbb E_Q[W^2]=N\exp\{-D_2(P_\theta\|Q)\}$.  Thus the order-two
R\'enyi divergence converts policy mismatch into an effective sample count.

This connection is classical for scalar importance-sampling estimators
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  The missing link for our setting is the
gradient itself.  A reliable estimate of the sampled reward does not in general imply a
reliable derivative.  We therefore treat the policy gradient directly as a vector-valued
importance-sampling estimand.  Under a normalized weighted second-moment condition on the
gradient contribution, the conditional mean-squared error of the estimator is at most
$\sigma_g^2/\mathrm{ESS}$.  In this sense, effective rather than nominal sample size
governs gradient reliability.

Estimator reliability matters because an update is useful only through its effect on the
population objective.  Smooth policy-gradient analyses convert a high-probability gradient
error bound into a guaranteed improvement bound
\citep{pirotta2013adaptive,papini2022smoothing}.  Combining that argument with the
ESS-controlled error radius gives the main result of this paper: in fixed-rollout RLVR,
nominal batch size in the one-step improvement guarantee is replaced by sequence ESS.
A larger ESS produces a smaller uncertainty radius, a larger admissible step, and a larger
certified increase in population reward.

The theorem is finite-update rather than asymptotic.  It is applied conditionally at the
current policy, using an unused minibatch from a one-pass disjoint traversal.  This avoids a
global optimization argument and makes the role of sample reuse explicit.  The result also
clarifies what ESS does and does not provide.  ESS controls the amplification caused by
importance weights; the scale of the gradient integrand remains a separate problem-dependent
quantity, just as the range of a scalar integrand remains in standard importance-sampling
bounds.  Consequently, the theory yields no universal numerical ESS threshold.

We test each link in an exactly evaluable contextual bandit.  The finite population exposes
the true gradient and reward, allowing us to measure estimator error and the population
effect of each sampled update.  Lower ESS predicts larger gradient error and a sharply higher
frequency of harmful steps.  Independent redraws at frozen policies provide a complementary
bias--variance diagnostic.  We then evaluate the operational implication in language-model
RLVR, where the true gradient is unavailable but sequence ESS is observable.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item We formulate the RLVR policy gradient as a vector-valued
  importance-sampling estimator and show that, under a normalized weighted
  second-moment condition, its conditional mean-squared error is bounded by
  $\sigma_g^2/\mathrm{ESS}$.
  \item We combine the ESS-controlled reliability bound with policy-objective
  smoothness to obtain a concise high-probability guarantee for the population
  improvement of an adaptive gradient step.
  \item We validate the predicted reliability transition with an exact
  contextual-bandit oracle and evaluate sequence ESS as an operational
  diagnostic in language-model training.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Related perspectives}

\paragraph{Importance sampling and effective sample size.}
Policy optimization via importance sampling uses the second moment of the
likelihood ratio, equivalently order-two R\'enyi divergence, to quantify the
uncertainty introduced by off-distribution evaluation
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  These results show that the effective
sample count, rather than the nominal count, determines the variance of a
bounded scalar estimator.  We use the same principle for the vector-valued
policy-gradient contribution.

\paragraph{Reliable policy-gradient updates.}
Smoothness-based policy-gradient analyses lower-bound population improvement
from a finite gradient estimate and use the resulting bound to select step sizes
and sample sizes \citep{pirotta2013adaptive,papini2022smoothing}.  Our analysis
supplies the off-policy reliability radius entering that argument.  The resulting
theorem states directly how sequence ESS changes the certified update in
fixed-rollout RLVR.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:setting}

\subsection{Policy-gradient estimation in RLVR}

Let $X\sim\nu$ be a prompt, let $Q(\cdot\mid X)$ be the rollout policy, and let
$P_\theta(\cdot\mid X)$ be the current policy.  A complete response is denoted by
$Y=(Y_1,\ldots,Y_T)$, and $R(X,Y)$ is its verifier reward.  The population
objective is $J(\theta):=\mathbb E_{P_\theta}[R]$.  We assume
$P_\theta(\cdot\mid X)\ll Q(\cdot\mid X)$ and write the complete-response
likelihood ratio as $W_\theta(X,Y):=P_\theta(Y\mid X)/Q(Y\mid X)$.

Let $b(X)$ be a detached prompt-level baseline, set
$A_\theta(X,Y):=R(X,Y)-b(X)$, and define the sequence score
$S_\theta(X,Y):=\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y\mid X)$.  Since the response is
autoregressive, $S_\theta$ is the sum of the token scores.  With
$H_\theta:=A_\theta S_\theta$, the score identity and a change of measure give
\begin{equation}
  g(\theta):=\nabla J(\theta)
  =\mathbb E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta],
  \qquad
  \widehat g_N(\theta)
  :=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N W_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}.
  \label{eq:true-gradient}
\end{equation}
The first quantity is the population gradient and the second is its
importance-weighted estimate from $N$ rollout responses.

We analyze a single optimization phase.  The rollout batch is randomly
partitioned into disjoint minibatches before optimization, and each minibatch is
used at most once in the theoretical traversal.  Let $\mathcal F_{k-1}$ denote
all information available before update $k$.  Then the current parameter
$\theta_k$ is $\mathcal F_{k-1}$-measurable, while the unused minibatch is fresh
conditional on $\mathcal F_{k-1}$.  The estimator in
Equation~\eqref{eq:true-gradient} is therefore conditionally unbiased at the
current policy.  When several responses share a prompt or a group-relative
baseline, the intact prompt group is treated as one independent sampling unit;
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} gives the corresponding bound.

\subsection{R\'enyi divergence and effective sample size}

For $\alpha>1$, the R\'enyi divergence from the current policy to the rollout
policy is
$D_\alpha(P_\theta\|Q):=(\alpha-1)^{-1}\log\mathbb E_Q[W_\theta^\alpha]$.
The order-two case is matched to squared estimation error:
$d_2(P_\theta\|Q):=\exp\{D_2(P_\theta\|Q)\}=\mathbb E_Q[W_\theta^2]$.
Following importance-sampling policy optimization
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}, define the population effective sample
size and its normalized version by
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ESS}_N(\theta\|Q)
  :=\frac{N}{d_2(P_\theta\|Q)}
  =N\exp\{-D_2(P_\theta\|Q)\},
  \qquad
  \rho_\theta:=\frac{\mathrm{ESS}_N(\theta\|Q)}{N}.
  \label{eq:population-ess}
\end{equation}
When $P_\theta=Q$, $\mathrm{ESS}_N=N$.  As the policies separate,
$D_2(P_\theta\|Q)$ increases and the effective count decreases.  The identity
$\mathbb E_Q[(W_\theta-1)^2]=\rho_\theta^{-1}-1$ shows that this decrease is
exactly the growth of the centered ratio second moment.

\section{Theoretical analysis}
\label{sec:theory}

The analysis has two steps.  First, sequence ESS controls the error of the
importance-weighted policy-gradient estimator.  Second, objective smoothness
translates that error into a population-improvement guarantee for the realized
update.

\subsection{ESS controls gradient-estimator reliability}
\label{sec:raw}

Condition on $\mathcal F_{k-1}$ and abbreviate
$g_k:=g(\theta_k)$, $\widehat g_k:=\widehat g_N(\theta_k)$, and
$\mathrm{ESS}_k:=\mathrm{ESS}_N(\theta_k\|Q)$.  We impose the vector analogue
of the integrand-scale condition used in scalar importance-sampling bounds.

\begin{assumption}[Normalized weighted gradient moment]
\label{ass:weighted-gradient-moment}
There is a finite $\sigma_{g,k}$, measurable with respect to
$\mathcal F_{k-1}$, such that
$\mathbb E_Q[W_k^2\|H_k\|_2^2\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]
 /\mathbb E_Q[W_k^2\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]\le \sigma_{g,k}^2$.
\end{assumption}

Assumption~\ref{ass:weighted-gradient-moment} does not bound the population
gradient.  It separates the scale of the vector integrand from the amplification
caused by distribution mismatch.  The latter is exactly the quantity summarized
by ESS.

\begin{lemma}[Gradient reliability under policy mismatch]
\label{lem:ess-gradient-reliability}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:weighted-gradient-moment}, the current estimator is
conditionally unbiased, its conditional mean-squared error is at most
$\sigma_{g,k}^2/\mathrm{ESS}_k$, and for any $\delta\in(0,1)$ its error is at
most $\sigma_{g,k}/\sqrt{\delta\,\mathrm{ESS}_k}$ with conditional probability
at least $1-\delta$.
\end{lemma}

Lemma~\ref{lem:ess-gradient-reliability} is the direct gradient counterpart of
the scalar importance-sampling bounds: once the gradient scale is fixed,
reliability improves at the rate $\mathrm{ESS}_k^{-1/2}$.  In normalized form,
the radius is $\sigma_{g,k}/\sqrt{\delta N\rho_k}$, so nominal batch size is
discounted exactly by current policy mismatch.

\subsection{Reliable gradients imply population improvement}
\label{sec:mse-improvement}

The next result combines Lemma~\ref{lem:ess-gradient-reliability} with the
standard quadratic lower bound for a smooth policy objective
\citep{pirotta2013adaptive,papini2022smoothing}.  Its assumptions and step size
are stated inline so that the main conclusion remains visible.

\begin{theorem}[ESS-controlled policy improvement]
\label{thm:current-ess}
Suppose Assumption~\ref{ass:weighted-gradient-moment} holds, $J$ is
$L_k$-smooth along the update segment, and $\delta\in(0,1)$.  Write
$\varepsilon_k(\delta):=\sigma_{g,k}/\sqrt{\delta\,\mathrm{ESS}_k}$ and choose
$\alpha_k:=L_k^{-1}(1-\varepsilon_k(\delta)/\|\widehat g_k\|_2)_+$, with
$\alpha_k=0$ when $\widehat g_k=0$.  Then, conditional on
$\mathcal F_{k-1}$, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta_k+\alpha_k\widehat g_k)-J(\theta_k)
  \ge
  \frac{1}{2L_k}
  \left(
    \|\widehat g_k\|_2
    -
    \frac{\sigma_{g,k}}
         {\sqrt{\delta\,\mathrm{ESS}_k}}
  \right)_+^2 .
  \label{eq:ess-policy-improvement}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Effective rather than nominal sample size]
\label{rem:ess-interpretation}
Theorem~\ref{thm:current-ess} states the paper's central mechanism.  On-policy,
$\mathrm{ESS}_k=N$ and the uncertainty radius has the usual $N^{-1/2}$ rate.
Off-policy, $N$ is replaced by $N\exp\{-D_2(P_{\theta_k}\|Q)\}$.  A larger
sequence ESS therefore yields a smaller gradient-error radius, a larger
certified step, and a larger guaranteed improvement.  The lower bound is
strictly positive only when the observed gradient norm exceeds its uncertainty
radius.  Consequently, the relevant ESS level depends on the batch size,
confidence level, gradient scale, and current signal; no universal numerical
threshold follows from the theorem.
\end{remark}

The result is local and finite-update.  It does not require the current policy
to maximize a sampled surrogate, nor does it make an asymptotic stationarity
claim.  The one-pass condition is used only to ensure that the minibatch remains
fresh for the current adaptive policy.

\subsection{Observable reliability diagnostic}
\label{sec:practice}

Population ESS is unavailable during training.  We use the standard normalized
plug-in estimate
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho_k
  :=
  \frac{(\sum_{i=1}^N W_{k,i})^2}
       {N\sum_{i=1}^N W_{k,i}^2}.
  \label{eq:sample-ess}
\end{equation}
It is computed from complete-response ratios before the update.  A finite batch
can miss rare large-ratio responses and therefore overstate population support,
so $\widehat\rho_k$ is an observable diagnostic rather than a finite-sample
substitute inside Theorem~\ref{thm:current-ess}.  The numerical value $0.1$
used in the experiments is prespecified as a conservative operational event; it
is not a constant derived from Equation~\eqref{eq:ess-policy-improvement}.

\section{Contextual-bandit validation of the theory}
\label{sec:simulation}

The contextual bandit tests the two links in
Theorem~\ref{thm:current-ess}.  First, sequence ESS should track the error of the
importance-weighted policy-gradient estimator.  Second, updates made from
unreliable estimates should be more likely to reduce the true population
objective.  Because the finite population exposes both the exact gradient and
the exact reward, these quantities can be measured directly.  We additionally
compare alternative gradient estimators at frozen policies as a method-specific
bias--variance diagnostic.  The experiment does not estimate the unknown
$\sigma_{g,k}$ or directly test the adaptive step size in
Equation~\eqref{eq:ess-policy-improvement}.

\subsection{Testbed and oracle}

We use the standard reduction from classification to a contextual bandit on Optdigits
\citep{wang2017adaptive,alpaydin1998optdigits}.  Each handwritten-digit image is
a context, the policy emits a 16-token binary encoding of the digit, and an
exact-match verifier supplies reward.  Because the dataset is a finite
population, we can enumerate the exact reward and policy gradient.  The
sequence action still produces the product importance ratios that drive ESS in
RLVR, so the testbed removes language-model complexity without removing the
statistical mechanism of interest.

The optimization loop follows grouped RLVR
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath,yu2025dapo}.  Each rollout samples 128 prompts and
16 responses per prompt, computes leave-one-out advantages, and supplies four
epochs of 16 sequential minibatch updates.  Ratios and ESS are recomputed before
every update, and a new rollout is collected only afterward.  This repeated-epoch
trajectory is a stress test beyond the one-epoch freshness condition in
Theorem~\ref{thm:current-ess}; only the first traversal has the required disjoint
freshness, and the frozen-policy redraws provide an independent estimator
diagnostic.  We run 100 paired replications; Appendix~\ref{app:bandit}
gives the compact protocol and full result tables.

\subsection{The predicted regime change appears}

ESS first predicts the reliability of the permissive gradient.  Its MSE rises
from $1.6\times10^{-2}$ above ESS $0.8$ to $1.31\times10^{-1}$ between $0.03$
and $0.1$, and to $4.77\times10^{-1}$ below $0.03$.  In the same two low-ESS
regions, permissive steps reduce population reward by $4.0\%$ and $13.3\%$,
while matched oracle-gradient steps improve reward.  ESS therefore identifies
estimation failure rather than the absence of an improving direction.

Frozen-policy redraws then measure the gradient-MSE decomposition in
Equation~\eqref{eq:crossover}.  With the fixed $N=512$ diagnostic, the
sequence-level mask has
lower MSE below population ESS $0.4$, while the permissive estimator has lower
MSE above it.  A disjoint calibration split places the crossover at $0.42$ and
reduces held-out MSE by $4.3\%\pm1.1\%$ relative to the better static estimator.
Thus ESS predicts which estimator has lower gradient error under this fixed
construction; it does not establish which update has greater population gain.

Finally, we evaluate the deliberately conservative rule
$\widehat\rho<0.1$.  It selects only $3.3\%$ of updates; within that event, PPO
has lower MSE on $83.7\%\pm1.4\%$ of updates and reduces
conditional MSE by $53.3\%\pm2.9\%$.  Across eight rollout cycles, each with four
optimization epochs over its fixed batch, the resulting trajectory reaches
$0.4940\pm0.0050$ final reward, compared with
$0.4606\pm0.0098$ for always permissive and $0.4038\pm0.0005$ for always
masked.  The sparse trigger therefore captures the low-ESS side of the
  empirical diagnostic region with an observable training statistic.

The controlled study therefore supports the empirical claim that ESS tracks a
reliability transition relevant to the sequence-level masking analogue.  It
does not by itself establish the objective certificate or transfer the result
to token-local PPO, CISPO, or full-sequence truncation.  The next section asks
whether a related operational transition is visible in language-model RLVR,
where the population gradient cannot be enumerated.

\section{Language-model evidence for the regime change}
\label{sec:llm-experiments}

The contextual-bandit study provides controlled evidence for adjacent
diagnostics under an exact oracle, but its repeated-epoch trajectory is outside
the one-epoch theorem and does not directly test the confidence certificates.
This section tests its operational implication in language-model training,
where neither the population gradient nor its MSE is observable.  If ESS marks
the predicted reliability transition, a conservative intervention should retain
permissive updates while ESS is high and prevent their late failure after ESS
falls.  The experiment asks whether this reliability transition remains visible
at language-model scale.

\subsection{A minimal intervention}

At every policy update, we compute sequence ESS from the raw ratios before any
masking or truncation.  All methods use the same valid sequences and minibatch
construction.  We then evaluate $0.1$ as a prespecified heuristic:
when $\widehat\rho\ge0.1$, the method retains the permissive update; when
$\widehat\rho<0.1$, it activates the safeguard associated with the comparison.
We test conventional GRPO clipping, Clip-Higher \citep{yu2025dapo}, the
divergence constraint of DPPO \citep{qi2026dppo}.  We also pair conventional
clipping with a permissive TIS update whose ratio cap is 3.

\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{LLM}}
  =\begin{cases}
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{permissive}},&\widehat\rho\ge0.1,\\
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{safeguard}},&\widehat\rho<0.1.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:llm-gate}
\end{equation}
Each conditional run is compared with its always-permissive and
always-protected counterparts.  This pairing tests the regime prediction while
holding the safeguard itself fixed.  The threshold is set before the reported
runs and is shared across update rules, providing one observable approximation
to the low-support regime.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

We optimize Qwen3-30B-A3B for 200 training steps and evaluate on AIME-2024 with
mean@16.  The primary comparison is the complete evaluation trajectory and its
step-200 endpoint.  We also align normalized sequence ESS and the fraction of
clipped updates to the training axis to verify that the rule responds to a loss
of effective support.  The supplied artifacts report one trajectory per
configuration, so we report endpoint differences without uncertainty and treat
them as trajectory-level evidence.

\subsection{Observed behavior}

Figure~\ref{fig:llm-main} gives a consistent trajectory-level result.  The
ESS-conditional runs improve the GRPO, Clip-Higher, and DPPO endpoints by
$6.9$ to $12.3$ percentage points.  The TIS comparison isolates the stability
failure most clearly: the static TIS 3 run improves early but finishes at
$0.4\%$, whereas the conditional run finishes at $44.8\%$.  The consistency
across safeguards supports ESS as a shared reliability coordinate.

Detailed response-length, entropy, and TIS diagnostics are reported in

\section{Conclusion}

Fixed-rollout policy optimization is reliable only to the extent that the
rollout continues to support the current learner.  This paper makes that
statement precise at the level of the policy gradient.  The gradient is a
vector-valued importance-sampling estimator, and its conditional mean-squared
error is bounded by a problem-dependent gradient scale divided by sequence
ESS.  Smoothness then converts the resulting high-probability error radius into
a lower bound on one-step population improvement.  The main conclusion is
simple: effective sample size replaces nominal batch size in the reliable
policy-gradient update.

The result explains why a rollout can support many efficient updates before
failing abruptly.  As the current policy moves away from the rollout policy,
order-two R\'enyi divergence increases, sequence ESS decreases, and the
certified gradient radius expands.  The theorem does not identify a universal
ESS threshold, because the transition also depends on gradient scale and signal
strength.  The contextual bandit confirms the predicted relation among ESS,
gradient error, and harmful updates, while the language-model experiments show
that the same sequence-level diagnostic remains informative at scale.  A
natural next step is to estimate the gradient-scale term online and use the
resulting uncertainty radius directly in adaptive update rules.

The testbed uses a sequence policy, not a language model.  Its token heads are
conditionally independent so that the population gradient remains exact, but
its response ratio is the same product of token ratios that appears in RLVR.
During training, the learner observes only sampled responses, rollout log
probabilities, and verifier rewards.  Labels are used only by the oracle that
evaluates the finite-population reward and gradient.  This separation preserves
the RLVR feedback structure while exposing the quantities needed to test the
theory.

\subsection{Finite population and initial policy}

Optdigits is a handwritten-digit classification benchmark with 5,620 labeled
examples
\citep{alpaydin1998optdigits}.  Each original $32\times32$ binary image is
partitioned into $4\times4$ blocks; foreground counts produce 64 integer
features in $\{0,\ldots,16\}$.  We concatenate the supplied 3,823 training and
1,797 test examples, divide each feature by 16, and append an intercept.  This
gives a finite population $\{(x_j,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{M}$ with $M=5{,}620$ and
$x_j\in\mathbb{R}^{65}$.  The original split is not used because the estimand
is finite-population policy optimization, not classification generalization.

Each label $y\in\{0,\ldots,9\}$ has a four-bit binary representation.  Let
$c(y)\in\{0,1\}^{16}$ repeat this code four times.  The repetition changes only
the response length, not which answer is correct, and creates a sequence ratio
that compounds over 16 token probabilities.  The policy has one Bernoulli head
per token position,
\begin{align}
  p_{\theta,\ell}(x)&=\sigma(\theta_\ell^\top x),\nonumber\\
  P_\theta(a_{1:16}\mid x)
  &=\prod_{\ell=1}^{16}
    p_{\theta,\ell}(x)^{a_\ell}
    \{1-p_{\theta,\ell}(x)\}^{1-a_\ell},
  \qquad \theta\in\mathbb{R}^{16\times65}.
  \label{eq:optdigits-policy}
\end{align}
The verifier reward is
$R(x_j,a_{1:16})=\mathbf{1}\{a_{1:16}=c(y_j)\}$.

Initialization is deterministic and uses no experimental outcomes.  Starting
from zero, we fit all token heads for 500 full-population logistic-regression
steps with step size $0.8$ and no regularization.  We then multiply the fitted
weights by $0.55$ to keep the initial policy stochastic, which gives exact
initial reward $V(\theta_0)=0.095766$.  This procedure uses no data split,
minibatching, early stopping, or model selection.

For compactness, define
\begin{equation}
  q_j(\theta)
  :=P_\theta(c(y_j)\mid x_j)
  =\prod_{\ell=1}^{16}
  \left[c_{j\ell}p_{j\ell}
  +(1-c_{j\ell})(1-p_{j\ell})\right],
  \label{eq:correct-sequence-probability}
\end{equation}
where $c_{j\ell}=c(y_j)_\ell$ and
$p_{j\ell}=p_{\theta,\ell}(x_j)$.  The finite-population objective is
\begin{equation}
  V(\theta)=\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^{M}q_j(\theta).
  \label{eq:optdigits-value}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Exact gradient and group-relative advantage}

The Bernoulli score identity gives the exact finite-population gradient
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_{\theta_\ell}V(\theta)
  =\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^{M}
    q_j(\theta)\{c_{j\ell}-p_{j\ell}\}x_j.
  \label{eq:optdigits-gradient}
\end{equation}
where $q_j(\theta)$ is the correct-sequence probability,
$c_{j\ell}$ is its bit at position $\ell$, and $p_{j\ell}$ is the policy
probability of bit one.  The oracle enumerates all 5,620 contexts at the current
pre-update policy.  We measure a sampled estimator by
\begin{equation}
  E_t=\|\widehat g_t-\nabla V(\theta_t)\|_F^2,
  \label{eq:optdigits-gradient-error}
\end{equation}
and report its mean within each fixed ESS interval.

For response $g$ to prompt $i$, we use the leave-one-out advantage
\begin{equation}
  A_{ig}=R_{ig}-\frac1{G-1}\sum_{h\ne g}R_{ih}
  =\frac{G}{G-1}(R_{ig}-\bar R_i).
  \label{eq:loo-group-advantage}
\end{equation}
It has the sign of the group-centered GRPO advantage
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}; standard-deviation scaling is omitted so every
minibatch targets the same population gradient.  The baseline excludes the
focal response and is therefore independent of it conditional on the prompt.
Importance weighting and the zero-mean score identity then preserve the target
in Equation~\eqref{eq:optdigits-gradient}.

\subsection{Rollout and minibatch procedure}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{RLVR-style OptDigits policy optimization}
\label{alg:bandit-loop}
\centering
\fbox{\begin{minipage}{0.92\linewidth}
\small
\textbf{Input:} initial policy $\theta_0$; eight rollouts; 128 prompts per
rollout; $G=16$ responses per prompt; 16 minibatches; four optimization epochs.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Set the rollout policy $Q\leftarrow P_\theta$.  Sample prompts and
  grouped responses, then compute verifier rewards and leave-one-out advantages.
  \item Randomly partition the intact prompt groups into 16 minibatches, each
  containing eight groups and $N=128$ complete responses.
  \item In each of four optimization epochs, traverse all minibatches.  Before
  every update, recompute $W=P_\theta(Y\mid x)/Q(Y\mid x)$, policy scores, and
  sample ESS.  Form the designated raw, PPO-masked, or conditional estimator.
  \item After all 64 updates, discard the rollout and repeat from the new policy.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}}
\end{algorithm}

Every response enters one update per optimization epoch.  Within a rollout,
responses, rewards, advantages, and rollout probabilities remain fixed, whereas
current probabilities, ratios, scores, and ESS are recomputed before each
update.  Theorem~\ref{thm:current-ess} applies to the first disjoint traversal; later
epochs deliberately test behavior after sample reuse.  Sixteen minibatches are
used because the first four never reach ESS below $0.1$ in the diagnostic runs;
a shorter traversal would not expose the regime under study.

\subsection{Estimators and diagnostics}

Let $S_{ig,t}=\nabla_\theta\log
P_{\theta_{r,t-1}}(Y_{ig}\mid x_i)$.  For each $N=128$ minibatch, the
permissive and PPO-masked gradients are
\begin{align}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{raw},t}
  &=\frac1N\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t}A_{ig}S_{ig,t},
    \label{eq:simulation-raw-gradient}\\
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{PPO},t}
  &=\frac1N\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t}M_{ig,t}^{\mathrm{seq}}A_{ig}S_{ig,t},
    \label{eq:simulation-ppo-gradient}
\end{align}
where the experiment uses the response-level analogue
\begin{equation}
  M_{ig,t}^{\mathrm{seq}}
  =\mathbf{1}\{A_{ig}\ge0,W_{ig,t}\le1+\epsilon\}
   +\mathbf{1}\{A_{ig}<0,W_{ig,t}\ge1-\epsilon\},
  \qquad \epsilon=0.2.
  \label{eq:simulation-sequence-mask}
\end{equation}
This construction applies the advantage-sign mask once to the complete-response
ratio rather than separately to token ratios.  It is therefore a controlled
sequence-level masking analogue, not the exact token-local PPO/GRPO gradient and
not numerical ratio truncation.  The third method uses
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{cond},t}
  =\begin{cases}
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{raw},t},&\widehat\rho_t\ge0.1,\\
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{PPO},t},&\widehat\rho_t<0.1,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad
  \widehat\rho_t
  =\frac{(\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t})^2}
  {N\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t}^2}.
  \label{eq:simulation-gate}
\end{equation}
The $0.1$ threshold is fixed before the runs and is not selected from a reported
outcome.  All methods share the initialization, prompt draws, response-sampling
uniforms, and group permutations within each of 100 paired replications.

The first 20 conditional replications provide 2,560 pre-update diagnostics.  At
each state, we evaluate the raw gradient against
Equation~\eqref{eq:optdigits-gradient} and compare its counterfactual reward
step with the exact-gradient step.  Results use seven prespecified ESS intervals:
\[
  \begin{gathered}
    \relax[0,0.03),\ [0.03,0.1),\ [0.1,0.2),\ [0.2,0.4),\\
    [0.4,0.6),\ [0.6,0.8),\ [0.8,1].
  \end{gathered}
\]
Within-bin error bars are standard errors.  Optimization curves use all 100
replications and report relative population-reward improvement from the common
initial policy with 95\% confidence intervals.  One rollout costs 2,048
verifier responses.

\subsection{Fixed-checkpoint crossover diagnostic}

To estimate conditional MSE rather than one realized error, we freeze the
rollout and current policies at selected pre-update checkpoints and redraw
independent minibatches.  This diagnostic does not change training.

The finite Bernoulli policy also makes population sequence ESS exact.  For
prompt $x$ and token position $\ell$, let $q_{x\ell}$ and $p_{x\ell}$ denote
the rollout and current probabilities of token one.  Independence across the
16 response positions gives
\begin{align}
  \E_Q[W^2\mid x]
  &=\prod_{\ell=1}^{16}
    \left\{
      \frac{p_{x\ell}^2}{q_{x\ell}}
      +\frac{(1-p_{x\ell})^2}{1-q_{x\ell}}
    \right\},
    \label{eq:simulation-weight-second-moment}\\
  \rho_{\mathrm{pop}}
  &=\left\{
    \frac1{5620}\sum_{x=1}^{5620}\E_Q[W^2\mid x]
  \right\}^{-1}.
    \label{eq:simulation-population-ess}
\end{align}
These equations use $\E_Q[W]=1$ and contain no minibatch noise.  We evaluate
eight checkpoints from each of the seven ESS intervals, giving 56 frozen states
selected from the 2,560 trajectory checkpoints.  At each state, 32 paired
redraws compare raw and PPO-masked estimates.  One independent block contains
eight prompts and 16 responses per prompt, hence $N=128$ sequences; the
$N=512$ estimate averages four blocks.  The block is the independent unit
because responses within a prompt share an advantage.  For checkpoint $c$,
estimator $e$, and size $N$, we report
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{c,e,N}
  =\frac1{32}\sum_{s=1}^{32}
    \left\|
      \widehat g_{c,e,N}^{(s)}-\nabla V(\theta_c)
    \right\|_F^2.
  \label{eq:simulation-crossover-mse}
\end{equation}
We average redraws within a state and states within an ESS interval; standard
errors use the eight states as independent units.  PPO reduction is
$100(\widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\mathrm{raw}}-
\widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\mathrm{PPO}})/
\widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\mathrm{raw}}$.  Positive values favor masking.

\subsection{Formula calibration and held-out decision test}

We split the eight states in each ESS interval into four calibration and four
test states, yielding 28 of each.  At every state,
100 paired $N=128$ blocks estimate the raw covariance trace $\widehat V_W$, the
PPO covariance trace $\widehat V_C$, and the PPO squared bias $\widehat B_C$.
The squared-bias estimate subtracts the sampling variance of the estimated mean
and is truncated at zero.  Averaging $K\in\{1,2,4,8\}$ independent blocks gives
\begin{equation}
  \widehat R_W(K):=\frac{\widehat V_W}{K},
  \qquad
  \widehat R_C(K):=\widehat B_C+\frac{\widehat V_C}{K}.
  \label{eq:formula-risks}
\end{equation}
These are the two sides of the diagnostic decomposition in
Equation~\eqref{eq:crossover} at block level.  For each
$K$, we choose the ESS midpoint that minimizes fitted risk while applying PPO
below the threshold and raw above it.  The only interior calibration solution is
$K=4$, or $N=512$, with threshold $0.417$.

Held-out evaluation uses 100 paired redraws at each test state.
The fitted threshold never sees test-state errors; standard errors use the 28
states as independent units.  The subsequent optimization study freezes that
threshold, uses 512 sequences per update, traverses each rollout for four epochs,
and runs eight rollout cycles in 100 paired replications.  Sample ESS, rather
than population ESS, determines the training intervention.

The fixed-$0.1$ trajectory also records shadow raw and PPO gradients whenever
sample ESS is below $0.417$.  Comparing each with the exact gradient yields the
three intervals $[0,0.1)$, $[0.1,0.25)$, and $[0.25,0.417)$.  These shadow
calculations do not alter training.  Means and standard errors use the 100
replications as independent units.

\section{Proofs for the theoretical results}
\label{app:proofs}

This appendix proves the gradient-reliability lemma and the population-improvement
theorem.  It then records the intact-group extension and the generic
bias--variance identity used by the frozen-policy diagnostics.

\subsection{Conditional policy-gradient identity}

Condition on $\mathcal F_{k-1}$.  The current parameter is fixed and the unused
responses are drawn from $Q$.  Since $W_k=dP_{\theta_k}/dQ$ and the baseline is
detached and prompt-measurable,
\begin{align}
  \mathbb E_Q[W_kH_k\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]
  &=
  \mathbb E_{P_{\theta_k}}
  [(R-b(X))\nabla_\theta\log P_{\theta_k}(Y\mid X)
   \mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]\\
  &=
  \nabla J(\theta_k)
  =g_k .
  \label{eq:conditional-gradient-identity}
\end{align}
The baseline term vanishes because the conditional expectation of the score is
zero.

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:ess-gradient-reliability}}

Let $Z_i:=W_{k,i}H_{k,i}$.  Conditional independence and
Equation~\eqref{eq:conditional-gradient-identity} give
\begin{align}
  \mathbb E[
    \|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2
    \mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]
  &=
  \frac1N
  \mathbb E_Q[
    \|Z_1-g_k\|_2^2
    \mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]\\
  &=
  \frac1N
  \left\{
    \mathbb E_Q[
      W_k^2\|H_k\|_2^2
      \mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]
    -
    \|g_k\|_2^2
  \right\}\\
  &\le
  \frac{\sigma_{g,k}^2}
       {\mathrm{ESS}_k}.
  \label{eq:ess-gradient-mse}
\end{align}
The last line uses Assumption~\ref{ass:weighted-gradient-moment} and
$\mathrm{ESS}_k=N/\mathbb E_Q[W_k^2\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]$.
Applying Markov's inequality to
$\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2$ yields
\[
  \Pr\left(
    \|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2
    >
    \frac{\sigma_{g,k}}
         {\sqrt{\delta\,\mathrm{ESS}_k}}
    \,\middle|\,
    \mathcal F_{k-1}
  \right)
  \le\delta ,
\]
which proves the high-probability statement.

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:current-ess}}

Let $\varepsilon_k=\varepsilon_k(\delta)$ and consider the event
$\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2\le\varepsilon_k$, which has conditional probability at
least $1-\delta$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:ess-gradient-reliability}.  Smoothness gives,
for any $\alpha\ge0$,
\begin{align}
  J(\theta_k+\alpha\widehat g_k)-J(\theta_k)
  &\ge
  \alpha\langle g_k,\widehat g_k\rangle
  -\frac{L_k\alpha^2}{2}\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2\\
  &\ge
  \alpha\|\widehat g_k\|_2
  \{\|\widehat g_k\|_2-\varepsilon_k\}
  -\frac{L_k\alpha^2}{2}\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2 .
  \label{eq:smoothness-with-error}
\end{align}
The second line follows from Cauchy--Schwarz:
$\langle g_k,\widehat g_k\rangle
=\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2+
\langle g_k-\widehat g_k,\widehat g_k\rangle
\ge\|\widehat g_k\|_2
(\|\widehat g_k\|_2-\varepsilon_k)$.
The right-hand side of Equation~\eqref{eq:smoothness-with-error} is a concave
quadratic in $\alpha$.  Its nonnegative maximizer is
$\alpha_k=L_k^{-1}(1-\varepsilon_k/\|\widehat g_k\|_2)_+$, with the stated
zero convention.  Substitution gives
Equation~\eqref{eq:ess-policy-improvement}.

\subsection{Intact prompt groups}

Suppose $m$ prompts are independent and each prompt has $G$ conditionally
independent responses.  Keep every prompt group intact and define its gradient
contribution by
$\Xi_{k,i}:=G^{-1}\sum_{g=1}^G W_{k,ig}H_{k,ig}$.  The minibatch estimator is
$m^{-1}\sum_i\Xi_{k,i}$.  A leave-one-out baseline is admissible because, for a
focal response, it is measurable with respect to the prompt and the other
responses and is conditionally independent of the focal score.  Jensen's
inequality gives
\[
  \|\Xi_{k,i}\|_2^2
  \le
  \frac1G\sum_{g=1}^G
  W_{k,ig}^2\|H_{k,ig}\|_2^2 .
\]
Therefore the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:ess-gradient-reliability} applies with
$m$ independent units and the corresponding member-level weighted moment.  The
resulting bound is conservative because it counts independent prompts rather
than all responses as independent observations.

\subsection{Bias--variance identity used in the diagnostics}

Let $X_W$ be an unbiased per-unit contribution with mean $g$, and let $X_C$ be
an alternative contribution.  Write $b_C:=\mathbb E[X_C]-g$ and let
$\Sigma_W$ and $\Sigma_C$ be the corresponding covariance matrices.  For
averages of $N$ independent units,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  <\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C)
  >
  N\|b_C\|_2^2 .
  \label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
Indeed,
$\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)=N^{-1}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)$ and
$\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)=\|b_C\|_2^2+
N^{-1}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C)$.
