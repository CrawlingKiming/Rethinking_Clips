\title{\bf When Does Clipping Help?\\
Effective Sample Size and Policy-Gradient Reliability}
\author{Dongkyu Derek Cho, Thomas Voice}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Clipping is widely treated as a safeguard for stable policy optimization.  Yet
permissive updates can learn faster and reach higher reward before sometimes
collapsing late in training.  Existing methods respond by changing clipping
boundaries or constraints, but this pattern lacks a common statistical
explanation.  We identify the effective support of the rollout data, measured
by effective sample size (ESS), as the missing link.  We prove that effective
support controls the gap between finite-batch and population policy improvement
for the current learner, without assuming a bounded policy-gradient norm.  A
smoothness argument then characterizes when the optimizer's realized
displacement raises the selected sampled objective, without requiring a global
argmax argument.  This analysis shows why permissive optimization can be reliable
while rollout coverage is broad and why a bounded full-sequence estimator can
give a stronger conservative certificate after coverage concentrates.  An
exactly evaluable contextual bandit measures associated ESS, gradient-error,
and policy-improvement diagnostics.
A simple ESS-triggered intervention carries this prediction to language-model
training, where the reported trajectories avoid late collapse and improve final
scores across several safeguards.  Together, the analysis and experiments
support ESS as a useful reliability coordinate for deciding when conservative
intervention may help.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Policy optimization on fixed rollout data is commonly stabilized by clipping.
The updated policy no longer matches the policy that generated the data, so PPO
suppresses selected samples after their likelihood ratios cross a prescribed
boundary \citep{schulman2017ppo}.  RLVR inherits this mechanism because an
expensive rollout batch is typically followed by several policy updates
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}.  This standard account treats clipping as the
protection needed when policy mismatch grows.

The empirical record is more complicated.  Clipping can discard gradients from
low-probability tokens that express useful new behavior, which can slow early
learning and alter entropy dynamics \citep{yu2025dapo,park2025clipping}.  More
permissive objectives can therefore learn faster and sometimes reach higher
reward, but the same runs may collapse later.  Figure~\ref{fig:llm-motivation}
shows this pattern for two truncated-importance-sampling (TIS) updates: both
improve rapidly without PPO clipping and fail only after sequence ESS approaches
zero.  The absence of clipping is therefore not itself the instability.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures_mains/motivation/overall.pdf}
  \caption{Empirical motivation in Qwen3-30B-A3B RLVR.  With TIS caps of 3
  and 5, AIME-2024 mean@16 initially improves without PPO clipping but later
  collapses as normalized sequence ESS vanishes.  The dotted line marks the
  prespecified $0.1$ threshold studied below.}
  \label{fig:llm-motivation}
\end{figure}

Existing approaches mainly change the intervention rather than explain this
change of regime.  A clipping threshold specifies how to alter an individual
sample, an update count measures time spent on a rollout, and a divergence
summarizes policy movement \citep{qi2026dppo}.  None directly answers whether
the fixed rollout still supports reliable evidence about the current policy.
Without that reliability variable, conservative and permissive objectives remain a
collection of useful but objective-specific heuristics.

This paper identifies sequence effective sample size (ESS) as the missing
reliability variable.  ESS is high when importance weight is distributed across
many responses and low when a few responses dominate the update.  Our central
claim is objective-level: current ESS controls how accurately an unused
minibatch estimates the population improvement of the current learner over its
rollout policy.  This view explains both sides of the empirical pattern:
permissive optimization can be efficient while finite-batch improvement remains
representative, whereas conservative control may become preferable when that
transfer becomes uncertain.

We establish this explanation through one theoretical chain.  First, the
one-epoch disjoint-minibatch structure makes the current unused minibatch a
valid estimator of current population improvement.  Bounded verifier reward
then yields a confidence radius proportional to
$\sqrt{(\rho_k^{-1}-1)/m}$, with no score-norm assumption.  Second, the bound
ranks the reliability of alternative estimators at the current policy.  Because
a practical optimizer does not globally maximize the selected random objective,
a smoothness inequality certifies the sampled gain of its realized displacement.
Third, we separate PPO/GRPO gradient
masking, CISPO token-coefficient capping, and full-sequence ratio truncation;
only the last directly inherits the bounded objective-estimator certificate.

An exactly evaluable contextual bandit tests each link without relying on an
LLM-scale proxy.  Its RLVR-style rollout loop preserves sequence ratios and
group-relative advantages, while its finite population exposes the true
gradient and reward.  Across 100 paired runs, lower ESS predicts larger
permissive-gradient error and harmful policy steps.  Independent redraws at
frozen policies then measure a supporting gradient-MSE crossover under a fixed
diagnostic construction.  Because gradient MSE is not itself an optimization
criterion and the training loop reuses data for four epochs, these results are
evidence for the proposed mechanism rather than a direct proof of the
one-epoch objective certificate.

The theory also yields a simple operational rule.  A conservative ESS threshold
preserves permissive learning while support is broad and activates protection
after support deteriorates.  In the controlled study, the prespecified $0.1$
trigger selects a sparse set of high-error updates and improves lower-tail
optimization.  In Qwen3-30B-A3B RLVR, the same trigger improves the reported
final AIME-2024 score under GRPO, Clip-Higher, DPPO, and TIS comparisons, and
prevents the late TIS collapse shown in Figure~\ref{fig:llm-motivation}.  These
trajectory-level results test the operational implication in the setting where
the true gradient is no longer observable; they do not directly validate the
current-policy confidence bound.

\subsection{Related perspectives}

\paragraph{Clipping in RLVR.}
PPO introduced clipping as a local policy-update mechanism
\citep{schulman2017ppo}, and GRPO carried this surrogate into RLVR
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}.  Recent methods expose the cost of applying the
same constraint throughout training.  DAPO relaxes the upper boundary,
Clip-Low changes entropy behavior, CISPO retains gradients beyond the PPO
boundary, and DPPO replaces ratio clipping with a divergence-based constraint
\citep{yu2025dapo,park2025clipping,minimax2025m1,qi2026dppo}.  These methods
change the intervention.  Our analysis identifies a common reliability signal
that can motivate intervention while distinguishing the guarantees available
for their different mathematical objects.

\paragraph{Reliability under policy mismatch.}
Importance-sampling analyses use second moments and ESS to quantify coverage
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  Recent LLM methods likewise adapt to
weight concentration, ESS, or sequence-level mismatch
\citep{zheng2025m2po,shen2026vespo,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}.  Our analysis
provides the missing theoretical link: current sequence ESS controls the
finite-to-population gap for current policy improvement.  We then connect this
objective-level guarantee to local policy optimization without introducing a
bounded score norm.

Published policy-gradient analyses use quadratic smoothness bounds to translate
a gradient step into guaranteed performance gain
\citep{pirotta2013adaptive,papini2022smoothing}; broader stochastic policy-gradient
analyses study how gradient estimation affects optimization
\citep{yuan2022general,kallus2020efficient,driggs2022biased}.  In our chain,
smoothness establishes ascent of the sampled reward objective for the realized
update.  Current ESS supplies the separate finite-to-population criterion used
to select which sampled estimator is reliable at that update.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item We derive a current-ESS certificate for finite-to-population policy
improvement in one-epoch RLVR without a bounded score or gradient norm.
\item We use a smooth empirical-ascent lemma to replace a global argmax
argument: after current ESS identifies the reliable estimator regime, the lemma
certifies when the realized optimizer displacement raises the selected sampled
objective.
\item We distinguish PPO/GRPO masking, CISPO coefficient capping, and
full-sequence ratio truncation, and show that only the last directly yields a
bounded conservative objective-estimation certificate.
\item We evaluate the predicted ESS--error relationship and supporting
estimator diagnostics with an exact contextual-bandit oracle, then
use a minimal ESS-triggered intervention to test its operational implication in
language-model RLVR.
\end{enumerate}

The next section states the fixed-rollout problem in which this reliability
question can be analyzed exactly.

\section{Preliminaries: fixed-rollout policy optimization}
\label{sec:setting}

RLVR alternates between data collection and optimization.  A rollout policy
generates a batch of complete responses, and a changing current policy is then
updated on that fixed batch.  We analyze one such optimization phase and keep
the rollout policy explicit throughout.

\subsection{Population target and sampled update}

Let $\theta\in\mathbb R^d$ denote the policy parameter.  Let $X\sim\nu$ be a
prompt, let $Q(\cdot\mid X)$ be the rollout policy, and let
$R(X,Y)$ be the terminal verifier reward of a complete response.  During the
optimization phase, the policy of interest is $P_\theta$, with population
reward $J(\theta):=\E_{P_\theta}[R]$.  Assuming $P_\theta\ll Q$, define the
sequence likelihood ratio
\begin{equation}
  W_\theta(X,Y)
  :=\frac{P_\theta(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)},
  \qquad
  \E_Q[W_\theta\mid X]=1.
  \label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}

The importance-weighted population objective is not a surrogate for $J$; it is
exactly $J$:
\begin{equation}
  L_Q(\theta):=\E_Q[W_\theta R]
  =\E_{P_\theta}[R]
  =J(\theta).
  \label{eq:population-objective-identity}
\end{equation}
The only gap studied below is therefore the statistical gap between this
population objective and its finite-sample estimate.  In particular, assuming
$0\le R\le R_{\max}$, improvement relative to the rollout policy satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \Delta(\theta;Q)
  :=J(\theta)-J(Q)
  =\E_Q[(W_\theta-1)R].
  \label{eq:population-improvement}
\end{equation}
For an evaluation sample $\mathcal E$ of $n$ responses from $Q$, define
\begin{equation}
  \widehat L_{\mathcal E}(\theta)
  :=\frac1n\sum_{Z_i\in\mathcal E}W_{\theta,i}R_i.
  \label{eq:empirical-reward-objective}
\end{equation}
Its centered improvement estimator is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\Delta_{\mathcal E}(\theta;Q)
  :=\frac1n\sum_{Z_i\in\mathcal E}(W_{\theta,i}-1)R_i.
  \label{eq:empirical-improvement}
\end{equation}
Centering at $Q$ is essential for the reliability statement: the estimator and
its uncertainty both vanish when $P_\theta=Q$.  Equations
\eqref{eq:population-objective-identity}--\eqref{eq:empirical-improvement}
establish the population--sample relationship used throughout the theory.

The policy gradient is obtained by differentiating the corresponding finite
objective.  Let $b(X)$ be a detached prompt-only baseline, set $A=R-b(X)$, and
define the current-policy score and gradient contribution by
\begin{equation}
  S_\theta:=\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y\mid X),
  \qquad
  H_\theta:=A S_\theta.
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
Assuming that differentiation and expectation can be interchanged, the target
gradient is
\begin{align}
  g(\theta):=\nabla J(\theta)
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[R S_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-1}\\
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[(R-b(X))S_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-2}\\
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[H_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-3}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta].
  \label{eq:true-gradient}
\end{align}
The first line is the score-function identity.  The second uses
$\E_{P_\theta}[b(X)S_\theta\mid X]
=b(X)\nabla_\theta\sum_yP_\theta(y\mid X)=0$.  The third substitutes
\eqref{eq:score}, and the fourth changes measure from $P_\theta$ to $Q$.
Given $N$ independent prompt-response pairs from the rollout distribution, the
permissive importance-weighted estimator is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_W(\theta)
  :=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N W_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}.
  \label{eq:raw-estimator}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:true-gradient} identifies the desired update, whereas
Equation~\eqref{eq:raw-estimator} describes what the fixed batch can estimate.
This derivative explains how an empirical optimizer proposes a new policy.  It
is not the primary reliability theorem: the bounded-reward objective in
Equation~\eqref{eq:empirical-improvement} admits an ESS guarantee without any
bound on the score or gradient contribution.

\subsection{Effective support of the rollout distribution}

Sequence ESS summarizes how much support the rollout distribution provides for
the current policy.  Assuming $\E_Q[W_\theta^2]<\infty$, define the normalized
population ESS by
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\theta:=\frac{1}{\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
  =\exp\{-D_2(P_\theta\|Q)\}.
  \label{eq:population-ess}
\end{equation}
Here $D_2(P_\theta\|Q)$ is the order-2 R\'enyi divergence between complete
prompt-response distributions, so the identity is sequence-level.  Because
$\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$, its relation to ratio dispersion is exact:
\begin{align}
  \E_Q[(W_\theta-1)^2]
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta^2]-2\E_Q[W_\theta]+1
    \label{eq:weight-variance-proof-1}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta^2]-1
    \label{eq:weight-variance-proof-2}\\
  &=\rho_\theta^{-1}-1.
    \label{eq:weight-variance}
\end{align}
The first line expands the square, the second uses $\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$, and the
third uses Equation~\eqref{eq:population-ess}.  Hence $N\rho_\theta$ is the
population effective sequence count, consistent with the second-moment
coverage principle used in importance-sampling policy optimization
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  When $\rho_\theta$ is near one, the batch
retains nearly all of its nominal support.  When it approaches zero, a batch of
$N$ responses behaves as if it contained far fewer effective observations.

ESS measures ratio concentration, not the size or direction of gradient
contributions attached to those ratios.  Its direct implication below is
therefore an objective-estimation guarantee based on bounded reward.  Gradient
MSE is retained only as a separate empirical diagnostic.

\section{Theoretical analysis}
\label{sec:theory}

The theory follows one objective through population evaluation, finite-sample
estimation, and local optimization.  Equation
\eqref{eq:population-objective-identity} gives the starting point:
$L_Q(\theta)=J(\theta)$ for raw full-sequence importance sampling.  Thus there is
no population surrogate gap to bridge.  The two genuine questions are whether a
finite rollout minibatch estimates this objective reliably and whether the
optimizer's realized step raises the sampled objective selected at the current
policy.  Current sequence ESS answers the first question.  A smoothness bound
answers the second without assuming a bounded score or gradient norm.

\subsection{Current policy improvement from an unused minibatch}
\label{sec:raw}

Partition the rollout batch independently of the sampled response values as
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal D=\mathcal B_1\mathbin{\dot\cup}\cdots
  \mathbin{\dot\cup}\mathcal B_K,
  \qquad |\mathcal B_k|=m.
  \label{eq:disjoint-minibatches}
\end{equation}
Let $\mathcal F_{k-1}$ contain the rollout policy, optimizer state, partition,
and the minibatches processed before update $k$.  The current parameter
$\theta_k$ depends only on $\mathcal F_{k-1}$, so the unused minibatch
$\mathcal B_k$ remains a fresh sample from $Q$ for evaluating
$P_{\theta_k}$.  For grouped RLVR, prompt groups are kept intact and the group
is the independent unit; $m$ then denotes the number of groups and each summand
below is the within-group average.

Define the current full-sequence ratio and population ESS by
\begin{equation}
  W_k:=\frac{P_{\theta_k}(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)},
  \qquad
  \rho_k:=\frac{1}{\E_Q[W_k^2\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]}
  =\exp\{-D_2(P_{\theta_k}\|Q)\}.
  \label{eq:current-ess}
\end{equation}
For any policy $P_\theta$ evaluated on $\mathcal B_k$, define
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\Delta_k(\theta)
  :=\frac1m\sum_{Z_i\in\mathcal B_k}(W_{\theta,i}-1)R_i.
  \label{eq:minibatch-improvement-function}
\end{equation}
The current policy is independent of this unused minibatch, so write
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_k:=J(\theta_k)-J(Q),
  \qquad
  \widehat\Delta_k:=\widehat\Delta_k(\theta_k).
  \label{eq:current-improvement-estimator}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Current-ESS population-improvement certificate]
\label{thm:current-ess}
Assume $0\le R\le R_{\max}$ and $\E_Q[W_k^2\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]<\infty$.
Under Equation~\eqref{eq:disjoint-minibatches},
\begin{align}
  \E[\widehat\Delta_k\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]&=\Delta_k,
  \label{eq:current-improvement-unbiased}\\
  \Var(\widehat\Delta_k\mid\mathcal F_{k-1})
  &\le\frac{R_{\max}^2}{m}(\rho_k^{-1}-1).
  \label{eq:current-improvement-variance}
\end{align}
Consequently, for $0<\delta<1$, with conditional probability at least
$1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_k
  \ge\widehat\Delta_k
  -R_{\max}\sqrt{
    \frac{1-\delta}{\delta m}(\rho_k^{-1}-1)
  }.
  \label{eq:current-ess-certificate}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Define the current-ESS confidence penalty
\begin{equation}
  \mathfrak r_{\delta,m}(\rho)
  :=R_{\max}\sqrt{
    \frac{1-\delta}{\delta m}(\rho^{-1}-1)
  }.
  \label{eq:ess-confidence-penalty}
\end{equation}
Theorem~\ref{thm:current-ess} states that
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta_k)
  \ge
  J(Q)+\widehat\Delta_k-\mathfrak r_{\delta,m}(\rho_k)
  \label{eq:current-reward-lcb}
\end{equation}
with conditional probability at least $1-\delta$.  The right-hand side is a
lower confidence bound on the population reward of the current learner.  It is
exact at $P_{\theta_k}=Q$ and becomes less informative as
$\rho_k^{-1}-1=\chi^2(P_{\theta_k}\|Q)$ grows.  The dependence is entirely on
bounded reward and the second moment of the sequence ratio, not on a bound for
$\|\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta\|$ or $\|\nabla J(\theta)\|$.

\subsection{From the current certificate to the realized optimizer step}
\label{sec:mse-improvement}

The current-policy lower bounds determine whether the raw estimator or
full-sequence truncation provides the stronger reward certificate at update
$k$.  Let $e_k\in\{W,c\}$ denote the estimator selected by this current
comparison.  Its sampled reward objective is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat L_k(\theta)
  :=\frac1m\sum_{Z_i\in\mathcal B_k}
  \phi_{e_k}(W_{\theta,i})R_i,
  \qquad
  \phi_W(w)=w,
  \quad
  \phi_c(w)=\min\{w,c\}.
  \label{eq:selected-sampled-objective}
\end{equation}
To analyze the finite step actually taken, write
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_k:=\nabla\widehat L_k(\theta_k),
  \qquad d_k:=\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k.
  \label{eq:minibatch-objective}
\end{equation}

The current confidence radius has completed its role once the estimator is
selected at $\theta_k$; it is not a second quantity for the optimizer to predict
or differentiate.  The remaining optimization question is whether the realized
displacement ascends $\widehat L_k$.

\begin{lemma}[Smooth sampled-objective ascent]
\label{lem:smooth-empirical-ascent}
If $\widehat L_k$ is $L_k$-smooth on the segment from $\theta_k$ to
$\theta_{k+1}$, then
\begin{equation}
  \widehat L_k(\theta_{k+1})-\widehat L_k(\theta_k)
  \ge
  \langle\widehat g_k,d_k\rangle
  -\frac{L_k}{2}\|d_k\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:general-smoothness-gain}
\end{equation}
For the plain-gradient update $d_k=\eta\widehat g_k$,
\begin{equation}
  \widehat L_k(\theta_{k+1})-\widehat L_k(\theta_k)
  \ge
  \eta\left(1-\frac{L_k\eta}{2}\right)
  \|\widehat g_k\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:empirical-smoothness-gain}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

Lemma~\ref{lem:smooth-empirical-ascent} is the standard quadratic lower bound
for a smooth objective, used here as a local replacement for an unavailable
global argmax argument.  This use has direct precedent in policy optimization.
\citet[Lemma~3.2 and Theorem~3.3]{pirotta2013adaptive} apply a Taylor lower bound
along a policy-gradient step to obtain a performance-improvement bound.
\citet[Proposition~3, Lemma~6, and Theorem~7]{papini2022smoothing} state the
quadratic smoothness bound, establish smoothness of policy performance for their
policy class, and derive the corresponding result for an arbitrary parameter
update.  In our setting, the lemma certifies the sampled objective actually used
to construct the update.

This is the optimization conclusion that replaces a global argmax.  Any
realized displacement satisfying
\begin{equation}
  \langle\widehat g_k,d_k\rangle
  >\frac{L_k}{2}\|d_k\|_2^2
  \label{eq:realized-ascent-condition}
\end{equation}
strictly raises the selected sampled objective.  For a plain-gradient step,
$0<\eta<2/L_k$ is sufficient; Equation~\eqref{eq:general-smoothness-gain} also
applies directly to the displacement produced by an adaptive optimizer.

Theorem~\ref{thm:current-ess} and Lemma~\ref{lem:smooth-empirical-ascent} have
separate roles at the same update.  The theorem measures how reliably the
current rollout estimates population reward and therefore which current
estimator has the stronger lower bound.  The lemma verifies that the optimizer
actually ascends the sampled objective selected by that comparison.  The full
theoretical chain is consequently
\[
  \text{current-policy ESS reliability}
  \ \longrightarrow\
  \text{current estimator selection}
  \ \longrightarrow\
  \text{smooth realized ascent}.
\]
The next subsection makes the estimator-selection step explicit.

\subsection{Three interventions with different mathematical objects}
\label{sec:clipping}

The confidence analysis is estimator-specific.  If an estimator has population
target $L_e(\theta)$, empirical value $\widehat L_e(\theta)$, concentration
radius $r_e(\theta)$, and a one-sided mismatch bound
$\beta_e(\theta)\ge[L_e(\theta)-J(\theta)]_+$, where
$[u]_+:=\max\{u,0\}$, then its generic reward lower
bound has the form
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta)
  \ge \widehat L_e(\theta)-r_e(\theta)-\beta_e(\theta).
  \label{eq:estimator-specific-lcb}
\end{equation}
Raw full-sequence importance sampling has zero mismatch by
Equation~\eqref{eq:population-objective-identity} and pays the ESS radius.
Full-sequence truncation lies below $J$, so its one-sided penalty
$\beta_e$ is also zero, and it pays a uniform bounded-variable radius.
PPO/GRPO and CISPO alter token-gradient contributions;
without a separate bound on their population surrogate mismatch, neither can be
inserted into Equation~\eqref{eq:current-reward-lcb}.

\subsubsection{Full-sequence truncation gives a conservative reward bound}
\label{sec:ratio-truncation}

For $c\ge1$, define
\begin{equation}
  U_c(W):=\min\{W,c\},
  \qquad
  \widehat\Delta_{c,k}
  :=\frac1m\sum_{Z_i\in\mathcal B_k}(U_c(W_{k,i})-1)R_i.
  \label{eq:upper-truncation}
\end{equation}
Because $R\ge0$, truncation produces a population lower bound rather than an
uncontrolled surrogate:
\begin{equation}
  \E_Q[(U_c(W_k)-1)R\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]-\Delta_k
  =-\E_Q[(W_k-c)_+R\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]\le0.
  \label{eq:cap-bias}
\end{equation}

\begin{corollary}[Full-sequence truncation certificate]
\label{cor:upper-truncation}
Under Theorem~\ref{thm:current-ess}, with conditional probability at least
$1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_k
  \ge\widehat\Delta_{c,k}
  -cR_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{2m}}.
  \label{eq:cap-objective-certificate}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

The raw and truncated certificates now give a precise objective-level answer to
when truncation helps.  Allocate failure probability $\delta/2$ to each bound
and define their empirical lower bounds, excluding the common $J(Q)$ term, by
\begin{align}
  \mathsf B_{W,k}
  &:=\widehat\Delta_k-\mathfrak r_{\delta/2,m}(\rho_k),
  \label{eq:raw-empirical-lcb}\\
  \mathsf B_{c,k}
  &:=\widehat\Delta_{c,k}
  -cR_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2m}}.
  \label{eq:truncated-empirical-lcb}
\end{align}
At a common simultaneous confidence level, truncation gives the stronger
population-reward floor exactly when
\begin{align}
  &\mathfrak r_{\delta/2,m}(\rho_k)
  -cR_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2m}}
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad>
  \widehat\Delta_k-\widehat\Delta_{c,k}
  =\frac1m\sum_{Z_i\in\mathcal B_k}(W_{k,i}-c)_+R_i.
  \label{eq:objective-crossover}
\end{align}
The left-hand side is the reduction in uncertainty; the right-hand side is the
sampled reward signal discarded by truncation.  High ESS favors the raw
objective because its uncertainty is already small.  Low ESS enlarges the raw
radius until the bounded conservative estimator can provide the stronger lower
bound.  This device appears in ACER with bias correction
\citep{wang2017acer}, in the V-trace correction used by IMPALA
\citep{espeholt2018impala}, and in recent LLM methods that control extreme weight
moments \citep{zheng2025m2po,fakoor2026p3o}.

\subsubsection{PPO clipping as an advantage-sign gradient mask}

For a detached advantage $A$ and token ratio
$r_t(\theta)=P_\theta(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t})/
Q(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t})$, the token-averaged PPO/GRPO surrogate is
\begin{equation}
  \ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
  :=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\min\left\{
    r_tA,\operatorname{clip}(r_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:ppo-objective}
\end{equation}
Clipping this scalar surrogate does not simply cap each gradient coefficient.
Its derivative instead masks token-gradient contributions according to
advantage sign.

\begin{proposition}[PPO clipping is advantage-sign gradient masking]
\label{prop:ppo-mask}
Away from the two clipping boundaries, define
\begin{equation}
  M_{t,\epsilon}
  :=\mathbf{1}\{A\ge0, r_t\le1+\epsilon\}
   +\mathbf{1}\{A<0, r_t\ge1-\epsilon\}.
  \label{eq:ppo-mask}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_\theta\ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
  =\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T
  M_{t,\epsilon}r_tA\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t}).
  \label{eq:ppo-gradient}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

PPO/GRPO therefore removes an upper-ratio token gradient only for nonnegative
advantages and a lower-ratio token gradient only for negative advantages.  In
particular, a large ratio with negative advantage remains active.  This is a
directional gradient mask, not a global bound on importance coefficients.

\subsubsection{CISPO coefficient capping retains token gradients}

CISPO applies a different operation.  Let $c_{\mathrm C}(r_t)$ denote its
capped token coefficient and $\operatorname{sg}$ stop differentiation through
that coefficient.  Its token-gradient estimator has the form
\begin{equation}
  G^{\mathrm{CISPO}}(Z)
  =\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T
  \operatorname{sg}\!\{c_{\mathrm C}(r_t)\}
  A\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t}).
  \label{eq:cispo-gradient}
\end{equation}
The cap limits the magnitude of a detached token coefficient, but it does not
zero the corresponding score.  CISPO therefore retains token-gradient support
beyond the PPO boundary \citep{minimax2025m1}.  This coefficient capping is
neither PPO/GRPO masking nor truncation of the full response ratio
$W_\theta=\prod_t r_t$.

The distinction is consequential for the theory.  PPO can set a token-gradient
contribution to zero while leaving a large negative-advantage ratio active.
CISPO bounds a detached token coefficient but retains the token score.
Full-sequence truncation instead changes the scalar response-level estimator
whose expectation is compared directly with $J$.  Only the last operation has
the one-sided reward bias in Equation~\eqref{eq:cap-bias}; the first two require
an additional surrogate-mismatch analysis before they receive an objective-level
certificate.

Gradient MSE remains a useful diagnostic, but it is not a policy-improvement
criterion.  To record that diagnostic without conflating the interventions, let
$X_W:=W_\theta H_\theta$ be the permissive per-response contribution and let
$X_C$ be any modified per-response contribution with a finite second moment.
Define
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_C:=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N X_{C,i},
  \qquad b_C:=\E_Q[X_C-X_W],
  \label{eq:modified-estimator}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma_W:=\Cov_Q(X_W),
  \qquad \Sigma_C:=\Cov_Q(X_C).
  \label{eq:bias-covariance}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Gradient-MSE decomposition, diagnostic only]
\label{thm:crossover}
For fixed $\theta$ and finite second moments,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  =\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  =\|b_C\|_2^2+\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C).
  \label{eq:two-risks}
\end{equation}
Therefore the modified contribution has lower gradient MSE exactly when
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  <\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  \iff
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C)
  >N\|b_C\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

This decomposition does not say which update improves reward.  For example, a
zero estimator can have lower MSE than a high-variance unbiased estimator while
producing no learning.  The contextual-bandit MSE experiments therefore test a
supporting statistical mechanism, not a sufficient decision rule.  The
objective crossover in Equation~\eqref{eq:objective-crossover}, rather than the
gradient-MSE crossover alone, is the theoretical comparison relevant to a
population-reward lower bound.

\subsection{An observable test of the theory}
\label{sec:practice}

Population ESS is unavailable during training, but its plug-in estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho
  :=\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^N W_i)^2}{N\sum_{i=1}^N W_i^2}.
  \label{eq:sample-ess}
\end{equation}
The statistic is computed from raw sequence ratios before any intervention.
It is a plug-in diagnostic, not the population $\rho_k$ in
Theorem~\ref{thm:current-ess}.  In a heavy-tailed regime, a finite minibatch may
miss rare high-ratio responses and overstate coverage.  We therefore use sample
ESS as an observable regime coordinate and test its decisions empirically,
rather than substituting it into the theorem as an exact confidence radius.

We use $\widehat\rho=0.1$ as a prespecified empirical trigger and test whether
it selects a regime in which conservative interventions improve observed
training outcomes.  No theorem identifies $0.1$ as a universal boundary or
proves that the same gate ranks PPO, CISPO, and sequence truncation.  For the
first two, the gate is motivated by the common coverage problem but remains an
empirical intervention because their estimator-specific mismatch is not bounded
by the sequence-ESS theorem.  Recent LLM RL methods likewise adapt
updates using ESS, weight moments, or sequence reshaping
\citep{zheng2025m2po,shen2026vespo,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}.

Section~\ref{sec:simulation} measures adjacent reliability diagnostics with an
exact population oracle and then evaluates the observable trigger in an
RLVR-style optimization loop.

\section{Contextual-bandit validation of the theory}
\label{sec:simulation}

This study probes the explanation developed in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.  It asks
whether ESS predicts finite-batch error, whether that error is associated with
policy improvement, and whether masked and permissive gradient estimators
exchange their MSE ordering as effective support deteriorates.  The first two
questions are adjacent diagnostics for the objective-level mechanism; the third
is explicitly a supporting diagnostic rather than a sufficient rule for
choosing an update.  The reported study does not measure empirical coverage of
Equation~\eqref{eq:current-ess-certificate} or directly evaluate the
confidence-bound crossover in Equation~\eqref{eq:objective-crossover}.

\subsection{Testbed and oracle}

We use the standard reduction from classification to a contextual bandit on Optdigits
\citep{wang2017adaptive,alpaydin1998optdigits}.  Each handwritten-digit image is
a context, the policy emits a 16-token binary encoding of the digit, and an
exact-match verifier supplies reward.  Because the dataset is a finite
population, we can enumerate the exact reward and policy gradient.  The
sequence action still produces the product importance ratios that drive ESS in
RLVR, so the testbed removes language-model complexity without removing the
statistical mechanism of interest.

The optimization loop follows grouped RLVR
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath,yu2025dapo}.  Each rollout samples 128 prompts and
16 responses per prompt, computes leave-one-out advantages, and supplies four
epochs of 16 sequential minibatch updates.  Ratios and ESS are recomputed before
every update, and a new rollout is collected only afterward.  This repeated-epoch
trajectory is a stress test beyond the one-epoch freshness condition in
Theorem~\ref{thm:current-ess}; only the first traversal has the required disjoint
freshness, and the frozen-policy redraws provide an independent estimator
diagnostic.  We run 100 paired replications; Appendix~\ref{app:bandit}
gives the compact protocol and full result tables.

\subsection{The predicted regime change appears}

ESS first predicts the reliability of the permissive gradient.  Its MSE rises
from $1.6\times10^{-2}$ above ESS $0.8$ to $1.31\times10^{-1}$ between $0.03$
and $0.1$, and to $4.77\times10^{-1}$ below $0.03$.  In the same two low-ESS
regions, permissive steps reduce population reward by $4.0\%$ and $13.3\%$,
while matched oracle-gradient steps improve reward.  ESS therefore identifies
estimation failure rather than the absence of an improving direction.

Frozen-policy redraws then measure the gradient-MSE decomposition in
Theorem~\ref{thm:crossover}.  With the fixed $N=512$ diagnostic, the
sequence-level mask has
lower MSE below population ESS $0.4$, while the permissive estimator has lower
MSE above it.  A disjoint calibration split places the crossover at $0.42$ and
reduces held-out MSE by $4.3\%\pm1.1\%$ relative to the better static estimator.
Thus ESS predicts which estimator has lower gradient error under this fixed
construction; it does not establish which update has greater population gain.

Finally, we evaluate the deliberately conservative rule
$\widehat\rho<0.1$.  It selects only $3.3\%$ of updates; within that event, PPO
has lower MSE on $83.7\%\pm1.4\%$ of updates and reduces
conditional MSE by $53.3\%\pm2.9\%$.  Across eight rollout cycles, each with four
optimization epochs over its fixed batch, the resulting trajectory reaches
$0.4940\pm0.0050$ final reward, compared with
$0.4606\pm0.0098$ for always permissive and $0.4038\pm0.0005$ for always
masked.  The sparse trigger therefore captures the low-ESS side of the
  empirical diagnostic region with an observable training statistic.

The controlled study therefore supports the empirical claim that ESS tracks a
reliability transition relevant to the sequence-level masking analogue.  It
does not by itself establish the objective certificate or transfer the result
to token-local PPO, CISPO, or full-sequence truncation.  The next section asks
whether a related operational transition is visible in language-model RLVR,
where the population gradient cannot be enumerated.

\section{Language-model evidence for the regime change}
\label{sec:llm-experiments}

The contextual-bandit study provides controlled evidence for adjacent
diagnostics under an exact oracle, but its repeated-epoch trajectory is outside
the one-epoch theorem and does not directly test the confidence certificates.
This section tests its operational implication in language-model training,
where neither the population gradient nor its MSE is observable.  If ESS marks
the predicted reliability transition, a conservative intervention should retain
permissive updates while ESS is high and prevent their late failure after ESS
falls.  The experiment asks whether this reliability transition remains visible
at language-model scale.

\subsection{A minimal intervention}

At every policy update, we compute sequence ESS from the raw ratios before any
masking or truncation.  All methods use the same valid sequences and minibatch
construction.  We then evaluate $0.1$ as a prespecified heuristic:
when $\widehat\rho\ge0.1$, the method retains the permissive update; when
$\widehat\rho<0.1$, it activates the safeguard associated with the comparison.
We test conventional GRPO clipping, Clip-Higher \citep{yu2025dapo}, the
divergence constraint of DPPO \citep{qi2026dppo}.  We also pair conventional
clipping with a permissive TIS update whose ratio cap is 3.

\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{LLM}}
  =\begin{cases}
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{permissive}},&\widehat\rho\ge0.1,\\
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{safeguard}},&\widehat\rho<0.1.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:llm-gate}
\end{equation}
Each conditional run is compared with its always-permissive and
always-protected counterparts.  This pairing tests the regime prediction while
holding the safeguard itself fixed.  The threshold is set before the reported
runs and is shared across update rules, providing one observable approximation
to the low-support regime.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

We optimize Qwen3-30B-A3B for 200 training steps and evaluate on AIME-2024 with
mean@16.  The primary comparison is the complete evaluation trajectory and its
step-200 endpoint.  We also align normalized sequence ESS and the fraction of
clipped updates to the training axis to verify that the rule responds to a loss
of effective support.  The supplied artifacts report one trajectory per
configuration, so we report endpoint differences without uncertainty and treat
them as trajectory-level evidence.

Figure~\ref{fig:llm-main} gives a consistent trajectory-level result.  The

\section{Conclusion}

The role of conservative intervention changes as fixed rollout data cease to
represent the current learner.  The theory follows one reward objective across
that change.  Raw full-sequence importance sampling satisfies
$L_Q(\theta)=J(\theta)$ exactly, while current sequence ESS controls the gap
between its unused-minibatch estimate and population improvement over the
rollout policy.  Smoothness then certifies the sampled gain of the optimizer's
realized displacement after the current-policy certificate selects the
estimator regime.  Thus current ESS supplies the population-reliability
criterion, while smoothness supplies the local optimization conclusion.  This
argument needs neither a bounded score norm nor a global argmax assumption.

The intervention analysis also separates three objects that are often called
clipping.  PPO/GRPO masks selected token gradients, CISPO caps detached token
coefficients while retaining their scores, and full-sequence truncation changes
the scalar importance estimator.  Only full-sequence truncation directly
inherits the bounded conservative objective certificate.  Its comparison with
the raw estimator is explicit: truncation is preferable when the reduction in
confidence radius exceeds the sampled reward signal it discards.  The
contextual-bandit and language-model trajectories show that sample ESS is an
informative operational diagnostic, but they do not turn the plug-in threshold
into a universal guarantee.  Direct tests should next measure empirical
coverage of the current-policy bound and the raw-versus-truncated confidence
crossover on a strictly disjoint one-epoch traversal.

the RLVR feedback structure while exposing the quantities needed to test the
theory.

\subsection{Finite population and initial policy}

Optdigits is a handwritten-digit classification benchmark with 5,620 labeled
examples
\citep{alpaydin1998optdigits}.  Each original $32\times32$ binary image is
partitioned into $4\times4$ blocks; foreground counts produce 64 integer
features in $\{0,\ldots,16\}$.  We concatenate the supplied 3,823 training and
1,797 test examples, divide each feature by 16, and append an intercept.  This
gives a finite population $\{(x_j,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{M}$ with $M=5{,}620$ and
$x_j\in\mathbb{R}^{65}$.  The original split is not used because the estimand
is finite-population policy optimization, not classification generalization.

Each label $y\in\{0,\ldots,9\}$ has a four-bit binary representation.  Let
$c(y)\in\{0,1\}^{16}$ repeat this code four times.  The repetition changes only
the response length, not which answer is correct, and creates a sequence ratio
that compounds over 16 token probabilities.  The policy has one Bernoulli head
per token position,
\begin{align}
  p_{\theta,\ell}(x)&=\sigma(\theta_\ell^\top x),\nonumber\\
  P_\theta(a_{1:16}\mid x)
  &=\prod_{\ell=1}^{16}
    p_{\theta,\ell}(x)^{a_\ell}
    \{1-p_{\theta,\ell}(x)\}^{1-a_\ell},
  \qquad \theta\in\mathbb{R}^{16\times65}.
  \label{eq:optdigits-policy}
\end{align}
The verifier reward is
$R(x_j,a_{1:16})=\mathbf{1}\{a_{1:16}=c(y_j)\}$.

Initialization is deterministic and uses no experimental outcomes.  Starting
from zero, we fit all token heads for 500 full-population logistic-regression
steps with step size $0.8$ and no regularization.  We then multiply the fitted
weights by $0.55$ to keep the initial policy stochastic, which gives exact
initial reward $V(\theta_0)=0.095766$.  This procedure uses no data split,
minibatching, early stopping, or model selection.

For compactness, define
\begin{equation}
  q_j(\theta)
  :=P_\theta(c(y_j)\mid x_j)
  =\prod_{\ell=1}^{16}
  \left[c_{j\ell}p_{j\ell}
  +(1-c_{j\ell})(1-p_{j\ell})\right],
  \label{eq:correct-sequence-probability}
\end{equation}
where $c_{j\ell}=c(y_j)_\ell$ and
$p_{j\ell}=p_{\theta,\ell}(x_j)$.  The finite-population objective is
\begin{equation}
  V(\theta)=\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^{M}q_j(\theta).
  \label{eq:optdigits-value}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Exact gradient and group-relative advantage}

The Bernoulli score identity gives the exact finite-population gradient
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_{\theta_\ell}V(\theta)
  =\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^{M}
    q_j(\theta)\{c_{j\ell}-p_{j\ell}\}x_j.
  \label{eq:optdigits-gradient}
\end{equation}
where $q_j(\theta)$ is the correct-sequence probability,
$c_{j\ell}$ is its bit at position $\ell$, and $p_{j\ell}$ is the policy
probability of bit one.  The oracle enumerates all 5,620 contexts at the current
pre-update policy.  We measure a sampled estimator by
\begin{equation}
  E_t=\|\widehat g_t-\nabla V(\theta_t)\|_F^2,
  \label{eq:optdigits-gradient-error}
\end{equation}
and report its mean within each fixed ESS interval.

For response $g$ to prompt $i$, we use the leave-one-out advantage
\begin{equation}
  A_{ig}=R_{ig}-\frac1{G-1}\sum_{h\ne g}R_{ih}
  =\frac{G}{G-1}(R_{ig}-\bar R_i).
  \label{eq:loo-group-advantage}
\end{equation}
It has the sign of the group-centered GRPO advantage
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}; standard-deviation scaling is omitted so every
minibatch targets the same population gradient.  The baseline excludes the
focal response and is therefore independent of it conditional on the prompt.
Importance weighting and the zero-mean score identity then preserve the target
in Equation~\eqref{eq:optdigits-gradient}.

\subsection{Rollout and minibatch procedure}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{RLVR-style OptDigits policy optimization}
\label{alg:bandit-loop}
\centering
\fbox{\begin{minipage}{0.92\linewidth}
\small
\textbf{Input:} initial policy $\theta_0$; eight rollouts; 128 prompts per
rollout; $G=16$ responses per prompt; 16 minibatches; four optimization epochs.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Set the rollout policy $Q\leftarrow P_\theta$.  Sample prompts and
  grouped responses, then compute verifier rewards and leave-one-out advantages.
  \item Randomly partition the intact prompt groups into 16 minibatches, each
  containing eight groups and $N=128$ complete responses.
  \item In each of four optimization epochs, traverse all minibatches.  Before
  every update, recompute $W=P_\theta(Y\mid x)/Q(Y\mid x)$, policy scores, and
  sample ESS.  Form the designated raw, PPO-masked, or conditional estimator.
  \item After all 64 updates, discard the rollout and repeat from the new policy.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}}
\end{algorithm}

Every response enters one update per optimization epoch.  Within a rollout,
responses, rewards, advantages, and rollout probabilities remain fixed, whereas
current probabilities, ratios, scores, and ESS are recomputed before each
update.  Theorem~\ref{thm:current-ess} applies to the first disjoint traversal; later
epochs deliberately test behavior after sample reuse.  Sixteen minibatches are
used because the first four never reach ESS below $0.1$ in the diagnostic runs;
a shorter traversal would not expose the regime under study.

\subsection{Estimators and diagnostics}

Let $S_{ig,t}=\nabla_\theta\log
P_{\theta_{r,t-1}}(Y_{ig}\mid x_i)$.  For each $N=128$ minibatch, the
permissive and PPO-masked gradients are
\begin{align}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{raw},t}
  &=\frac1N\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t}A_{ig}S_{ig,t},
    \label{eq:simulation-raw-gradient}\\
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{PPO},t}
  &=\frac1N\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t}M_{ig,t}^{\mathrm{seq}}A_{ig}S_{ig,t},
    \label{eq:simulation-ppo-gradient}
\end{align}
where the experiment uses the response-level analogue
\begin{equation}
  M_{ig,t}^{\mathrm{seq}}
  =\mathbf{1}\{A_{ig}\ge0,W_{ig,t}\le1+\epsilon\}
   +\mathbf{1}\{A_{ig}<0,W_{ig,t}\ge1-\epsilon\},
  \qquad \epsilon=0.2.
  \label{eq:simulation-sequence-mask}
\end{equation}
This construction preserves the advantage-sign masking mechanism of
Proposition~\ref{prop:ppo-mask}, but applies it once to the complete-response
ratio rather than separately to token ratios.  It is therefore a controlled
sequence-level masking analogue, not the exact token-local PPO/GRPO gradient and
not numerical ratio truncation.  The third method uses
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{cond},t}
  =\begin{cases}
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{raw},t},&\widehat\rho_t\ge0.1,\\
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{PPO},t},&\widehat\rho_t<0.1,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad
  \widehat\rho_t
  =\frac{(\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t})^2}
  {N\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t}^2}.
  \label{eq:simulation-gate}
\end{equation}
The $0.1$ threshold is fixed before the runs and is not selected from a reported
outcome.  All methods share the initialization, prompt draws, response-sampling
uniforms, and group permutations within each of 100 paired replications.

The first 20 conditional replications provide 2,560 pre-update diagnostics.  At
each state, we evaluate the raw gradient against
Equation~\eqref{eq:optdigits-gradient} and compare its counterfactual reward
step with the exact-gradient step.  Results use seven prespecified ESS intervals:
\[
  \begin{gathered}
    \relax[0,0.03),\ [0.03,0.1),\ [0.1,0.2),\ [0.2,0.4),\\
    [0.4,0.6),\ [0.6,0.8),\ [0.8,1].
  \end{gathered}
\]
Within-bin error bars are standard errors.  Optimization curves use all 100
replications and report relative population-reward improvement from the common
initial policy with 95\% confidence intervals.  One rollout costs 2,048
verifier responses.

\subsection{Fixed-checkpoint crossover diagnostic}

To estimate conditional MSE rather than one realized error, we freeze the
rollout and current policies at selected pre-update checkpoints and redraw
independent minibatches.  This diagnostic does not change training.

The finite Bernoulli policy also makes population sequence ESS exact.  For
prompt $x$ and token position $\ell$, let $q_{x\ell}$ and $p_{x\ell}$ denote
the rollout and current probabilities of token one.  Independence across the
16 response positions gives
\begin{align}
  \E_Q[W^2\mid x]
  &=\prod_{\ell=1}^{16}
    \left\{
      \frac{p_{x\ell}^2}{q_{x\ell}}
      +\frac{(1-p_{x\ell})^2}{1-q_{x\ell}}
    \right\},
    \label{eq:simulation-weight-second-moment}\\
  \rho_{\mathrm{pop}}
  &=\left\{
    \frac1{5620}\sum_{x=1}^{5620}\E_Q[W^2\mid x]
  \right\}^{-1}.
    \label{eq:simulation-population-ess}
\end{align}
These equations use $\E_Q[W]=1$ and contain no minibatch noise.  We evaluate
eight checkpoints from each of the seven ESS intervals, giving 56 frozen states
selected from the 2,560 trajectory checkpoints.  At each state, 32 paired
redraws compare raw and PPO-masked estimates.  One independent block contains
eight prompts and 16 responses per prompt, hence $N=128$ sequences; the
$N=512$ estimate averages four blocks.  The block is the independent unit
because responses within a prompt share an advantage.  For checkpoint $c$,
estimator $e$, and size $N$, we report
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{c,e,N}
  =\frac1{32}\sum_{s=1}^{32}
    \left\|
      \widehat g_{c,e,N}^{(s)}-\nabla V(\theta_c)
    \right\|_F^2.
  \label{eq:simulation-crossover-mse}
\end{equation}
We average redraws within a state and states within an ESS interval; standard
errors use the eight states as independent units.  PPO reduction is
$100(\widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\mathrm{raw}}-
\widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\mathrm{PPO}})/
\widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\mathrm{raw}}$.  Positive values favor masking.

\subsection{Formula calibration and held-out decision test}

We split the eight states in each ESS interval into four calibration and four
test states, yielding 28 of each.  At every state,
100 paired $N=128$ blocks estimate the raw covariance trace $\widehat V_W$, the
PPO covariance trace $\widehat V_C$, and the PPO squared bias $\widehat B_C$.
The squared-bias estimate subtracts the sampling variance of the estimated mean
and is truncated at zero.  Averaging $K\in\{1,2,4,8\}$ independent blocks gives
\begin{equation}
  \widehat R_W(K):=\frac{\widehat V_W}{K},
  \qquad
  \widehat R_C(K):=\widehat B_C+\frac{\widehat V_C}{K}.
  \label{eq:formula-risks}
\end{equation}
These are the two sides of the diagnostic decomposition in
Theorem~\ref{thm:crossover} at block level.  For each
$K$, we choose the ESS midpoint that minimizes fitted risk while applying PPO
below the threshold and raw above it.  The only interior calibration solution is
$K=4$, or $N=512$, with threshold $0.417$.

Held-out evaluation uses 100 paired redraws at each test state.
The fitted threshold never sees test-state errors; standard errors use the 28
states as independent units.  The subsequent optimization study freezes that
threshold, uses 512 sequences per update, traverses each rollout for four epochs,
and runs eight rollout cycles in 100 paired replications.  Sample ESS, rather
than population ESS, determines the training intervention.

The fixed-$0.1$ trajectory also records shadow raw and PPO gradients whenever
sample ESS is below $0.417$.  Comparing each with the exact gradient yields the
three intervals $[0,0.1)$, $[0.1,0.25)$, and $[0.25,0.417)$.  These shadow
calculations do not alter training.  Means and standard errors use the 100
replications as independent units.

\section{Proofs for the theoretical results}
\label{app:proofs}

This appendix gives every algebraic step omitted from the main theoretical
development.  The order follows Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:current-ess}}

Condition on $\mathcal F_{k-1}$.  The current parameter is fixed and the units
in $\mathcal B_k$ are independent draws from $Q$.  Change of measure gives
\begin{align}
  \E[\widehat\Delta_k\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]
  &=\E_Q[(W_k-1)R\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]\\
  &=J(\theta_k)-J(Q)\\
  &=\Delta_k.
\end{align}
For $Z_k=(W_k-1)R$, independence and $0\le R\le R_{\max}$ imply
\begin{align}
  \Var(\widehat\Delta_k\mid\mathcal F_{k-1})
  &=\frac1m\Var_Q(Z_k\mid\mathcal F_{k-1})\\
  &\le\frac1m\E_Q[Z_k^2\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]\\
  &\le\frac{R_{\max}^2}{m}
    \E_Q[(W_k-1)^2\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]\\
  &=\frac{R_{\max}^2}{m}(\rho_k^{-1}-1).
\end{align}
The final equality uses $\E_Q[W_k\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]=1$ and
Equation~\eqref{eq:current-ess}.  Conditional Cantelli's inequality states
\begin{equation}
  \Pr(\widehat\Delta_k-\Delta_k\ge a\mid\mathcal F_{k-1})
  \le\frac{\sigma_k^2}{\sigma_k^2+a^2},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_k^2$ is the conditional variance above.  Setting
$a=\sigma_k\sqrt{(1-\delta)/\delta}$ and substituting its upper bound proves
Equation~\eqref{eq:current-ess-certificate}.

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:smooth-empirical-ascent}}

The quadratic lower bound for an $L_k$-smooth function gives
\begin{align}
  \widehat L_k(\theta_k+d_k)-\widehat L_k(\theta_k)
  &\ge
  \left\langle\nabla\widehat L_k(\theta_k),d_k\right\rangle
  -\frac{L_k}{2}\|d_k\|_2^2\\
  &=
  \langle\widehat g_k,d_k\rangle
  -\frac{L_k}{2}\|d_k\|_2^2,
\end{align}
which proves Equation~\eqref{eq:general-smoothness-gain}.  Substituting
$d_k=\eta\widehat g_k$ gives
\begin{align}
  \langle\widehat g_k,\eta\widehat g_k\rangle
  -\frac{L_k}{2}\|\eta\widehat g_k\|_2^2
  &=
  \eta\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2
  -\frac{L_k\eta^2}{2}\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2\\
  &=
  \eta\left(1-\frac{L_k\eta}{2}\right)
  \|\widehat g_k\|_2^2,
\end{align}
which proves Equation~\eqref{eq:empirical-smoothness-gain}.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:ppo-mask}}

For $A\ge0$, the minimum selects $r_tA$ when $r_t\le1+\epsilon$ and
the constant $(1+\epsilon)A$ otherwise.  Its derivative is therefore
$r_tA\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t})$ in the first region and
zero in the second.  For $A<0$, the minimum selects the constant
$(1-\epsilon)A$ when $r_t<1-\epsilon$ and $r_tA$ otherwise.  Summing the two
cases over tokens gives Equation~\eqref{eq:ppo-gradient} away from the
boundaries.

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:crossover}}

The permissive estimator is unbiased, so independence gives
\begin{equation}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  =\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W).
\end{equation}
For the modified estimator, $\E[\widehat g_C]-g=b_C$.  Expanding around its
own mean eliminates the cross term:
\begin{align}
  \E\|\widehat g_C-g\|_2^2
  &=\|b_C\|_2^2
    +\E\|\widehat g_C-\E\widehat g_C\|_2^2\\
  &=\|b_C\|_2^2+\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C).
\end{align}
Subtracting the two risks proves Equation~\eqref{eq:crossover}.

\subsection{Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:upper-truncation}}

Let $Z_c=(U_c(W_k)-1)R$.  Since $0\le U_c(W_k)\le c$ and
$0\le R\le R_{\max}$, $Z_c$ lies in an interval of width at most
$cR_{\max}$.  Hoeffding's inequality therefore gives, with conditional
probability at least $1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}
  \E_Q[Z_c\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]
  \ge\widehat\Delta_{c,k}
  -cR_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{2m}}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cap-bias} gives
$\Delta_k\ge\E_Q[Z_c\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]$.  Combining the two inequalities
proves Equation~\eqref{eq:cap-objective-certificate}.
For data-dependent selection between the raw and truncated bounds, assign
failure probability $\delta/2$ to each.  Their simultaneous validity follows
from a union bound, so the larger lower bound remains valid with probability at
least $1-\delta$.
