\title{\bf When to Clip in RLVR:\\
Sequence ESS and the MSE Crossover of Policy-Gradient Estimators}
\author{Anonymous authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) repeatedly reuses a
batch while updating the learner, so the data become progressively stale and
full-sequence likelihood ratios can concentrate.  Clipping controls this
concentration, but it also removes gradient signal; consequently, applying it
throughout batch reuse can be as undesirable as never applying it.  We
formulate the decision of when to clip as a finite-sample comparison between
an unbiased importance-weighted policy-gradient estimator and a bounded,
biased alternative.

Starting from the exact terminal-reward policy-improvement identity, we derive
the raw sequence-gradient estimator and its exact mean-squared error (MSE).
Normalized sequence effective sample size (ESS) isolates the contribution of
likelihood-ratio concentration, while a separate weighted gradient scale
captures how large ratios align with large gradient contributions.  For a
bounded coefficient $U_\theta$, we show that clipping improves estimator MSE
if and only if its covariance reduction exceeds $N$ times its squared gradient
bias.  This exact crossover clarifies both why low ESS can justify clipping
and why ESS alone cannot determine the preferred estimator.

We connect the estimator comparison to optimization through a self-contained
nonconvex analysis: for ordinary stochastic gradient ascent on an
$L$-smooth objective, average gradient MSE is the additive statistical term in
a standard stationarity bound.  This result supports an ESS-gated rule that
retains the low-bias update while sequence coverage is adequate and activates
clipping only in the regime where variance reduction can pay for the induced
bias.  Finally, we give a falsifiable validation protocol based on direct
estimator resampling and matched-state intervention forks.  The protocol tests
whether a candidate ESS threshold, such as $0.1$, coincides with both the MSE
crossover and a reversal in subsequent policy improvement; the threshold is
not asserted to be universal.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

RLVR algorithms collect responses from a rollout policy and perform several
PPO-type gradient updates on the same batch.  Reuse is computationally
essential, but it makes the data progressively stale: after the first update,
the batch is still distributed according to the rollout policy $Q$, whereas
the learner is $P_\theta$.  The complete-response likelihood ratio
\begin{equation}
  W_\theta(X,Y)
  =\frac{P_\theta(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)}
  \label{eq:intro-sequence-weight}
\end{equation}
therefore becomes increasingly heterogeneous as inner-loop optimization
continues.  When a small number of responses carry most of the likelihood-ratio
mass, a large nominal batch can provide only a small effective number of
sequence-level observations.

The practical question is therefore not whether clipping can control stale
importance weights, but when its variance reduction is worth the gradient
signal it removes.  A natural working hypothesis is a single crossing: a
low-bias no-clip or CISPO-style update should be preferable while normalized
sequence ESS is healthy, whereas a conservative bounded update may become
preferable after sequence coverage deteriorates.  We use
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho_{\mathrm{seq}}
  =\frac{(\sum_i W_i)^2}{N\sum_iW_i^2}
  \le 0.1
  \label{eq:intro-threshold}
\end{equation}
as a candidate intervention rule, not as a universal constant.  The central
empirical question is whether this boundary coincides with a change in both
gradient-estimator MSE and subsequent policy improvement under matched states.

Two established theories explain different halves of this observation.
Importance-sampling policy optimization shows that off-policy reuse becomes
statistically unreliable as the order-two R\'enyi divergence between target
and behavior distributions grows.  POIS makes this dependence explicit: its
policy-performance bound contains
$d_2(P\|Q)/N$, equivalently inverse population ESS, and it optimizes a
pessimistic importance-weighted objective rather than trusting the raw
estimate indefinitely \cite{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  This explains why
sequence ESS is a principled diagnostic of stale-batch reliability, but it does
not answer when a clipped \emph{policy-gradient estimator} is preferable to an
unclipped one.

Biased stochastic-gradient theory supplies the second half.  Once clipping is
introduced, variance alone is no longer the relevant statistical quantity:
the estimator is biased, so its error relative to the population gradient is
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g)
  =\|\E\widehat g-g\|_2^2
  +\operatorname{tr}\Cov(\widehat g).
  \label{eq:intro-mse-decomposition}
\end{equation}
Driggs et al.\ develop a general biased-gradient framework and show in their
nonconvex analysis that convergence can depend on estimator bias only through
a bounded-MSE condition; their Theorem~20 and Remark~21 explicitly identify
large-bias, small-MSE estimators as potentially preferable to small-bias,
large-MSE estimators \cite{driggs2022biased}.  Tang gives an RL-specific
example in which a deliberately biased score-function estimator has much lower
variance and a better convergence dependence than the corresponding unbiased
estimator \cite{tang2022biased}.  These results motivate MSE as an
optimization-relevant risk, not merely a descriptive statistic.

The qualification is equally important.  Uncontrolled bias can create an
error floor or divergence in smooth SGD \cite{ajalloeian2020biased}; stochastic
gradient clipping can fail to converge to the true optimum under standard
noise assumptions \cite{koloskova2023clipping}; and policy-gradient dynamics
can differ even for estimators with the same mean and variance
\cite{chung2021baselines}.  We therefore do not use MSE as a universal ranking
of RL algorithms.  Our claim is narrower and testable: MSE is the correct
finite-sample error of a possibly biased gradient estimator, and under a
standard smooth plain-gradient model it enters the stationarity guarantee
directly.  The practical PPO/CISPO ranking must still be verified by
controlled interventions.

Our analysis begins from the exact terminal-reward policy-improvement identity
\begin{equation}
  J(P_\theta)-J(Q)
  =\E_Q[(W_\theta-1)(R-b(X))],
  \label{eq:intro-improvement-identity}
\end{equation}
where $b(X)$ is any prompt-only baseline treated as fixed when differentiating.
Differentiating its empirical version gives the raw full-sequence gradient
estimator
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_W(\theta)
  =\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N W_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i},
  \qquad
  H_\theta=(R-b(X))\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y\mid X).
  \label{eq:intro-gradient}
\end{equation}
For fixed $\theta$, this estimator is unbiased and has exact MSE
\begin{equation}
  m_W(\theta)
  =\frac1N\left\{
    \E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:intro-raw-mse}
\end{equation}
The exact error depends on both ratio concentration and the gradient
contributions associated with large ratios.  Sequence ESS isolates the former:
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\theta
  =\frac1{\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
  =e^{-D_2(P_\theta\|Q)}.
  \label{eq:intro-pop-ess}
\end{equation}
If the weighted gradient-contribution scale remains controlled, the raw MSE
grows as $1/(N\rho_\theta)$.

A conservative intervention replaces $W_\theta$ by a bounded coefficient
$U_\theta$.  The modified estimator has bias
\begin{equation}
  b_U(\theta)=\E_Q[(U_\theta-W_\theta)H_\theta]
  \label{eq:intro-bias}
\end{equation}
and covariance $\Sigma_U/N$.  Its MSE is lower than that of the raw estimator
if and only if
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U)
  >N\|b_U\|_2^2.}
  \label{eq:intro-crossover}
\end{equation}
This is the exact bias--variance crossover behind the gate.  High ESS limits
the ratio-driven component of raw MSE, so clipping has less variance to remove
while still changing the gradient.  Low ESS enlarges the raw second-moment
term, creating a regime in which a bounded estimator can pay for its bias.
ESS alone does not determine the sign of \eqref{eq:intro-crossover}; the
candidate threshold $0.1$ must therefore be validated for each studied model,
batch size, objective, and clipping rule.

Finally, we prove why this estimator comparison is relevant to an actual
first-order update.  For ordinary stochastic gradient ascent on an
$L$-smooth objective, with
$\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k+\eta\widehat g_k$ and $\eta\le 1/L$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac1T\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\E\|\nabla J(\theta_k)\|_2^2
  \le
  \frac{2\{J_{\max}-\E J(\theta_0)\}}{\eta T}
  +\frac1T\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}
  \E\|\widehat g_k-\nabla J(\theta_k)\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:intro-stationarity}
\end{equation}
Thus gradient MSE is exactly the additive statistical term in this standard
nonconvex stationarity bound.  The theorem does not analyze Adam exactly and
does not establish global trajectory dominance; it provides the precise
optimization bridge needed to interpret \eqref{eq:intro-crossover}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Exact sequence-gradient reliability.}
We derive the finite full-sequence policy-gradient estimator from the exact
RLVR policy-improvement identity and compute its MSE.  Sequence ESS factors out
the likelihood-ratio second moment, while the remaining term records the size
of the gradient contributions emphasized by those ratios.

\item \textbf{An exact clipping crossover.}
For any modified sequence coefficient, the clipped estimator is more accurate
than the raw estimator exactly when covariance reduction exceeds $N$ times
squared clipping bias.  For upper truncation, we derive an explicit removed-
tail bias and bounded-variance envelope, connecting the result to classical
truncated importance sampling \cite{ionides2008truncated}.

\item \textbf{A concrete optimization justification for gradient MSE.}
We prove a self-contained smooth nonconvex stationarity bound in which average
gradient MSE is the additive statistical term.  This connects the RLVR
estimator crossover to the biased-SGD mechanism of Driggs et al., while
stating the bias and policy-dynamics limitations identified in subsequent
optimization and policy-gradient theory.

\item \textbf{An ESS-gated rule with a falsifiable validation protocol.}
We turn the estimator crossover into a candidate two-mode controller and
specify matched-state experiments that directly measure raw MSE, clipping
bias, covariance reduction, and subsequent policy improvement around the
threshold.  These measurements distinguish a genuine estimator crossover
from a merely correlational ESS heuristic.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Batch reuse and exact sequence-level policy improvement}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{The RLVR inner loop}

Let $X\sim\nu$ be a prompt.  A rollout policy $Q$ generates a complete response
$Y=(Y_1,\ldots,Y_T)$ and a terminal verifier reward $R=R(X,Y)$.  During one
outer iteration, the resulting batch
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal D=\{(X_i,Y_i,R_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}
  \label{eq:batch}
\end{equation}
is reused for several policy-gradient updates.  The learner after an inner
update is denoted by $P_\theta$; the behavior distribution of the stored
responses remains $Q$.

Assume $P_\theta\ll Q$.  The exact full-response likelihood ratio is
\begin{equation}
  W_\theta(X,Y)
  :=\frac{P_\theta(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)}
  =\prod_{t=1}^{T}
  \frac{p_\theta(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t})}
       {q(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t})}.
  \label{eq:full-ratio}
\end{equation}
Because the prompt distribution is shared,
\begin{equation}
  \E_Q[W_\theta\mid X]=1,
  \qquad
  \E_Q[W_\theta]=1.
  \label{eq:mean-one}
\end{equation}
Assume throughout that the terminal reward is bounded,
\begin{equation}
  0\le R\le R_{\max}<\infty.
  \label{eq:bounded-reward}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Population improvement and its finite estimator}

Define
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta):=\E_{P_\theta}[R],
  \qquad
  \DeltaJ(\theta;Q):=J(\theta)-J(Q).
  \label{eq:performance}
\end{equation}
Let $b(X)$ be any prompt-only baseline that is treated as constant when
$\theta$ is differentiated, and write
\begin{equation}
  A(X,Y):=R(X,Y)-b(X).
  \label{eq:advantage}
\end{equation}
The baseline is introduced only to reduce variance; because it depends on the
prompt but not on the sampled response, it does not change the population
policy improvement.

\begin{lemma}[Exact centered policy-improvement identity]
\label{lem:paired}
For every $\theta$ with $P_\theta\ll Q$,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \DeltaJ(\theta;Q)
  =\E_Q[(W_\theta-1)A].}
  \label{eq:paired-identity}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Change of measure gives $\E_Q[W_\theta R]=\E_{P_\theta}[R]=J(\theta)$.
Also,
\begin{align}
  \E_Q[(W_\theta-1)b(X)]
  &=\E\!\left[b(X)
    \{\E_Q[W_\theta\mid X]-1\}\right]=0
\end{align}
by \eqref{eq:mean-one}.  Subtracting $\E_Q[R]=J(Q)$ proves the claim.
\end{proof}

The corresponding finite-batch estimator is
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \widehat\Delta_N(\theta;Q)
  :=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}
  (W_{\theta,i}-1)A_i.}
  \label{eq:finite-improvement}
\end{equation}
For a fixed $\theta$, it is unbiased for \eqref{eq:paired-identity}.  The
centering by one is useful: at the rollout policy, $P_\theta=Q$, every summand
is exactly zero.

\subsection{Sequence ESS as second-moment coverage}

Define the exponentiated order-two R\'enyi divergence and normalized population
sequence ESS by
\begin{align}
  d_2(P_\theta\|Q)
  &:=\E_Q[W_\theta^2]
    =\exp\{D_2(P_\theta\|Q)\},
  \label{eq:d2}\\
  \rho_\theta
  &:=\frac{(\E_QW_\theta)^2}{\E_QW_\theta^2}
    =\frac{1}{\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}.
  \label{eq:rho}
\end{align}
Because $\E_QW_\theta=1$,
\begin{equation}
  \E_Q[(W_\theta-1)^2]
  =d_2(P_\theta\|Q)-1
  =\rho_\theta^{-1}-1.
  \label{eq:chi-rho}
\end{equation}
Thus $\rho_\theta$ measures second-moment coverage of the learner by the
rollout policy.  It is directional: it asks whether samples from $Q$ can
represent expectations under $P_\theta$ without concentrating most likelihood-
ratio mass on a few responses.  This is the same second-moment quantity used
by POIS: its high-confidence importance-sampling bound scales with
$d_2(P_\theta\|Q)/N$, and its practical objective penalizes target policies
whose behavior-policy coverage is poor \cite{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.
Our next step is different: we differentiate the finite estimator and study
the error of the gradient supplied to the optimizer.

For completeness, the scalar policy-improvement estimator satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \Var(\widehat\Delta_N(\theta;Q))
  \le
  \frac{\|A\|_\infty^2}{N}
  (\rho_\theta^{-1}-1).
  \label{eq:scalar-ess-bound}
\end{equation}
The remainder of the paper develops the corresponding result for the gradient
that is actually used to update the policy.

\section{From the finite estimator to the policy-gradient estimator}
\label{sec:gradient-theory}

\subsection{Differentiating the empirical policy-improvement objective}

Define the full-response score
\begin{equation}
  S_\theta(X,Y)
  :=\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y\mid X)
  =\sum_{t=1}^{T}
  \nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t}).
  \label{eq:sequence-score}
\end{equation}
Since
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_\theta W_\theta
  =W_\theta S_\theta,
  \label{eq:weight-derivative}
\end{equation}
differentiating the population identity gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  g(\theta)
  :=\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
  =\E_Q[W_\theta A S_\theta].}
  \label{eq:true-gradient}
\end{equation}
The rollout return $J(Q)$ is constant in $\theta$, so differentiating policy
improvement and differentiating $J(\theta)$ give the same gradient.

Differentiating the finite estimator \eqref{eq:finite-improvement} gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \widehat g_N(\theta)
  :=\nabla_\theta\widehat\Delta_N(\theta;Q)
  =\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}
  W_{\theta,i}A_iS_{\theta,i}.}
  \label{eq:raw-gradient}
\end{equation}
This is the exact full-sequence importance-weighted policy-gradient estimator.
No additional optimization abstraction has been introduced: it is simply the
gradient of the finite policy-improvement estimator.

For concise notation, define the unweighted sequence-gradient contribution
\begin{equation}
  H_\theta(X,Y):=A(X,Y)S_\theta(X,Y).
  \label{eq:H-definition}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
  g(\theta)=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta],
  \qquad
  \widehat g_N(\theta)=\frac1N\sum_iW_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}.
  \label{eq:g-H}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Exact finite-sample gradient reliability}

The next result treats $\theta$ as fixed while the $N$ responses are sampled
independently from $Q$.  This is the standard pointwise statement underlying
importance-sampling analysis.  Its relation to repeated same-batch adaptation
is discussed after the theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Exact error of the raw sequence-gradient estimator]
\label{thm:raw-gradient-error}
For fixed $\theta$ and independent responses from $Q$,
\begin{equation}
  \E[\widehat g_N(\theta)]=g(\theta),
  \label{eq:gradient-unbiased}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \E\|\widehat g_N(\theta)-g(\theta)\|_2^2
  =\frac1N\left\{
  \E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]
  -\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right\}.}
  \label{eq:exact-gradient-mse}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Unbiasedness follows directly from \eqref{eq:g-H}.  Let
$Z=W_\theta H_\theta$ and $\mu=\E_QZ=g(\theta)$.  Independence gives
\begin{align}
  \E\left\|\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}(Z_i-\mu)\right\|_2^2
  &=\frac1N\E\|Z-\mu\|_2^2\\
  &=\frac1N\{\E\|Z\|_2^2-\|\mu\|_2^2\},
\end{align}
which is \eqref{eq:exact-gradient-mse}.
\end{proof}

Equation \eqref{eq:exact-gradient-mse} identifies the exact statistical object:
\begin{equation}
  \E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2].
  \label{eq:joint-second-moment}
\end{equation}
ESS measures the concentration of $W_\theta$, but the gradient error also
depends on whether large weights coincide with large gradient contributions.
To make this distinction explicit, define
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}
  :=\frac{
    \E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]
  }{
    \E_Q[W_\theta^2]
  }.
  \label{eq:weighted-gradient-scale}
\end{equation}
The role of $G_{2,\theta}$ is simple: it is the average squared size of the
sequence-gradient contribution under the distribution that emphasizes
responses in proportion to $W_\theta^2$.  It separates gradient magnitude from
ratio concentration.  Since $\E_QW_\theta^2=\rho_\theta^{-1}$,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \E\|\widehat g_N-g\|_2^2
  =\frac1N\left(
    \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{\rho_\theta}
    -\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right)
  \le\frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}.}
  \label{eq:ess-gradient-exact}
\end{equation}
This is the precise sense in which sequence ESS governs raw gradient
reliability: for a controlled weighted gradient scale, decreasing ESS enlarges
the squared estimation error as $1/(N\rho_\theta)$.

A simpler worst-case version follows from bounded gradient contributions.

\begin{corollary}[ESS reliability under bounded sequence contributions]
\label{cor:bounded-gradient}
If
\begin{equation}
  \|H_\theta(X,Y)\|_2\le G_\theta
  \quad Q\text{-almost surely},
  \label{eq:H-bound}
\end{equation}
then
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \E\|\widehat g_N(\theta)-g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \le\frac{G_\theta^2}{N\rho_\theta}.}
  \label{eq:gradient-ess-bound}
\end{equation}
Consequently, a sufficient condition for root-mean-squared gradient error at
most $\varepsilon$ is
\begin{equation}
  N\rho_\theta\ge\frac{G_\theta^2}{\varepsilon^2}.
  \label{eq:reliability-condition}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

This result gives a direct interpretation to the effective sequence count
$N\rho_\theta$.  When that count is large relative to the scale of individual
sequence-gradient contributions, the raw estimator is reliable and there is
little statistical reason to introduce clipping bias.

\subsection{The additional uncertainty caused by stale-batch reweighting}

Even at $P_\theta=Q$, a policy-gradient estimate has ordinary Monte Carlo
noise.  To isolate the additional uncertainty introduced by moving away from
the rollout policy, write
\begin{equation}
  W_\theta H_\theta
  =H_\theta+(W_\theta-1)H_\theta.
  \label{eq:gradient-decomposition}
\end{equation}
The first term is the on-policy contribution.  The second is the correction
required because the learner no longer equals the rollout policy.

Define
\begin{equation}
  \widehat c_N(\theta)
  :=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}(W_{\theta,i}-1)H_{\theta,i},
  \qquad
  c(\theta):=\E_Q[(W_\theta-1)H_\theta].
  \label{eq:off-policy-correction}
\end{equation}

\begin{corollary}[ESS controls additional stale-batch uncertainty]
\label{cor:off-policy-correction}
Under \eqref{eq:H-bound},
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \E\|\widehat c_N(\theta)-c(\theta)\|_2^2
  \le
  \frac{G_\theta^2}{N}
  (\rho_\theta^{-1}-1).}
  \label{eq:off-policy-error}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply the calculation in \Cref{thm:raw-gradient-error} to the random vector
$(W_\theta-1)H_\theta$ and use
\begin{align}
  \E_Q[(W_\theta-1)^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]
  &\le G_\theta^2\E_Q[(W_\theta-1)^2]\\
  &=G_\theta^2(\rho_\theta^{-1}-1).
\end{align}
\end{proof}

Unlike the total gradient error, the bound in \eqref{eq:off-policy-error}
vanishes exactly at $P_\theta=Q$.  It therefore isolates the worst-case
additional uncertainty created by repeated reuse of stale rollouts.

\begin{remark}[Pointwise theory and repeated use of the same batch]
The unbiasedness and expectation statements above hold for a fixed $\theta$
with respect to a fresh random batch from $Q$.  In the actual inner loop,
$\theta_k$ is produced using the same batch, so it is statistically dependent
on that batch.  A fully uniform theorem over the complete neural optimization
path would require additional complexity or algorithmic-stability control.
The pointwise theory identifies the exact second-moment mechanism.  The
forked intervention experiments then test whether the raw ESS measured along
the adaptive training path predicts the practical optimizer transition.
\end{remark}

\section{Clipping as a bias--variance intervention}
\label{sec:clipping}

\subsection{A modified sequence coefficient}

Let $U_\theta=U(W_\theta)\ge0$ be the sequence coefficient used after a
conservative intervention.  Weight truncation is a classical way to exchange
importance-sampling bias for lower variance \cite{ionides2008truncated}; POIS
records the same tradeoff at the policy-performance-estimator level
\cite{metelli2020is}.  Here we analyze the corresponding vector gradient
estimator.  An upper cap is the important example,
\begin{equation}
  U_\theta=\min\{W_\theta,c\},
  \qquad c>0.
  \label{eq:upper-cap}
\end{equation}
The modified gradient estimator is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_{U,N}(\theta)
  :=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}U_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}.
  \label{eq:modified-gradient}
\end{equation}
Unlike the raw estimator, it is not generally unbiased for $g(\theta)$.  Its
bias is
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  b_U(\theta)
  :=\E_Q[\widehat g_{U,N}(\theta)]-g(\theta)
  =\E_Q[(U_\theta-W_\theta)H_\theta].}
  \label{eq:clipping-bias}
\end{equation}
This vector is the gradient signal changed by clipping.  Let
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma_W(\theta):=\Cov_Q(W_\theta H_\theta),
  \qquad
  \Sigma_U(\theta):=\Cov_Q(U_\theta H_\theta).
  \label{eq:covariances}
\end{equation}
Their traces are the sums of coordinate-wise variances of one sequence
contribution.

\subsection{The exact crossover}

\begin{theorem}[Exact finite-sample clipping criterion]
\label{thm:clipping-crossover}
For fixed $\theta$,
\begin{align}
  \E\|\widehat g_N-g\|_2^2
  &=\frac{1}{N}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W),
  \label{eq:raw-gradient-risk}\\
  \E\|\widehat g_{U,N}-g\|_2^2
  &=\|b_U\|_2^2
    +\frac{1}{N}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U).
  \label{eq:modified-gradient-risk}
\end{align}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \E\|\widehat g_{U,N}-g\|_2^2
  <\E\|\widehat g_N-g\|_2^2
  \iff
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U)
  >N\|b_U\|_2^2.}
  \label{eq:exact-crossover}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The raw identity follows from unbiasedness.  For the modified estimator, add
and subtract its expectation:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_{U,N}-g
  =\{\widehat g_{U,N}-\E\widehat g_{U,N}\}+b_U.
\end{equation}
The cross term vanishes after expectation, giving
\eqref{eq:modified-gradient-risk}.  Rearranging the two risks gives
\eqref{eq:exact-crossover}.
\end{proof}

This theorem formalizes the proposed story exactly.  Clipping is not justified
merely because ESS is low or because post-clipping ESS increases.  It is
justified when the amount of gradient variance removed is larger than the
squared gradient bias paid at sample size $N$.

\paragraph{High-ESS regime.}
When $\rho_\theta$ is high, the ratio-concentration component of raw MSE is
controlled.  If $G_{2,\theta}$ is also stable, the exact raw error in
\eqref{eq:ess-gradient-exact} is small and clipping has limited covariance to
remove while still paying $\|b_U\|_2^2$.  This makes the low-bias estimator the
natural default, but the conclusion depends on the joint gradient moment, not
on ESS alone.

\paragraph{Low-ESS regime.}
When $\rho_\theta$ is low, the raw error envelope grows because
$\E_QW_\theta^2=\rho_\theta^{-1}$.  A bounded coefficient can prevent this
ratio-driven growth, making the left side of \eqref{eq:exact-crossover} large
enough to pay the bias.  Low ESS therefore identifies the regime in which
clipping can become preferable; the exact sign remains an empirical property
of the chosen estimator and training state.

A necessary condition makes the role of ESS explicit.  Under
\eqref{eq:H-bound}, if clipping improves squared estimation error, then
\begin{equation}
  N\|b_U\|_2^2
  <\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)
  \le\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]
  \le\frac{G_\theta^2}{\rho_\theta}.
  \label{eq:necessary-low-ess}
\end{equation}
Hence a necessary condition is
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\theta
  <\frac{G_\theta^2}{N\|b_U\|_2^2}
  \quad\text{when }b_U\ne0.
  \label{eq:necessary-rho}
\end{equation}
The boundary depends on batch size and on how much useful gradient signal the
chosen clipping rule removes; it cannot be universal.

\subsection{A transparent upper-cap envelope}

For $U_\theta=\min\{W_\theta,c\}$, define the removed ratio mass
\begin{equation}
  m_c(\theta):=\E_Q[(W_\theta-c)_+].
  \label{eq:removed-mass}
\end{equation}
Under \eqref{eq:H-bound},
\begin{equation}
  \|b_c(\theta)\|_2
  =\left\|\E_Q[(W_\theta-c)_+H_\theta]\right\|_2
  \le G_\theta m_c(\theta).
  \label{eq:cap-bias-bound}
\end{equation}
The cap also gives a variance bound that no longer depends on the raw ESS.
Since $0\le U_\theta\le c$, $U_\theta^2\le cU_\theta$, and
$\E_Q U_\theta\le\E_QW_\theta=1$,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U)
  \le\E_Q[U_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]
  \le cG_\theta^2.
  \label{eq:cap-variance-bound}
\end{equation}
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \E\|\widehat g_{c,N}-g\|_2^2
  \le
  G_\theta^2\left\{
    m_c(\theta)^2+\frac{c}{N}
  \right\}.}
  \label{eq:cap-risk-envelope}
\end{equation}
The first term is the bias price for removed tail mass; the second is the
bounded residual variance.  In contrast, the raw envelope is
$G_\theta^2/(N\rho_\theta)$.  These two envelopes display the intended regime
transition, although the exact decision remains \eqref{eq:exact-crossover}
 because an upper bound need not equal the realized risk.

\subsection{Why gradient MSE is optimization-relevant}
\label{sec:mse-optimization}

The crossover in \Cref{thm:clipping-crossover} is initially an estimator
statement.  It says which random vector is closer, in expected squared
Euclidean distance, to the population gradient.  This is the correct
statistical comparison once clipping introduces bias: variance alone can be
made arbitrarily small by shrinking the estimator toward zero, while MSE
charges both that systematic distortion and the remaining sampling noise.

There is also a concrete optimization reason to study this quantity.  In
Section~3 of Driggs et al.\ \cite{driggs2022biased}, the error of a biased
stochastic gradient is explicitly decomposed into squared bias and variance.
Their nonconvex Theorem~20 controls convergence for estimators satisfying a
bounded mean-squared error (BMSE) property, and Remark~21 states that bias
affects that guarantee only through MSE.  This is why their recursively biased
estimators can outperform estimators with smaller bias but larger total error.
Tang \cite{tang2022biased} gives a policy-gradient example of the same
principle: a biased score-function estimator trades vanishing bias for a
substantial variance reduction and obtains better sample-size dependence in
meta-RL.

These results do not justify an unconditional statement that lower MSE always
produces the better RL trajectory.  Ajalloeian and Stich
\cite{ajalloeian2020biased} show that biased-gradient convergence depends on
how bias scales with the true gradient and that additive bias creates an
accuracy floor.  Chung et al.\ \cite{chung2021baselines} construct policy-
gradient examples in which estimators with the same mean and variance induce
different learning dynamics because the sampling distribution changes with
the policy.  Accordingly, our use of MSE is deliberately precise: we show that
it is the additive estimator-error term in a standard smooth nonconvex
stationarity bound for the ordinary first-order update used as our theoretical
model.

Let $\mathcal F_k$ denote the optimization history before iteration $k$, so
$\theta_k$ is $\mathcal F_k$-measurable.  Consider
\begin{equation}
  \theta_{k+1}=\theta_k+\eta\widehat g_k,
  \label{eq:plain-sgd-update}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat g_k$ may be biased.  Define its conditional gradient MSE by
\begin{equation}
  m_k
  :=\E\!\left[
    \|\widehat g_k-\nabla J(\theta_k)\|_2^2
    \mid\mathcal F_k
  \right].
  \label{eq:iteration-mse}
\end{equation}
This is the expected squared error of the vector actually supplied to the
plain gradient update, conditional on the current training state.

\begin{theorem}[MSE-controlled stationarity for plain stochastic gradient ascent]
\label{thm:mse-stationarity}
Assume that $J$ is $L$-smooth and bounded above by $J_{\max}$.  Run
\eqref{eq:plain-sgd-update} for $T$ iterations with a constant step size
$0<\eta\le 1/L$.  Then, for any possibly biased stochastic gradient estimators
$\widehat g_0,\ldots,\widehat g_{T-1}$,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \frac1T\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}
  \E\|\nabla J(\theta_k)\|_2^2
  \le
  \frac{2\{J_{\max}-\E J(\theta_0)\}}{\eta T}
  +\frac1T\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\E[m_k].}
  \label{eq:mse-stationarity-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $g_k=\nabla J(\theta_k)$.  $L$-smoothness gives the lower bound
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta_{k+1})-J(\theta_k)
  \ge
  \eta g_k^\top\widehat g_k
  -\frac{L\eta^2}{2}\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:smooth-ascent-step}
\end{equation}
Use the identity
\begin{equation}
  2g_k^\top\widehat g_k
  =\|g_k\|_2^2+\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2
  -\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
Substitution into \eqref{eq:smooth-ascent-step} yields
\begin{align}
  J(\theta_{k+1})-J(\theta_k)
  \ge{}&
  \frac{\eta}{2}\|g_k\|_2^2
  +\frac{\eta}{2}(1-L\eta)\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2
  -\frac{\eta}{2}\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2\\
  \ge{}&
  \frac{\eta}{2}\|g_k\|_2^2
  -\frac{\eta}{2}\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2,
  \label{eq:mse-progress}
\end{align}
where the last line uses $\eta\le1/L$.  Take expectations, sum over
$k=0,\ldots,T-1$, telescope the objective values, and use
$\E J(\theta_T)\le J_{\max}$.  Rearranging gives
\eqref{eq:mse-stationarity-bound}.
\end{proof}

The theorem is the minimal bridge needed by this paper.  For a common step
size and the same smooth objective, a smaller conditional gradient MSE gives a
smaller additive statistical term in the stationarity guarantee.  The result
requires neither unbiasedness nor a separate assumption on the direction of
the bias because both bias and variance are already charged through $m_k$.
It is a simpler plain-gradient counterpart of the nonconvex BMSE mechanism in
Driggs et al.\ \cite{driggs2022biased}, not a replacement for their broader
finite-sum analysis.

The conclusion is intentionally limited.  It does not prove that two
estimators with different MSEs generate globally ordered RL trajectories, it
does not analyze Adam's moment state, and it does not make exploration or
policy-dependent sampling disappear.  Those limitations are exactly why the
paper pairs the theorem with direct matched-state measurements of estimator
MSE and subsequent policy improvement.

\begin{corollary}[The clipping crossover enters the stationarity bound]
\label{cor:mse-crossover-optimization}
Suppose that at iteration $k$ the raw and modified estimators satisfy the
pointwise model of \Cref{thm:clipping-crossover}.  Define
\begin{align}
  m_{W,k}
  &:=\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{W,k}),
  \\
  m_{U,k}
  &:=\|b_{U,k}\|_2^2
  +\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{U,k}).
  \label{eq:raw-clipped-step-mse}
\end{align}
Using the modified estimator at step $k$ gives a smaller additive term in
\eqref{eq:mse-stationarity-bound} exactly when
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{W,k})
  -\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{U,k})
  >N\|b_{U,k}\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:optimization-crossover}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

Thus the estimator criterion is not disconnected from optimization: it is
exactly the condition under which clipping improves the MSE term in the
plain-gradient stationarity guarantee.

\subsection{The ESS gate as an empirical single-crossing rule}
\label{sec:single-crossing}

ESS does not determine $m_{W,k}-m_{U,k}$ by itself.  The exact difference also
depends on the sequence-gradient contributions and on what the clipping rule
removes.  Our empirical observation can be stated as a testable
single-crossing property.

\begin{assumption}[ESS-conditioned MSE crossover]
\label{ass:single-crossing}
For the fixed training recipe and the two estimators being compared, there is
a threshold $\tau\in(0,1)$ such that along the relevant training states,
\begin{align}
  \rho_k\ge\tau
  &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_{W,k}\le m_{U,k},
  \label{eq:single-high}\\
  \rho_k<\tau
  &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_{U,k}<m_{W,k}.
  \label{eq:single-low}
\end{align}
\end{assumption}

This assumption is not derived from ESS alone.  It is the formal version of the
observed ranking reversal and must be checked by the estimator diagnostics in
\Cref{sec:experiments}.  The theory explains why such a crossing is plausible:
$m_{W,k}$ contains the ratio second moment $\rho_k^{-1}$, whereas a bounded
coefficient controls the variance of $m_{U,k}$ but pays clipping bias.

Define the gated estimator
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_k^{\mathrm{gate}}
  =
  \begin{cases}
    \widehat g_{W,k}, & \rho_k\ge\tau,\\
    \widehat g_{U,k}, & \rho_k<\tau.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:theoretical-gate}
\end{equation}

\begin{corollary}[Pointwise best two-estimator MSE term under single crossing]
\label{cor:gated-bound}
Under \Cref{ass:single-crossing}, the per-step MSE of
\eqref{eq:theoretical-gate} is
\begin{equation}
  m_{\mathrm{gate},k}=\min\{m_{W,k},m_{U,k}\}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, \Cref{thm:mse-stationarity} gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \frac1T\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\E\|\nabla J(\theta_k)\|_2^2
  \le
  \frac{2\{J_{\max}-\E J(\theta_0)\}}{\eta T}
  +\frac1T\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}
  \min\{m_{W,k},m_{U,k}\}.}
  \label{eq:gated-stationarity}
\end{equation}
At every state visited by the gated algorithm, the MSE term is no larger than
the term obtained by using either counterfactual estimator at that same state.
This is a pointwise estimator comparison; because different update rules visit
different future states, it is not by itself a global trajectory-dominance
theorem.
\end{corollary}

This corollary gives the clean theoretical interpretation of the empirical
threshold $0.1$: it estimates the crossing point at which the lower-MSE input
to the optimizer changes from the raw estimator to the clipped estimator.  The
threshold is recipe-dependent because both MSE curves depend on batch size,
model, reward, learning rate, and clipping rule.

\section{From population ESS to the online gate}
\label{sec:online}

The population quantity $\rho_\theta=1/\E_QW_\theta^2$ is not known during
training.  POIS likewise replaces population R\'enyi quantities by empirical
ESS in practical optimization and notes that this substitution is approximate,
particularly when rare high-weight events are not observed
\cite{metelli2020is}.  The practical gate uses the raw plug-in statistic
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho_\theta
  :=\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{\theta,i})^2}
  {N\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{\theta,i}^2}.
  \label{eq:sample-ess}
\end{equation}
The effective sequence count is $N\widehat\rho_\theta$.  The gate must use raw,
pre-intervention ratios: clipping mechanically homogenizes coefficients and
can raise post-clipping ESS without restoring the coverage lost by the rollout
batch.

\subsection{The two-mode controller}

Let $Q_j$ generate rollout batch $j$, and let $P_{j,k}$ be the learner after
$k$ updates on that batch.  The method begins each fresh batch in an aggressive
mode and switches once when raw sequence ESS falls below $\tau_\rho$.

\begin{center}
\fbox{\begin{minipage}{0.92\linewidth}
\textbf{Algorithm: Sequence-ESS-Gated RLVR} (\method).

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item Collect $\mathcal D_j=\{(X_i,Y_i,R_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ from $Q_j$.
Initialize $P_{j,0}=Q_j$ and mode $m_{j,0}=\textsc{Aggressive}$.
\item For $k=0,1,\ldots,K_j-1$:
  \begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item Compute the raw full-sequence ratios
  $W_{i,j,k}=P_{j,k}(Y_i\mid X_i)/Q_j(Y_i\mid X_i)$ and
  $\widehat\rho_{j,k}$ from \eqref{eq:sample-ess}.
  \item If $m_{j,k}=\textsc{Aggressive}$ and
  $\widehat\rho_{j,k}<\tau_\rho$, set the mode to
  $\textsc{Conservative}$ for the remainder of the batch.
  \item In aggressive mode, apply the implemented no-clip or CISPO-style
  policy-gradient update.  In conservative mode, apply the implemented clipped
  update [EXACT CONSERVATIVE OBJECTIVE].
  \end{enumerate}
\item Collecting fresh rollouts resets the mode to
$\textsc{Aggressive}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

The mode is latched because a post-clipping ESS increase is mechanical and does
not make the old batch on-policy again.  A stricter alternative is to stop
batch reuse and refresh rollouts after severe ESS failure.

\subsection{Numerically stable computation and auxiliary diagnostics}

Let $\ell_i=\log W_i$ and $a=\max_i\ell_i$.  The normalized ESS is computed
without forming extreme weights:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho
  =\frac{(\sum_i e^{\ell_i-a})^2}
  {N\sum_i e^{2(\ell_i-a)}}.
  \label{eq:stable-ess}
\end{equation}
The sample mean ratio
\begin{equation}
  \overline W=\frac1N\sum_iW_i,
  \qquad
  \log\overline W
  =\operatorname{logsumexp}(\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_N)-\log N
  \label{eq:mean-weight}
\end{equation}
is useful as an auxiliary diagnostic because population ratios satisfy
$\E_QW=1$.  Normalized sample ESS alone is scale invariant: if every observed
weight equals the same small constant, it reports one even though the batch
has likely missed rare target mass.  We therefore report $\overline W$ and
$N\widehat\rho$ alongside the primary threshold, without changing the
headline gate unless an ablation supports doing so.

\section{Relation to practical PPO, GRPO, and CISPO updates}
\label{sec:practical}

The exact estimator in \eqref{eq:raw-gradient} uses the complete-response ratio
$W_T$.  Practical LLM policy-gradient methods usually use token-local
coefficients.  The distinction must remain explicit:
\begin{equation}
  r_t=\frac{p_\theta(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t})}{q(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t})},
  \qquad
  W_t=\prod_{s\le t}r_s,
  \qquad
  W_T=\frac{P_\theta(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)}.
  \label{eq:three-ratios}
\end{equation}
$W_T$ is the Radon--Nikodym derivative for the terminal response reward.
A local token ratio is not a full-response change of measure.

CISPO clips detached token coefficients while retaining token gradients
\cite{minimax2025cispo}; PPO/GRPO clipping changes or zeroes token-level
contributions; GSPO uses a length-normalized sequence score
\cite{zheng2025gspo}.  None is algebraically identical to
\eqref{eq:raw-gradient}.  Therefore the paper makes two distinct claims:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Exact statistical claim.}  Raw full-sequence ESS controls the
second-moment reliability of the exact finite-batch sequence-gradient
estimator, and full-sequence clipping obeys the bias--variance theorem in
\Cref{thm:clipping-crossover}.
\item \textbf{Empirical optimizer claim.}  The same raw sequence ESS predicts
when the implemented no-clip/CISPO update should be replaced by the implemented
conservative PPO-type update.
\end{enumerate}
The second claim requires controlled interventions; it is not presented as an
algebraic consequence of the first.

This positioning separates our claim from both classical trust-region methods
and recent adaptive LLM objectives.  TRPO constrains a policy surrogate using a
KL region, while PPO approximates this idea through token-level ratio clipping
\cite{schulman2015trpo,schulman2017ppo}; ratio clipping is not itself a literal
global trust-region guarantee \cite{wang2020truly}.  DPPO instead controls a
direct policy-distribution divergence \cite{qi2026dppo}.  Among recent LLM
methods, VESPO continuously reshapes sequence weights
\cite{shen2026vespo}, P3O adapts token-level control using an ESS statistic
\cite{fakoor2026p3o}, M2PO controls a second-moment proxy under stale rollouts
\cite{zheng2025m2po}, and VCPO uses sequence ESS to continuously scale the
update \cite{huang2026vcpo}.  Our distinction is the estimator crossover: the
low-bias objective is retained while its sequence-gradient MSE is smaller, and
the conservative objective is activated only after the measured ordering
reverses.

For the practical gradients themselves, let $G_i^{\mathrm{free}}$ and
$G_i^{\mathrm{clip}}$ denote the complete per-response gradient aggregates
produced by the two implemented losses.  Relative to the mean free-surrogate
gradient, the generic identity
\begin{align}
  &\E\left\|
  \frac1N\sum_iG_i^{\mathrm{clip}}
  -\E G_i^{\mathrm{free}}
  \right\|_2^2
  \nonumber\\
  &\qquad=
  \left\|\E(G_i^{\mathrm{clip}}-G_i^{\mathrm{free}})\right\|_2^2
  +\frac1N\operatorname{tr}\Cov(G_i^{\mathrm{clip}})
  \label{eq:practical-bias-variance}
\end{align}
still separates clipping bias from sampling variance.  What remains empirical
is whether raw sequence ESS accurately tracks the covariance increase of the
practical free surrogate.  The experiments below are designed to test exactly
that bridge.

\section{Experimental protocol and falsifiable predictions}
\label{sec:experiments}

The empirical section must establish both the performance result and the
mechanism.  All methods should use matched rollouts, optimizer steps, batch
sizes, and compute.

\subsection{Main training comparison}

Compare:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item always no-clip [EXACT OBJECTIVE];
\item always CISPO [EXACT COEFFICIENT/RANGE];
\item always conservative [EXACT CLIPPED OBJECTIVE];
\item \method with $\tau_\rho=0.1$;
\item stopping batch reuse and refreshing rollouts after ESS failure;
\item closest adaptive baselines, including VCPO, M2PO, VESPO, P3O, and DPPO
where implementations are available.
\end{enumerate}
Report reward, held-out reasoning accuracy, raw sequence ESS, effective
sequence count, mean ratio, clipping rate, gradient norm, KL/TV movement,
response length, and wall-clock cost.

\subsection{Forked intervention at matched states}

This is the key causal experiment.  At the same model parameters, rollout
batch, and optimizer state, fork the next updates at states satisfying
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho\in\{0.5,0.2,0.1,0.05,0.01\}.
  \label{eq:fork-levels}
\end{equation}
Apply the aggressive and conservative objectives for a fixed number of steps.
Measure fresh-rollout reward improvement, held-out accuracy, gradient and
optimizer-moment spikes, KL/TV movement, and collapse frequency.  The predicted
ranking is
\begin{equation}
  \text{aggressive better above the boundary, conservative better below it}.
  \label{eq:ranking-prediction}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Direct estimator diagnostics}

The theory predicts a crossing of gradient MSE, not merely a change in
coefficient ESS.  At selected training states, hold $\theta$ and $Q$ fixed,
resample independent rollout batches, and construct a high-accuracy reference
gradient from a much larger sample.  Estimate
\begin{align}
  m_W,
  \qquad
  m_U,
  \qquad
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U),
  \qquad
  \|b_U\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:direct-estimator-quantities}
\end{align}
Plot the signed difference
\begin{equation}
  \Delta m(\rho):=m_U-m_W
  \label{eq:mse-difference}
\end{equation}
against raw sequence ESS.  The central prediction is a sign change near the
gating threshold: $\Delta m>0$ above the boundary and $\Delta m<0$ below it.
Equivalently, test whether
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U)
  \approx N\|b_U\|_2^2
  \label{eq:empirical-crossover}
\end{equation}
near $\rho\simeq0.1$.  Then test whether the estimator with smaller MSE also
produces the better matched-state fork under the same optimizer state.  If the
full parameter gradient is too large to store, use random projections or
predeclared layerwise blocks; the projection must be fixed before resampling.

\subsection{Threshold, batch-size, and diagnostic ablations}

Sweep
\begin{equation}
  \tau_\rho\in\{0,0.03,0.05,0.1,0.2,0.3,1\},
  \label{eq:threshold-sweep}
\end{equation}
where zero means never switch and one means switch immediately.  Repeat at
multiple sequence batch sizes.  Since the theoretical reliability scale is
$N\rho$, a normalized threshold need not transfer unchanged when $N$ varies.
Compare raw sequence ESS with token ESS, outbound-token fraction, mean ratio,
KL, entropy, maximum ratio, and gradient norm as predictors of collapse.

\subsection{Reporting the threshold}

State where $0.1$ was selected, whether it was frozen before evaluation on
other models and tasks, the exact number of independent sequences entering
ESS, and whether group sampling or shared prompts induce dependence.  Also
state whether $Q$ in the denominator exactly matches the sampler, including
temperature, top-$p$/top-$k$, EOS handling, rejection filters, numerical
precision, and MoE routing.

\section{Limitations and scope}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Fixed-$\theta$ theory versus adaptive batch reuse.}
The exact expectation and covariance results condition on a fixed learner and
random data from $Q$.  In the inner loop, the learner depends on the reused
batch.  Extending the pointwise result uniformly over a neural optimization
path requires further complexity or stability assumptions.  The present paper
uses the exact pointwise theory to identify the mechanism and validates the
adaptive use of ESS experimentally.

\paragraph{ESS is not the complete gradient variance.}
The exact quantity is
$\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]$.  ESS isolates
$\E_QW_\theta^2$ but does not know whether extreme weights coincide with large
gradient contributions.  This is why direct variance and bias measurements are
part of the experimental protocol.

\paragraph{Full-sequence theory versus token-local practice.}
The exact derivation concerns the full-sequence importance-weighted gradient
and full-sequence capping.  PPO, GRPO, CISPO, and DPPO optimize different
surrogates.  Their regime reversal is an empirical result rather than a hidden
algebraic assumption.

\paragraph{Sample ESS and unseen tail mass.}
The plug-in statistic can miss rare high-weight events.  Mean-ratio and
effective-count diagnostics help reveal this failure but do not eliminate it.

\paragraph{Sampler correctness and support.}
$Q$ must be the actual response distribution used to generate the batch.
Temperature, top-$p$/top-$k$, rejection filters, EOS handling, numerical
train--inference mismatch, and MoE routing can invalidate the likelihood ratio.
If $P_\theta$ assigns mass outside the support of $Q$, exact importance
sampling is unavailable.

\paragraph{Threshold universality.}
The value $0.1$ is an empirical crossover under a fixed batch size, model,
learning rate, reward distribution, and conservative objective.  Theory
predicts dependence on $N$, gradient-contribution scale, clipping bias, and
variance reduction.  Transfer of the same threshold is an empirical finding,
not a universal theorem.

\paragraph{Plain-gradient optimization theorem.}
\Cref{thm:mse-stationarity} analyzes ordinary stochastic gradient ascent with
a common step size.  It is not an exact analysis of Adam's moment estimates,
nor does it imply that lower MSE determines exploration or pathwise policy
dynamics.  Its role is precise: it shows that MSE is an additive term in a
standard smooth nonconvex stationarity guarantee.

\section{Conclusion}

Repeated rollout-batch reuse in RLVR creates a changing statistical problem.
The raw full-sequence estimator is initially low bias and effective, but its
second moment can grow as the learner moves away from the rollout policy.
Sequence ESS identifies the likelihood-ratio component of this deterioration;
it does not by itself determine the complete gradient variance, which also
depends on the gradient contributions attached to large ratios.

Clipping should therefore be evaluated as a change of gradient estimator, not
as a cost-free safeguard.  Replacing $W_\theta$ by $U_\theta$ introduces the
bias $b_U=\E_Q[(U_\theta-W_\theta)H_\theta]$ while changing covariance.  The
exact finite-sample decision is
\[
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U)
  >N\|b_U\|_2^2.
\]
This criterion is stricter than low raw ESS, high post-clipping ESS, or a large
outbound-ratio fraction: variance reduction must actually pay for the gradient
signal removed by clipping.

Gradient MSE is optimization-relevant here for a concrete reason.  Under the
standard smooth plain-gradient model, average MSE is the additive statistical
term in the nonconvex stationarity bound.  This is the same mechanism
identified more generally by biased stochastic-gradient theory, while the
bias and policy-dynamics literature explains why it is not a universal global
ordering of RL algorithms.  The theoretical claim is therefore paired with
direct measurements of raw MSE, clipped bias, covariance reduction, and
matched-state policy improvement.

The resulting interpretation is an ESS-conditioned estimator crossover.  In
the healthy regime, the raw estimator's ratio-driven error is controlled and
clipping needlessly distorts the update.  In the low-ESS regime, a bounded
estimator can reduce enough covariance to justify its bias.  The observed
threshold must be measured rather than assumed: $0.1$ is a candidate operating
point for the proposed protocol, not a universal constant or a completed
empirical finding.  An ESS gate operationalizes the hypothesis by retaining
the low-bias objective until the validated crossover regime is reached and
activating the conservative objective thereafter.
Completing the matched-state resampling study across models, tasks, and batch
sizes is the necessary next step for turning this estimator-level account into
a calibrated training rule.

\section{General MDPs and per-decision importance sampling}
\label{app:mdp}

For a discounted MDP, the performance-difference lemma is
\begin{equation}
  J(\pi')-J(\pi)
  =\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
  \E_{s\sim d_{\pi'}}
  \E_{a\sim\pi'(\cdot\mid s)}[A_\pi(s,a)].
  \label{eq:mdp-pdl}
\end{equation}
TRPO replaces $d_{\pi'}$ by $d_\pi$ in a tractable surrogate and controls the
resulting occupancy residual.  This occupancy argument underlies TRPO
\cite{schulman2015trpo}.  It is distinct from the finite-sample reliability of
importance weighting.

If one changes measure on the complete trajectory distribution, the identity
is exact but the trajectory ratio inherits the horizon problem.  Per-decision
importance sampling can reduce variance for rewards observed before the end of
a trajectory \cite{precup2000eligibility,metelli2020is}.  In terminal-reward
RLVR, direct correction of the terminal reward uses the full-response ratio.

\section{Prompt groups and group-relative advantages}
\label{app:groups}

If several responses are sampled for one prompt and their advantages share a
group mean or standard deviation, the responses are statistically coupled.
The exact main-text derivation applies directly to raw verifier rewards or to a
prompt-only baseline fixed independently of the response.  For a grouped
training surrogate, the independent unit is the prompt cluster, not each token
and not necessarily each response.

Let $G_{jg}$ denote the full per-response gradient aggregate for response $g$
of prompt $j$, and define the cluster contribution
\begin{equation}
  C_j:=\frac1G\sum_{g=1}^{G}G_{jg}.
  \label{eq:cluster-gradient}
\end{equation}
Covariance and concentration should be computed across independent prompt
clusters $C_1,\ldots,C_M$.  Increasing group size can reduce conditional
variance within a prompt but does not create $MG$ independent prompts.

\section{Optional finite-variance robustification}
\label{app:robust}

The main gradient results are second-moment identities rather than high-
confidence bounds.  If one needs a robust estimate of a scalar projection
$v^\top g(\theta)$ under only finite variance, median-of-means can be applied
to the projected sequence contributions
$v^\top W_\theta H_\theta$.  For a fixed unit vector $v$, this gives error of
order
\begin{equation}
  \sqrt{
  \frac{
    \Var_Q(v^\top W_\theta H_\theta)
    \log(1/\delta)
  }{N}}
  \label{eq:robust-projection}
\end{equation}
without requiring bounded weights.  A simultaneous vector guarantee requires
additional dimension or complexity control and is not needed for the central
bias--variance result.
