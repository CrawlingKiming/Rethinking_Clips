\title{\bf When Does Clipping Help in RLVR?\\
Sequence ESS as a Bridge from Policy Drift to Estimator Reliability}
\author{Anonymous authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) often reuses each rollout
batch for several policy updates.  Such reuse does not make an unclipped update
intrinsically unstable.  A key source of finite-sample instability appears when
the updated policy is poorly covered by the rollout policy, causing sequence
importance weights to concentrate and the effective number of samples to
collapse.  We formalize this bridge through sequence effective sample size
(ESS).

For the exact terminal-reward policy gradient, we derive the finite-sample MSE
of the unclipped importance-weighted estimator.  Its ratio-driven term factors
through $(N\rho)^{-1}$, where $N$ is the batch size and
$\rho=1/\E_Q[W^2]$ is normalized sequence ESS.  Thus an unclipped estimator can
remain reliable after several updates when ESS is high and the weighted
gradient scale is controlled.  We then show
that clipping is preferred only when its covariance reduction exceeds $N$
times its squared gradient bias.  ESS therefore governs when clipping may
become useful, but does not by itself determine the decision.

The theory concerns a fixed full-sequence estimator.  Applying it to practical
token-level RLVR updates requires direct MSE measurements and matched-state
experiments, which we state as the remaining empirical tests.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

RLVR commonly takes multiple gradient steps on one batch of generated
responses.  The usual explanation for clipping is that reused data become
stale and unclipped updates therefore become unstable.  This explanation is
incomplete: staleness records how long a batch has been reused, not whether it
still supports an accurate update.  An unclipped estimator can remain reliable
after several steps if the new policy is still well covered by the rollout
policy.

The missing bridge is sequence effective sample size (ESS).  Let $Q$ be the
rollout policy, $P_\theta$ the updated policy, and
$W_\theta=P_\theta(Y\mid X)/Q(Y\mid X)$ their full-response likelihood ratio.
Normalized population ESS is
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\theta:=\frac{1}{\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}.
  \label{eq:intro-ess}
\end{equation}
When $W_\theta$ remains diffuse, $\rho_\theta$ is high and the batch retains
many effective sequences.  When a few responses dominate, $\rho_\theta$ falls.
ESS therefore converts policy drift into a finite-sample coverage quantity.

We make this connection precise for the exact terminal-reward policy gradient.
For a fixed learner and $N$ independent rollout responses, the MSE of the raw
importance-weighted gradient is
\begin{equation}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  =\frac{1}{N}\left(
    \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{\rho_\theta}-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right),
  \label{eq:intro-mse}
\end{equation}
where $G_{2,\theta}$ measures the squared gradient contributions emphasized by
$W_\theta^2$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:intro-mse} is the theoretical bridge: policy
drift matters through the concentration it induces, and sequence ESS isolates
that concentration.  In particular, no clipping is needed merely because a
batch is old; the statistical pressure for clipping appears as $N\rho_\theta$
shrinks.

Clipping becomes a second decision, not the definition of stability.  Replacing
$W_\theta$ by a bounded detached coefficient $U_\theta$ reduces variance but
introduces gradient bias $b_U$.  We prove the exact crossover
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_U)
  <\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  \iff
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U)
  >N\|b_U\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:intro-crossover}
\end{equation}
Low ESS creates room for clipping to help by enlarging the raw second moment;
whether it actually helps depends on the bias and covariance of the chosen
clipping rule.  ESS is thus a governing diagnostic, not a complete decision
rule.

The paper develops this argument in four steps.  We first derive the exact
full-sequence gradient from the RLVR objective.  We then show how sequence ESS
controls its ratio-driven finite-sample error.  Next, we derive the clipping
MSE crossover and connect gradient MSE to optimization.  Finally, we state the
minimal experiments needed to test whether the same bridge predicts practical
PPO-, GRPO-, or CISPO-style updates.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item We identify sequence ESS as the bridge between policy drift and the
finite-sample reliability of the unclipped full-sequence policy gradient.
\item We show exactly when clipping improves gradient MSE: covariance reduction
must exceed the squared-bias cost at batch size $N$.
\item We separate the proved estimator statement from the empirical claim that
sample ESS can gate practical token-level RLVR objectives.
\end{enumerate}

\section{From batch reuse to sequence ESS}
\label{sec:ess}

Let $X\sim\nu$ be a prompt, $Y$ a complete response generated by $Q$, and
$R=R(X,Y)$ a terminal verifier reward.  The learner $P_\theta$ is absolutely
continuous with respect to $Q$, and both policies share the prompt distribution.
The sequence likelihood ratio is
\begin{equation}
  W_\theta(X,Y)
  :=\frac{P_\theta(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)},
  \qquad
  \E_Q[W_\theta\mid X]=1.
  \label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}

Let $b(X)$ be a prompt-only baseline, detached from differentiation, and write
$A=R-b(X)$.  Change of measure gives the exact centered improvement identity
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta)-J(Q)=\E_Q[(W_\theta-1)A],
  \qquad
  J(\theta):=\E_{P_\theta}[R].
  \label{eq:improvement}
\end{equation}
Thus reuse itself introduces no approximation: for any fixed learner covered
by $Q$, importance weighting exactly recovers its expected reward.

The difficulty is finite-sample concentration.  Since $\E_QW_\theta=1$,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\theta
  :=\frac{1}{\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
  =\exp\{-D_2(P_\theta\|Q)\},
  \qquad
  \E_Q[(W_\theta-1)^2]=\rho_\theta^{-1}-1.
  \label{eq:population-ess}
\end{equation}
Hence $N\rho_\theta$ is the population effective sequence count.  This is the
same second-moment coverage principle used in importance-sampling policy
optimization \cite{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}: policy drift becomes
statistically dangerous only when it concentrates likelihood-ratio mass.

\section{ESS governs the ratio-driven error of the unclipped gradient}
\label{sec:raw}

Define the full-response score and the unweighted sequence contribution
\begin{equation}
  S_\theta:=\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y\mid X),
  \qquad
  H_\theta:=A S_\theta.
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
Assume differentiation and expectation can be interchanged and
$\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]<\infty$.  Differentiating
\eqref{eq:improvement} yields
\begin{equation}
  g(\theta):=\nabla J(\theta)=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta].
  \label{eq:true-gradient}
\end{equation}
For $N$ independent responses from $Q$, the raw estimator is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_W(\theta):=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N
  W_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}.
  \label{eq:raw-estimator}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Exact finite-sample error]
\label{thm:raw-mse}
For fixed $\theta$, $\widehat g_W$ is unbiased and
\begin{equation}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  =\frac1N\left\{
    \E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:raw-mse}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $Z=W_\theta H_\theta$.  Then $\E_QZ=g(\theta)$ and independence gives
$\E\|N^{-1}\sum_i(Z_i-\E Z)\|_2^2=N^{-1}\E\|Z-\E Z\|_2^2$, which is
\eqref{eq:raw-mse}.
\end{proof}

To expose the role of ESS, define
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}
  :=\frac{\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]}
          {\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}.
  \label{eq:gradient-scale}
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq:population-ess} into
\eqref{eq:raw-mse} gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  =\frac1N\left(
    \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{\rho_\theta}-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right)
  \le \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}.}
  \label{eq:ess-bridge}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:ess-bridge} is the main theoretical bridge.  Sequence ESS
governs the error contributed by ratio concentration, while $G_{2,\theta}$
records whether large ratios coincide with large gradient contributions.  If
$G_{2,\theta}$ remains controlled, high ESS guarantees that the raw estimator
remains reliable; policy drift or update count alone does not imply
instability.  Conversely, falling ESS increases the statistical pressure for a
variance-reducing intervention.

\begin{remark}[Pointwise scope]
The expectation is over a fresh batch at fixed $\theta$.  During same-batch
optimization, $\theta$ depends on the reused data.  Extending
\eqref{eq:ess-bridge} uniformly over that adaptive path requires additional
stability or complexity control.
\end{remark}

\section{When clipping becomes preferable}
\label{sec:clipping}

Let $U_\theta=U(W_\theta)$ be a bounded, detached sequence coefficient.  The
modified estimator
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_U:=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N U_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}
  \label{eq:clipped-estimator}
\end{equation}
has gradient bias and one-sample covariances
\begin{equation}
  b_U:=\E_Q[(U_\theta-W_\theta)H_\theta],
  \quad
  \Sigma_W:=\Cov_Q(W_\theta H_\theta),
  \quad
  \Sigma_U:=\Cov_Q(U_\theta H_\theta).
  \label{eq:bias-covariance}
\end{equation}
Detachment matters: differentiating a scalar expression containing
$U(W_\theta)$ generally produces additional chain-rule terms.

\begin{theorem}[Exact clipping crossover]
\label{thm:crossover}
For fixed $\theta$ and finite second moments,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  =\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_U)
  =\|b_U\|_2^2+\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U).
  \label{eq:two-risks}
\end{equation}
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_U)
  <\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  \iff
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U)
  >N\|b_U\|_2^2.}
  \label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The raw estimator is unbiased.  For the modified estimator, add and subtract
$\E\widehat g_U$; the centered term and constant bias have zero cross term in
expectation.  Equation~\eqref{eq:crossover} follows by subtraction.
\end{proof}

The two theorems produce the intended conclusion.  High ESS keeps the raw
ratio-driven error small, leaving little variance for clipping to remove.  Low
ESS increases the raw second moment and can make variance reduction worth its
bias.  But ESS cannot determine the sign of \eqref{eq:crossover}, because the
decision also depends on $G_{2,\theta}$ and on what the clipping rule removes.
The claim is therefore not ``clip below a universal ESS,'' but ``ESS locates
the regime in which the clipping MSE comparison becomes consequential.''

\subsection{Why MSE matters for optimization}

The crossover compares errors relative to the population gradient.  The next
result shows why this quantity matters for a standard first-order update.

\begin{proposition}[MSE-controlled stationarity]
\label{prop:stationarity}
Suppose $J$ is $L$-smooth and bounded above by $J_{\max}$.  For
$\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k+\eta\widehat g_k$ with $0<\eta\le 1/L$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac1T\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\E\|\nabla J(\theta_k)\|_2^2
  \le
  \frac{2\{J_{\max}-\E J(\theta_0)\}}{\eta T}
  +\frac1T\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}
   \E\|\widehat g_k-\nabla J(\theta_k)\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:stationarity}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Smoothness and the update give
\[
J(\theta_{k+1})-J(\theta_k)
\ge \eta\nabla J(\theta_k)^\top\widehat g_k
-\frac{L\eta^2}{2}\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2.
\]
Using $2a^\top b=\|a\|^2+\|b\|^2-\|a-b\|^2$ and
$\eta\le1/L$, the right side is at least
$\frac{\eta}{2}\|\nabla J(\theta_k)\|^2-
\frac{\eta}{2}\|\widehat g_k-\nabla J(\theta_k)\|^2$.
Take expectations, sum, and use $J(\theta_T)\le J_{\max}$.
\end{proof}

Thus clipping improves the statistical term in this stationarity guarantee
exactly when \eqref{eq:crossover} holds.  The proposition does not analyze Adam
or imply globally ordered RL trajectories; it supplies the limited optimization
bridge required by the estimator comparison.  This use of MSE is consistent
with broader analyses of biased stochastic gradients
\cite{driggs2022biased,tang2022biased}, while persistent clipping bias can still
create an error floor \cite{ajalloeian2020biased,koloskova2023clipping}.

\section{From theory to a practical ESS gate}
\label{sec:practice}

Population ESS is unavailable during training.  Its plug-in estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho
  :=\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^N W_i)^2}{N\sum_{i=1}^N W_i^2}.
  \label{eq:sample-ess}
\end{equation}
It should be computed from raw, pre-clipping sequence ratios.  A candidate gate
uses the low-bias objective while $\widehat\rho$ is high and switches to a
bounded objective when it falls below a calibrated threshold.

This rule is a hypothesis, not a consequence of
\Cref{thm:crossover}.  First, sample ESS can miss unobserved tail mass.  Second,
the same batch usually determines both the gate and the gradient.  Third,
practical PPO, GRPO, and CISPO objectives use token-local coefficients and are
not algebraically equal to \eqref{eq:raw-estimator}.  Recent LLM RL methods
already adapt updates using ESS, weight moments, or sequence reshaping
\cite{zheng2025m2po,shen2026vespo,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}; our distinct
empirical question is whether their relevant regime change coincides with the
gradient-MSE crossover in \eqref{eq:crossover}.

\section{Limitations}

The exact results are pointwise in a fixed full-sequence estimator.  They do
not establish uniform validity along an adaptive same-batch path or exact
equivalence to token-level RLVR losses.  Moreover, ESS captures weight
concentration but not the weighted gradient scale $G_{2,\theta}$, and its sample
estimate can miss rare target mass.  These limitations are why the MSE and
matched-state tests above are necessary.

\section{Conclusion}

An unclipped update is not intrinsically unstable.  Its ratio-driven reliability is
governed by whether the rollout batch still covers the updated policy.  For the
exact full-sequence gradient, sequence ESS makes this statement quantitative:
the ratio-driven error factors through $(N\rho_\theta)^{-1}$.  Clipping becomes useful
only when the covariance it removes exceeds the squared bias it introduces.
The resulting theory replaces a binary clipping intuition with a cumulative
chain: policy drift changes sequence weights, sequence ESS measures their
concentration, concentration governs raw estimator error, and that error
determines when a clipping comparison is warranted.  Establishing that this
chain governs practical token-level RLVR remains the empirical task.
