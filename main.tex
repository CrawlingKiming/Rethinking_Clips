\title{\bf When to Clip in RLVR:\\
Current Sequence ESS and One-Epoch Policy-Gradient Reliability}
\author{Anonymous authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) often performs a single
optimization epoch over a rollout batch divided into disjoint minibatches.
Each response is used once, but the learner changes after every minibatch.
Consequently, later minibatches remain fresh samples from the rollout policy
while becoming increasingly off-policy for the current learner.  We study the
resulting clipping trade-off.  In our experiments, unmasked and CISPO-style
updates learn more efficiently while the raw normalized effective sample size
(ESS) of full-response likelihood ratios remains above approximately $0.1$.
Below this boundary, switching to a conservative PPO-style masked update
prevents collapse and yields the strongest final performance.

We formalize the reliability of the gradient computed at the current
minibatch.  Conditional on all preceding minibatch updates, the current policy
is fixed and the unused minibatch is independent.  The exact full-sequence
importance-weighted gradient is therefore conditionally unbiased.  Its
conditional squared error admits an exact factorization into inverse current
sequence ESS and a gradient-contribution moment under the squared-weight tilted
distribution.  This result clarifies both the value and the limitation of ESS:
ESS measures likelihood-ratio concentration, but it does not by itself
determine gradient reliability.  In particular, a large coherent gradient is
signal rather than instability.

Under local smoothness of the population objective, a confidence radius for
the current gradient yields a lower bound on the population improvement of the
actual optimizer displacement.  The update is reliable when empirical ascent
exceeds gradient-estimation uncertainty and curvature cost.  We then separate
three operations that are frequently conflated: PPO/GRPO gradient masking,
CISPO token-coefficient capping, and full-sequence ratio truncation.  Their
biases are different, and only the last is a classical truncated
importance-sampling estimator.  The theory motivates a raw-ESS gate, while
contextual-bandit simulations and matched-minibatch RLVR interventions test
whether the practical ranking of masking and gradient-preserving updates
reverses at the observed boundary.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

RLVR algorithms collect complete responses from a rollout policy and update a
learner using the resulting verifier rewards.  The training recipe considered
in this paper uses one optimization epoch: the rollout batch is partitioned
into disjoint minibatches, and each response contributes to exactly one
parameter update.  Although there is no repeated use of an individual
response, the policy changes between minibatches.  A response in a later
minibatch was generated by the original rollout policy $Q$, whereas its
gradient is evaluated at the current learner $P_{\theta_k}$.  The corresponding
full-response likelihood ratio is
\begin{equation}
  W_k(X,Y)
  =\frac{P_{\theta_k}(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)}.
  \label{eq:intro-weight}
\end{equation}
As the learner moves away from $Q$, the distribution of $W_k$ can become
highly concentrated and a small number of responses can dominate the current
minibatch.

Clipping is intended to control this failure mode, but our experiments show
that its effect is regime dependent.  While raw full-sequence ESS remains
healthy, no-mask and CISPO-style updates preserve learning signal and improve
reasoning more rapidly than tightly clipped PPO/GRPO objectives.  After raw
normalized sequence ESS falls below approximately $0.1$, the ordering
reverses: aggressive updates become unstable, whereas a conservative masked
update remains reliable.  Starting with the low-distortion update and
activating masking only after this boundary produces the strongest final
result.

The statistical role of ESS follows from importance sampling.  POIS and the
broader importance-sampling literature show that the reliability of an
off-policy estimator depends jointly on a moment of its integrand and a
R\'enyi divergence between target and behavior distributions
\cite{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is,agapiou2017intrinsic,
chatterjee2018sample}.  For order two,
\begin{equation}
  d_2(P\|Q)
  =\E_Q\!\left[\left(\frac{dP}{dQ}\right)^2\right],
  \qquad
  \rho(P,Q)=\frac{1}{d_2(P\|Q)}.
  \label{eq:intro-renyi-ess}
\end{equation}
POIS uses this quantity to penalize an empirical off-policy performance
objective.  Our analysis begins from the same estimator--population
relationship, but focuses on the gradient supplied to a single minibatch
update in one-epoch RLVR.

The one-epoch structure makes this analysis direct.  Before minibatch $k$ is
used, the current parameters depend only on earlier, disjoint minibatches.
Conditional on this history, the current minibatch is still an independent
sample from $Q$.  Differentiating the finite full-sequence policy-improvement
estimator therefore gives a conditionally unbiased estimator of the current
population gradient.  Its exact conditional squared error is
\begin{equation}
  \E\!\left[\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2
  \mid\mathcal F_{k-1}\right]
  =\frac1m\left\{
    \E_Q[W_k^2\|H_k\|_2^2]-\|g_k\|_2^2
  \right\},
  \label{eq:intro-gradient-error}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the minibatch size and $H_k$ is the unweighted
per-response gradient contribution.  Writing
$\rho_k=1/\E_Q[W_k^2]$ factors the first term into inverse current ESS and a
squared-gradient moment under the $W_k^2$-tilted rollout distribution.  Thus
ESS controls the likelihood-ratio amplification in the estimator, but not the
complete error.  Two states can have the same ESS and very different gradient
reliability if their high-weight gradient contributions have different
magnitudes or directions.

This distinction is important for interpreting large gradients.  A large
coherent population gradient contributes to the signal term $\|g_k\|_2^2$ in
\eqref{eq:intro-gradient-error}; it is not, by itself, evidence of an unstable
update.  Low ESS is problematic when the uncertainty in the finite gradient
becomes large relative to the empirical ascent produced by the actual update.
Under local smoothness, if $d_k=\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k$ is the displacement
returned by the implemented optimizer and
$\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2\le \varepsilon_k$, then
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta_{k+1})-J(\theta_k)
  \ge
  \widehat g_k^\top d_k
  -\varepsilon_k\|d_k\|_2
  -\frac{L_k}{2}\|d_k\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:intro-one-step}
\end{equation}
The three terms respectively represent empirical ascent, the price of using a
finite gradient estimator, and local curvature.  The guarantee becomes
uninformative when gradient uncertainty dominates the update signal.

A second issue is terminology.  PPO/GRPO clipping is not the same operation as
truncating an importance ratio.  Away from the clipping boundary, the PPO
surrogate sets selected token-gradient contributions to zero; it is therefore
a sign-dependent gradient mask.  CISPO instead caps a detached token
coefficient while retaining every token gradient \cite{minimax2025cispo}.
Classical ratio truncation replaces the full importance ratio by a bounded
coefficient and yields a biased importance-sampling estimator
\cite{ionides2008truncated,metelli2020is}.  These operations have different
biases and should not share one theoretical argument.

\subsection{Related literature}

\paragraph{Importance sampling and policy optimization.}
POIS derives policy-performance bounds controlled by exponentiated R\'enyi
divergence and uses the resulting pessimistic objective for off-policy policy
optimization \cite{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  Truncated importance
sampling trades estimator bias for reduced sensitivity to extreme weights
\cite{ionides2008truncated}.  Our contribution is specific to the one-epoch
RLVR update: we exploit the conditional freshness of each unused minibatch to
relate current sequence ESS to the current gradient estimator and then to the
population improvement of the actual optimizer displacement.

\paragraph{Trust regions and clipping.}
TRPO controls a local policy surrogate through a divergence constraint
\cite{schulman2015trpo}, whereas PPO uses a clipped ratio surrogate
\cite{schulman2017ppo}.  The clipped surrogate is not a literal global
trust-region guarantee \cite{wang2020truly}; recent work instead studies direct
policy-distribution constraints or alternative clipping rules
\cite{qi2026dppo}.  CISPO caps detached token coefficients while preserving
gradients beyond the PPO boundary \cite{minimax2025cispo}.  These methods alter
the update in different ways.  Our theory distinguishes masking, coefficient
capping, and sequence-ratio truncation before comparing their empirical
behavior.

\paragraph{Sequence-level off-policy RL for language models.}
Recent methods use sequence-level weights, ESS, or second-moment diagnostics to
stabilize stale-policy training \cite{zheng2025m2po,shen2026vespo,
huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}.  Our setting is different: the rollout batch is
consumed in one epoch, so each current minibatch is conditionally fresh even
though it is off-policy for the learner.  This structure permits an exact
current-gradient reliability identity and isolates ESS as an online state
variable rather than a generic measure of training age.

\subsection{Contributions}

The paper makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item We derive the conditional finite-sample reliability of the current
full-sequence policy-gradient estimator in one-epoch RLVR and factor its second
moment into inverse current sequence ESS and a squared-weight tilted gradient
moment.
\item We translate a current-gradient confidence radius into a one-step
population-improvement lower bound for the actual optimizer displacement,
showing why large coherent gradients can be healthy and why low ESS matters
only through estimator uncertainty.
\item We distinguish PPO/GRPO gradient masking, CISPO token-coefficient capping,
and full-sequence ratio truncation, and derive separate bias--uncertainty
comparisons rather than treating them as one form of clipping.
\item We redesign the contextual-bandit and RLVR studies around the quantities
appearing in the theory: current ESS, gradient error, tilted gradient moment,
intervention bias, and fresh-rollout one-step improvement.
\end{enumerate}

\section{One-epoch RLVR and current sequence coverage}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Training filtration and fresh minibatches}

A rollout policy $Q$ generates a batch of independent training units.  In the
simplest case one unit is a prompt-response-reward triple
$Z=(X,Y,R)$.  The batch is partitioned before optimization into disjoint
minibatches
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal D
  =\mathcal B_1\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal B_K,
  \qquad
  \mathcal B_j\cap\mathcal B_k=\varnothing
  \quad (j\neq k),
  \label{eq:minibatch-partition}
\end{equation}
and every training unit is used exactly once.

Let $\mathcal F_{k-1}$ contain the initial learner, the rollout policy, the
optimizer state, and all minibatches and updates before minibatch $k$.  The
current parameter $\theta_k$ is $\mathcal F_{k-1}$-measurable.  Because
$\mathcal B_k$ is a disjoint subset of the original independent rollout
sample, it remains independent of $\mathcal F_{k-1}$.  Conditional on the
history, $\theta_k$ is therefore fixed while the observations in
$\mathcal B_k$ are fresh draws from $Q$.

This conditional freshness is the key statistical property of one-epoch
training.  It differs from multi-epoch PPO, in which an iterate may already
have been optimized using the same observations on which its next gradient is
computed.

\begin{remark}[Prompt groups]
If several responses share a group-relative advantage, the independent
training unit is the prompt group rather than an individual response.  The
same argument applies provided all responses sharing the group statistic are
kept together when minibatches are formed.  We return to this point in
\Cref{app:groups}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Population policy improvement and its finite estimator}

Let $P_\theta(\cdot\mid X)$ denote the learner and assume
$P_\theta\ll Q$.  Define the full-response likelihood ratio
\begin{equation}
  W_\theta(X,Y)
  :=\frac{P_\theta(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)}.
  \label{eq:sequence-ratio}
\end{equation}
For a prompt-only detached baseline $b(X)$, let
\begin{equation}
  A(X,Y):=R(X,Y)-b(X).
  \label{eq:advantage}
\end{equation}
Since $\E_Q[W_\theta\mid X]=1$,
\begin{equation}
  J(P_\theta)-J(Q)
  =\E_Q[(W_\theta-1)A].
  \label{eq:population-improvement}
\end{equation}
The corresponding finite estimator on a minibatch of size $m$ is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\Delta_k(\theta)
  :=\frac1m\sum_{Z_i\in\mathcal B_k}
  (W_\theta(Z_i)-1)A(Z_i).
  \label{eq:finite-improvement}
\end{equation}
At the current policy $\theta_k$, differentiating
\eqref{eq:finite-improvement} gives the full-sequence gradient estimator.

Define the full-response score and unweighted gradient contribution by
\begin{equation}
  S_\theta(X,Y)
  :=\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y\mid X),
  \qquad
  H_\theta(X,Y):=A(X,Y)S_\theta(X,Y).
  \label{eq:score-contribution}
\end{equation}
Because
$\nabla_\theta W_\theta=W_\theta S_\theta$, the population and empirical
gradients at the current iterate are
\begin{align}
  g_k
  &:=\nabla_\theta J(P_\theta)\big|_{\theta=\theta_k}
  =\E_Q[W_kH_k],
  \label{eq:population-gradient}\\
  \widehat g_k
  &:=\nabla_\theta\widehat\Delta_k(\theta)
  \big|_{\theta=\theta_k}
  =\frac1m\sum_{Z_i\in\mathcal B_k}W_{k,i}H_{k,i},
  \label{eq:empirical-gradient}
\end{align}
where $W_k:=W_{\theta_k}$ and $H_k:=H_{\theta_k}$.

Conditional freshness immediately yields
\begin{equation}
  \E[\widehat g_k\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]=g_k.
  \label{eq:conditional-unbiased}
\end{equation}
Thus the current unused minibatch provides an unbiased estimate of the
population full-sequence gradient even though $P_{\theta_k}$ may differ
substantially from $Q$.

\subsection{Current sequence ESS}

Define normalized population sequence ESS at the current iterate by
\begin{equation}
  \rho_k
  :=\frac{1}{\E_Q[W_k^2]}
  =\exp\{-D_2(P_{\theta_k}\|Q)\}.
  \label{eq:population-ess}
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
  \E_Q[(W_k-1)^2]
  =\rho_k^{-1}-1
  =\chi^2(P_{\theta_k}\|Q).
  \label{eq:chi-ess}
\end{equation}
This is the same second-moment quantity that governs importance-sampling
uncertainty in POIS \cite{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  Here it is evaluated
at the current learner before the unused minibatch is consumed.

The practical controller observes the raw sample statistic
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho_k
  =\frac{\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal B_k}W_{k,i}\right)^2}
  {m\sum_{i\in\mathcal B_k}W_{k,i}^2}.
  \label{eq:sample-ess}
\end{equation}
The theorem below uses population $\rho_k$; the sample quantity is an online
diagnostic whose finite-sample limitations are discussed in
\Cref{sec:gate}.

\section{Current gradient reliability}
\label{sec:reliability}

\subsection{Exact conditional error}

ESS alone cannot determine gradient quality: scaling all gradient
contributions leaves $W_k$ and hence ESS unchanged.  The relevant second
moment is the joint quantity $\E_Q[W_k^2\|H_k\|_2^2]$.  The role of ESS becomes
transparent after tilting the rollout law by squared importance weights.

Define
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dQ_k^{(2)}}{dQ}(Z)
  :=\frac{W_k(Z)^2}{\E_Q[W_k^2]}
  =\rho_k W_k(Z)^2,
  \label{eq:tilted-law}
\end{equation}
and let
\begin{equation}
  M_{2,k}
  :=\E_{Q_k^{(2)}}[\|H_k\|_2^2].
  \label{eq:tilted-moment}
\end{equation}
The quantity $M_{2,k}$ is the squared gradient-contribution moment in the
portion of the rollout distribution emphasized by the second moment of the
importance weights.

\begin{theorem}[Current-gradient reliability]
\label{thm:current-gradient}
Assume $\E_Q[W_k^2\|H_k\|_2^2]<\infty$.  Conditional on
$\mathcal F_{k-1}$,
\begin{equation}
  \E\!\left[
  \|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2
  \mid\mathcal F_{k-1}
  \right]
  =\frac1m\left\{
    \frac{M_{2,k}}{\rho_k}-\|g_k\|_2^2
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:exact-gradient-error}
\end{equation}
Consequently, for any $\delta\in(0,1)$, with conditional probability at least
$1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}
  \|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2
  \le
  \varepsilon_k(\delta)
  :=\sqrt{
  \frac{M_{2,k}/\rho_k-\|g_k\|_2^2}
  {m\delta}}.
  \label{eq:gradient-radius}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Conditional on $\mathcal F_{k-1}$, the $m$ elements of $\mathcal B_k$ are
independent with common mean $g_k$ by \eqref{eq:conditional-unbiased}.  Hence
\begin{align}
  \E\!\left[\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2
  \mid\mathcal F_{k-1}\right]
  &=\frac1m\operatorname{tr}
  \Cov_Q(W_kH_k)\\
  &=\frac1m\left\{
    \E_Q[W_k^2\|H_k\|_2^2]-\|g_k\|_2^2
  \right\}.
\end{align}
By \eqref{eq:tilted-law}--\eqref{eq:tilted-moment},
$\E_Q[W_k^2\|H_k\|_2^2]=M_{2,k}/\rho_k$, giving
\eqref{eq:exact-gradient-error}.  Markov's inequality applied to the
nonnegative random variable $\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2$ yields
\eqref{eq:gradient-radius}.
\end{proof}

The theorem identifies current ESS as one factor in the finite-gradient
reliability problem.  Holding $M_{2,k}$ and the coherent signal $\|g_k\|_2$
fixed, decreasing $\rho_k$ increases the uncertainty radius.  The statement is
necessarily conditional: ESS alone cannot control the gradient error because
it contains no information about the magnitude or orientation of $H_k$.

\begin{proposition}[ESS alone is insufficient]
\label{prop:ess-insufficient}
There is no finite bound on
$\E\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2$ that depends only on $m$ and $\rho_k$ over an
unrestricted class of gradient integrands.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix any nondegenerate importance-weight law $W_k$ and any gradient integrand
$H_k$ with nonzero estimation variance.  Replacing $H_k$ by $aH_k$ for
$a>0$ leaves $W_k$ and $\rho_k$ unchanged, while both the gradient covariance
and the mean-squared estimation error are multiplied by $a^2$.  Letting
$a\to\infty$ proves the claim.
\end{proof}

This observation is important empirically.  Low ESS indicates weak
second-moment coverage, not necessarily a harmful update.  If the high-weight
responses carry a large, coherent gradient, $\|g_k\|_2^2$ is also large and
the update may remain well determined.  The contextual-bandit study in
\Cref{sec:simulation} is designed to separate these cases.

\subsection{From gradient reliability to one-step population improvement}

The preceding result concerns estimation.  Its optimization consequence is
obtained without changing the optimizer.  Let
\begin{equation}
  d_k:=\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k
  \label{eq:actual-step}
\end{equation}
be the actual displacement returned after processing minibatch $k$, including
any optimizer state or learning-rate schedule.

\begin{assumption}[Local population smoothness]
\label{ass:smoothness}
For the realized displacement $d_k$, the population objective satisfies
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta_k+d_k)
  \ge
  J(\theta_k)+g_k^\top d_k
  -\frac{L_k}{2}\|d_k\|_2^2
  \label{eq:smoothness}
\end{equation}
for some local constant $L_k<\infty$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{theorem}[One-step population lower bound]
\label{thm:one-step}
Under \Cref{ass:smoothness}, on any event for which
$\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2\le\varepsilon_k$,
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta_{k+1})-J(\theta_k)
  \ge
  \widehat g_k^\top d_k
  -\varepsilon_k\|d_k\|_2
  -\frac{L_k}{2}\|d_k\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:one-step-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Starting from \eqref{eq:smoothness}, add and subtract the empirical gradient:
\begin{align}
  J(\theta_{k+1})-J(\theta_k)
  &\ge
  \widehat g_k^\top d_k
  +(g_k-\widehat g_k)^\top d_k
  -\frac{L_k}{2}\|d_k\|_2^2\\
  &\ge
  \widehat g_k^\top d_k
  -\|g_k-\widehat g_k\|_2\|d_k\|_2
  -\frac{L_k}{2}\|d_k\|_2^2,
\end{align}
where the second line is Cauchy--Schwarz.  Substituting the event bound proves
\eqref{eq:one-step-bound}.
\end{proof}

Combining \Cref{thm:current-gradient,thm:one-step} gives, with conditional
probability at least $1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta_{k+1})-J(\theta_k)
  \ge
  \widehat g_k^\top d_k
  -\|d_k\|_2
  \sqrt{\frac{M_{2,k}/\rho_k-\|g_k\|_2^2}{m\delta}}
  -\frac{L_k}{2}\|d_k\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:ess-one-step-bound}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:ess-one-step-bound} is the central theoretical statement.
It separates three quantities with different meanings:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item $\widehat g_k^\top d_k$ is the ascent indicated by the current finite
minibatch gradient along the actual optimizer displacement;
\item the middle term is the statistical price of replacing the population
gradient by the finite current-minibatch estimator;
\item the final term is the local curvature cost of a finite parameter move.
\end{enumerate}
A large gradient or a large update is therefore not intrinsically unstable.
The guarantee fails when estimation uncertainty and curvature dominate the
empirical ascent.

\begin{corollary}[Plain gradient-ascent specialization]
If $d_k=\eta_k\widehat g_k$, then on the same event
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta_{k+1})-J(\theta_k)
  \ge
  \eta_k\|\widehat g_k\|_2
  \left[
  \left(1-\frac{L_k\eta_k}{2}\right)
  \|\widehat g_k\|_2-
  \varepsilon_k
  \right].
  \label{eq:plain-step}
\end{equation}
Thus a sufficient condition for a positive lower bound is
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon_k
  <
  \left(1-\frac{L_k\eta_k}{2}\right)
  \|\widehat g_k\|_2.
  \label{eq:healthy-condition}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

The corollary is an interpretation for ordinary gradient ascent; the main
bound \eqref{eq:one-step-bound} is written for the realized optimizer
step and does not identify Adam or another optimizer with plain SGD.

\section{Three distinct interventions}
\label{sec:interventions}

The word \emph{clipping} is used for several mathematically different
operations in RLVR.  Conflating them obscures the mechanism.  We distinguish
gradient masking, coefficient capping, and sequence-ratio truncation.

\subsection{PPO/GRPO clipping is gradient masking}

Consider the usual token-level clipped surrogate.  Let
\begin{equation}
  r_t(\theta)
  :=\frac{p_\theta(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t})}
  {q(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t})}.
  \label{eq:token-ratio}
\end{equation}
For advantage $A$, the PPO contribution is
\begin{equation}
  \ell_t^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
  =\min\left\{
  r_t(\theta)A,
  \clip(r_t(\theta),1-\epsilon_-,1+\epsilon_+)A
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:ppo-objective}
\end{equation}
Away from the clipping boundaries, differentiation gives
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_\theta\ell_t^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
  =M_t(\theta,A)\,
  r_t(\theta)A\,
  \nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t}),
  \label{eq:ppo-gradient-mask}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  M_t(\theta,A)
  =1
  -\1\{A>0,\ r_t>1+\epsilon_+\}
  -\1\{A<0,\ r_t<1-\epsilon_-\}.
  \label{eq:mask}
\end{equation}
Thus PPO clipping does not replace the full sequence ratio by a truncated
importance weight.  It removes selected token-gradient contributions according
to the advantage sign and local ratio.

Let $G_k^{\mathrm{free}}(Z)$ denote the unmasked per-sequence aggregate under
the corresponding token-local surrogate and
$G_k^{\mathrm{mask}}(Z)$ the aggregate after \eqref{eq:mask}.  The population
masking bias relative to the unmasked surrogate gradient is
\begin{equation}
  b_k^{\mathrm{mask}}
  :=\E_Q[G_k^{\mathrm{mask}}-G_k^{\mathrm{free}}].
  \label{eq:mask-bias}
\end{equation}
Masking can reduce covariance by eliminating extreme contributions, but it can
also remove coherent signal.  Its benefit therefore depends on both the
masked covariance and the vector bias \eqref{eq:mask-bias}; no theorem about
full-sequence ratio truncation automatically applies.

\subsection{CISPO caps token coefficients without masking gradients}

A CISPO-style update instead caps a detached token coefficient while retaining
the score gradient.  Abstractly,
\begin{equation}
  G_k^{\mathrm{CISPO}}(Z)
  =\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T
  \sg\{c_{\mathrm{tok}}(r_{k,t})\}\,
  A(Z)\,
  \nabla_\theta\log p_{\theta_k}(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t}),
  \label{eq:cispo-gradient}
\end{equation}
where $c_{\mathrm{tok}}$ is the implemented coefficient cap.  Unlike
\eqref{eq:ppo-gradient-mask}, no token is removed solely because its ratio
crosses the PPO boundary.  The induced bias is
\begin{equation}
  b_k^{\mathrm{CISPO}}
  :=\E_Q[G_k^{\mathrm{CISPO}}-G_k^{\mathrm{free}}].
  \label{eq:cispo-bias}
\end{equation}
This is a token-surrogate bias, not the bias of a truncated full-sequence
importance sampler.

\subsection{Full-sequence ratio truncation is a separate estimator}

Classical truncated importance sampling replaces the full response weight by
\begin{equation}
  U_c(W_k):=\min\{W_k,c\}.
  \label{eq:sequence-truncation}
\end{equation}
The corresponding sequence-gradient estimator is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_k^{\mathrm{seqtr}}
  =\frac1m\sum_{Z_i\in\mathcal B_k}
  U_c(W_{k,i})H_{k,i}.
  \label{eq:sequence-trunc-grad}
\end{equation}
Its bias relative to the exact population sequence gradient is
\begin{equation}
  b_k^{\mathrm{seqtr}}
  =\E_Q[(U_c(W_k)-W_k)H_k]
  =-\E_Q[(W_k-c)_+H_k].
  \label{eq:sequence-trunc-bias}
\end{equation}
This is the classical bias--variance intervention studied in truncated
importance sampling \cite{ionides2008truncated,metelli2020is}.

\subsection{A common reliability comparison, but different biases}

For any practical per-sequence gradient contribution $G_k^a(Z)$ associated
with intervention $a$, define its population mean and covariance by
\begin{equation}
  \mu_k^a:=\E_Q[G_k^a],
  \qquad
  \Sigma_k^a:=\Cov_Q(G_k^a),
  \label{eq:intervention-moments}
\end{equation}
and bias relative to the target population gradient $g_k$ by
\begin{equation}
  b_k^a:=\mu_k^a-g_k.
  \label{eq:intervention-bias}
\end{equation}
For an unused minibatch of size $m$,
\begin{equation}
  \E\!\left[
  \|\widehat g_k^a-g_k\|_2^2
  \mid\mathcal F_{k-1}
  \right]
  =\|b_k^a\|_2^2
  +\frac1m\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_k^a).
  \label{eq:bias-variance-general}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:bias-variance-general} provides a common comparison without
pretending that the interventions are identical.  Masking may have lower
variance but large directional bias; CISPO capping may preserve more signal;
sequence truncation has the explicit tail bias in
\eqref{eq:sequence-trunc-bias}.  Their ranking can therefore change with the
current policy state.

A high-probability radius follows from
\eqref{eq:bias-variance-general}.  For example, with conditional probability at
least $1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}
  \|\widehat g_k^a-g_k\|_2
  \le
  \|b_k^a\|_2
  +\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_k^a)}{m\delta}}.
  \label{eq:intervention-radius}
\end{equation}
Substituting this radius into \eqref{eq:one-step-bound} compares interventions
through the same population-improvement criterion while preserving their
different bias mechanisms.

\section{From population ESS to an online gate}
\label{sec:gate}

The theory uses population $\rho_k$, whereas training observes only a finite
minibatch.  We compute all diagnostics from the raw, pre-intervention
full-sequence ratios.  Post-masking, post-capping, or post-truncation ESS is not
used to determine the regime because modifying the update can mechanically
make its coefficients appear more uniform.

For the current minibatch, define
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho_k
  =\frac{\left(\sum_iW_{k,i}\right)^2}
  {m\sum_iW_{k,i}^2},
  \qquad
  \overline W_k=\frac1m\sum_iW_{k,i},
  \qquad
  m_{\mathrm{eff},k}=m\widehat\rho_k.
  \label{eq:gate-diagnostics}
\end{equation}
The three statistics serve different purposes.  $\widehat\rho_k$ measures
observed concentration, $m_{\mathrm{eff},k}$ records the effective sequence
count, and $\overline W_k$ checks the population identity $\E_QW_k=1$.
Because normalized ESS is scale invariant, a minibatch in which all observed
weights are tiny can have high $\widehat\rho_k$ while missing important target
mass.

The practical healthy-state indicator is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Healthy}_k
  =\1\left\{
  \widehat\rho_k\ge\tau_\rho,\quad
  m\widehat\rho_k\ge n_{\min},\quad
  |\overline W_k-1|\le\eta_W
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:health-rule}
\end{equation}
The main empirical setting uses $\tau_\rho=0.1$, selected on
[DEVELOPMENT SETTING].  The theorem does not make this value universal.  The
threshold is an empirical proxy for the point at which the raw update's
reliability advantage changes relative to the available conservative branch.

Each rollout batch begins in the gradient-preserving mode.  Once the health
rule fails, the conservative mode is latched for the remainder of that rollout
batch.  Fresh rollouts reset the mode.  Latching prevents a conservative
intervention from raising a post-intervention diagnostic and immediately
switching the controller back.

\section{Contextual-bandit study}
\label{sec:simulation}

The controlled study is designed to test the theoretical mechanism rather
than merely reproduce an RLVR learning curve.  In particular, it must separate
ratio concentration from the magnitude and coherence of the corresponding
gradient contributions.

\subsection{Finite model and one-epoch protocol}

Let $x\in\{1,\ldots,K_x\}$ and
$a\in\{1,\ldots,K_a\}$.  The rollout and learner policies are categorical,
\begin{equation}
  Q(a\mid x)=\operatorname{softmax}(\phi_x)_a,
  \qquad
  P_\theta(a\mid x)=\operatorname{softmax}(\theta_x)_a,
  \label{eq:bandit-policies}
\end{equation}
and the verifier reward $R(x,a)\in[0,1]$ is specified exactly.  Since the
state-action space is finite, the population reward, population gradient,
sequence-ratio moments, and intervention biases can all be enumerated without
Monte Carlo approximation.

At the start of an outer iteration, draw a batch from $Q$ and partition it into
disjoint minibatches.  Process each minibatch once, update $\theta$, and never
reuse an observation.  Before each minibatch $k$, compute the exact population
quantities
\begin{equation}
  \rho_k,\qquad
  M_{2,k},\qquad
  g_k,\qquad
  \operatorname{tr}\Cov_Q(W_kH_k),
  \label{eq:bandit-oracles}
\end{equation}
and compare them with their minibatch diagnostics.

\subsection{Experiment 1: ESS is informative but not sufficient}

Construct two verifier geometries using the same pair $(Q,P_{\theta_k})$ and
therefore exactly the same distribution of likelihood ratios and the same
$\rho_k$.

\paragraph{Coherent-tail verifier.}
High-weight actions receive gradient contributions aligned with the population
gradient.  This construction produces a large $\|g_k\|_2$ and can retain a
small relative gradient error despite low ESS.

\paragraph{Heterogeneous-tail verifier.}
The same high-weight actions receive gradient contributions with conflicting
magnitudes or directions.  The ratio law and ESS are unchanged, while
$M_{2,k}$ and the covariance of $W_kH_k$ increase relative to the coherent
signal.

For each construction and each prescribed ESS level, draw many independent
unused minibatches and estimate
\begin{equation}
  \E\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2,
  \qquad
  \frac{\E\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2}{\|g_k\|_2^2},
  \qquad
  \Pp\!\left\{
  J(\theta_k+d_k)<J(\theta_k)
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:bandit-reliability-metrics}
\end{equation}
This experiment directly tests \Cref{prop:ess-insufficient}: identical ESS can
coexist with different gradient reliability.  It also tests the empirical
observation that a large coherent gradient can be healthy.

\subsection{Experiment 2: verify the current-gradient identity}

Across a grid of current policies, minibatch sizes, and ESS levels, compare the
Monte Carlo squared error to the exact quantity
\begin{equation}
  \frac1m\left(
  \frac{M_{2,k}}{\rho_k}-\|g_k\|_2^2
  \right).
  \label{eq:verify-identity}
\end{equation}
The two must agree up to simulation error.  This experiment validates the
statistical identity underlying the gate before any clipping method is
introduced.

\subsection{Experiment 3: masking versus capping versus truncation}

Freeze the same current policy and repeatedly redraw unused minibatches.
Compare the following estimators:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item the exact free full-sequence estimator $W_kH_k$;
\item the token-local unmasked surrogate used by the practical aggressive
branch;
\item PPO/GRPO gradient masking via \eqref{eq:mask};
\item CISPO-style token-coefficient capping via
\eqref{eq:cispo-gradient};
\item full-sequence ratio truncation via
\eqref{eq:sequence-truncation}.
\end{enumerate}
For every estimator, enumerate or estimate
\begin{equation}
  \|b_k^a\|_2^2,
  \qquad
  \frac1m\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_k^a),
  \qquad
  \E\|\widehat g_k^a-g_k\|_2^2,
  \qquad
  J(\theta_k+d_k^a)-J(\theta_k).
  \label{eq:intervention-study-metrics}
\end{equation}
The study must additionally report three different intervention frequencies:
\begin{equation}
  \text{mask rate},\qquad
  \text{token-cap saturation rate},\qquad
  \E[(W_k-c)_+].
  \label{eq:three-rates}
\end{equation}
They should not be collapsed into a generic ``clipping fraction.''

\paragraph{Contextual-bandit limitation.}
In a one-step bandit, the action ratio is also the complete-trajectory ratio.
Consequently, a CISPO-style coefficient cap and full-sequence ratio truncation
can coincide if they use the same numerical cap.  The bandit study can
separate gradient masking from coefficient truncation and can verify the
bias--variance accounting, but it cannot reproduce the token-versus-sequence
distinction of a long autoregressive response.  That distinction is evaluated
in the RLVR experiments.

\subsection{Experiment 4: threshold transfer}

Select $\tau_\rho$ once on a development construction.  Freeze it before
changing verifier geometry, minibatch size, or policy parameterization.  Test
whether the ranking reversal between the gradient-preserving and masking
branches continues to occur near the same current-ESS region.  Also report
$m\widehat\rho_k$ to determine whether the transition is better explained by
a normalized coverage threshold, an effective-count threshold, or both.

\section{RLVR experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

The language-model experiments test whether current sequence ESS predicts the
same reliability transition when the true gradient is unavailable.

\subsection{Training protocol}

For each rollout batch, record the exact partition into disjoint minibatches
and verify that no response is reused during the optimization epoch.  Before
processing minibatch $k$, compute the raw full-response ratios between the
current learner and the rollout policy, together with
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho_k,\qquad
  m\widehat\rho_k,\qquad
  \overline W_k,\qquad
  \frac1m\sum_i W_{k,i}^2\|G_{k,i}\|_2^2,
  \label{eq:rlvr-diagnostics}
\end{equation}
where $G_{k,i}$ denotes the implemented per-sequence gradient aggregate when
it is computationally feasible to record it.  ESS is always computed before
masking, capping, or sequence truncation.

\subsection{Matched one-minibatch forks}

The primary causal experiment forks training immediately before an unused
minibatch.  The policy parameters, optimizer state, rollout batch, and current
minibatch are identical across branches.  Apply exactly one update using:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item the unmasked or no-clip practical objective;
\item CISPO-style coefficient capping;
\item PPO/GRPO gradient masking;
\item full-sequence ratio truncation, when implemented as a diagnostic
baseline.
\end{enumerate}
The minibatch is then discarded in every fork.  Evaluate each post-update
policy using fresh rollouts.  Stratify the resulting one-step reward changes
by the \emph{pre-update current} raw sequence ESS.

The central prediction is a ranking reversal rather than monotone harm from
large gradients.  At high current ESS, the gradient-preserving branches should
retain more useful ascent.  At low current ESS, the masking branch should gain
from lower estimator variability strongly enough to offset its masking bias.

\subsection{Repeated-sampling gradient reliability}

At selected frozen policies spanning current ESS values above and below the
gate, draw repeated fresh minibatches from the same rollout policy.  Their
average provides a high-precision reference for the current gradient of each
implemented surrogate.  Measure
\begin{equation}
  \|\widehat g_k^a-g_{k,\mathrm{ref}}\|_2,
  \qquad
  \|b_k^a\|_2,
  \qquad
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_k^a),
  \qquad
  (\widehat g_k^a)^\top d_k^a,
  \label{eq:rlvr-gradient-study}
\end{equation}
using projected or layerwise summaries if full parameter-vector storage is
prohibitive.  This experiment tests whether the empirical $0.1$ threshold is
indeed associated with a change in gradient reliability rather than merely a
change in ratio statistics.

\subsection{Intervention diagnostics}

Report separately:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item the fraction of token gradients masked by PPO/GRPO;
\item the fraction of CISPO token coefficients at the cap;
\item the raw full-sequence ESS and effective sequence count;
\item the fraction or mass of sequences affected by full-sequence truncation;
\item fresh-rollout reward change after the matched update.
\end{enumerate}
This separation is necessary to determine which intervention removes variance
and which removes useful gradient signal.

\subsection{End-to-end ESS gate}

Finally compare always-unmasked, always-CISPO, always-masked, and the latched
ESS-gated controller under matched rollout and optimization budgets.  Freeze
$\tau_\rho=0.1$ using [DEVELOPMENT SETTING].  Report final reward, area under
the learning curve, collapse frequency, fraction of minibatches spent in each
mode, and the distribution of current ESS at switching times.  The end-to-end
experiment establishes whether the one-step reliability transition compounds
into better training performance.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{ESS is one factor, not a complete reliability statistic.}
The exact gradient error contains both $\rho_k$ and $M_{2,k}$.  The success of
a scalar ESS gate therefore requires the relationship between ratio
concentration and gradient geometry to be sufficiently stable in the training
regime.  This is an empirical regularity under investigation, not a universal
theorem based on ESS alone.

\paragraph{Population and sample ESS.}
The theory uses $\rho_k=1/\E_Q[W_k^2]$, whereas the controller uses the
minibatch plug-in statistic.  A finite minibatch can miss rare target mass.
Mean-ratio and effective-count diagnostics expose some failures but do not
convert the plug-in statistic into an exact confidence bound.

\paragraph{Data-dependent branch selection.}
Conditional unbiasedness holds for the current raw gradient because the
minibatch has not previously been used.  The practical gate selects a branch
using the same minibatch ESS statistic; the branch-specific lower bounds are
therefore mechanistic diagnostics rather than a formal simultaneous
certificate for the selected update.  A strictly selection-valid version can
use a small independent gate minibatch or a simultaneous two-branch bound.

\paragraph{Full-sequence theory and token-local practice.}
Current sequence ESS enters the exact full-sequence gradient identity.  PPO
masking and CISPO capping optimize token-local surrogates.  Their relation to
raw sequence ESS is established by the matched-minibatch and repeated-sampling
experiments, not by equating their coefficients with the full-response ratio.

\paragraph{Sampler correctness and support.}
$Q$ must be the actual distribution that generated the response, including
temperature, top-$p$/top-$k$, EOS handling, rejection filters, numerical
precision, and MoE routing.  If the learner places mass outside the support of
$Q$, the exact likelihood ratio is unavailable.

\paragraph{Threshold universality.}
The value $0.1$ depends on minibatch size, model, reward geometry, optimizer,
and the intervention.  Transfer of a frozen threshold is an empirical result,
not a consequence of the second-moment identity alone.

\section{Conclusion}

In one-epoch RLVR, later minibatches are off-policy but remain conditionally
fresh because every response is used once.  This structure permits an exact
analysis of the gradient estimator at the current policy.  Current sequence
ESS factors the likelihood-ratio contribution to its second moment, while a
squared-weight tilted gradient moment records the magnitude and coherence of
the associated gradient contributions.  Large gradients are therefore not
intrinsically unstable; instability arises when finite-gradient uncertainty
overwhelms the empirical ascent of the actual update.

The resulting view of clipping is operational.  PPO/GRPO clipping is a
gradient mask, CISPO is a token-coefficient cap, and full-sequence truncation
is a distinct robust importance-sampling estimator.  A conservative branch is
useful only when its reduction in sampling uncertainty compensates for the
bias and learning signal it removes.  Raw current sequence ESS is a practical
state variable for this trade-off, and the observed boundary near $0.1$ is the
empirical point at which the preferred update rule changes in the studied
training regime.

\section{General MDPs and per-decision importance sampling}
\label{app:mdp}

For a discounted MDP, the performance-difference lemma is
\begin{equation}
  J(\pi')-J(\pi)
  =\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
  \E_{s\sim d_{\pi'}}
  \E_{a\sim\pi'(\cdot\mid s)}[A_\pi(s,a)].
  \label{eq:mdp-pdl}
\end{equation}
TRPO replaces $d_{\pi'}$ by $d_\pi$ in a tractable surrogate and controls the
resulting occupancy residual.  This occupancy argument underlies TRPO
\cite{schulman2015trpo}.  It is distinct from the finite-sample reliability of
importance weighting.

If one changes measure on the complete trajectory distribution, the identity
is exact but the trajectory ratio inherits the horizon problem.  Per-decision
importance sampling can reduce variance for rewards observed before the end of
a trajectory \cite{precup2000eligibility,metelli2020is}.  In terminal-reward
RLVR, direct correction of the terminal reward uses the full-response ratio.

\section{Prompt groups and group-relative advantages}
\label{app:groups}

If several responses are sampled for one prompt and their advantages share a
group mean or standard deviation, the responses are statistically coupled.
The exact main-text derivation applies directly to raw verifier rewards or to a
prompt-only baseline fixed independently of the response.  For a grouped
training surrogate, the independent unit is the prompt cluster, not each token
and not necessarily each response.

Let $G_{jg}$ denote the full per-response gradient aggregate for response $g$
of prompt $j$, and define the cluster contribution
\begin{equation}
  C_j:=\frac1G\sum_{g=1}^{G}G_{jg}.
  \label{eq:cluster-gradient}
\end{equation}
Covariance and concentration should be computed across independent prompt
clusters $C_1,\ldots,C_M$.  Increasing group size can reduce conditional
variance within a prompt but does not create $MG$ independent prompts.
