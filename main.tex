\title{\bf When Does Clipping Help?\\
Effective Sample Size and Policy-Gradient Reliability}
\author{Dongkyu Derek Cho, Thomas Voice}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Clipping is widely treated as a safeguard for stable policy optimization.  Yet
permissive updates can learn faster and reach higher reward before sometimes
collapsing late in training.  Existing methods respond by changing clipping
boundaries or constraints, but this pattern lacks a common statistical
explanation.  We identify the effective support of the rollout data, measured
by effective sample size (ESS), as the missing link.  We prove that effective
support controls the gradient error of a permissive update, connect that error
to finite-time convergence, and characterize when clipping's variance reduction
repays its bias.  This analysis shows that the absence of clipping is not
inherently unstable: permissive gradients are reliable while support is broad,
whereas clipping can become useful after support concentrates.  An exactly
evaluable contextual bandit verifies the predicted connection among ESS,
gradient error, policy improvement, and the clipped-versus-permissive crossover.
A deliberately simple ESS-triggered intervention then tests this implication in
language-model training, where it prevents late collapse and improves final
scores across several safeguards.  The intervention is an operational check of
the theory, not the paper's primary contribution.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Policy optimization on fixed rollout data is commonly stabilized by clipping.
The updated policy no longer matches the policy that generated the data, so PPO
suppresses selected samples after their likelihood ratios cross a prescribed
boundary \citep{schulman2017ppo}.  RLVR inherits this mechanism because an
expensive rollout batch is typically followed by several policy updates
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}.  This standard account treats clipping as the
protection needed when policy mismatch grows.

The empirical record is more complicated.  Clipping can discard gradients from
low-probability tokens that express useful new behavior, which can slow early
learning and alter entropy dynamics \citep{yu2025dapo,park2025clipping}.  More
permissive objectives can therefore learn faster and sometimes reach higher
reward, but the same runs may collapse later.  Figure~\ref{fig:llm-motivation}
shows this pattern for two truncated-importance-sampling (TIS) updates: both
improve rapidly without PPO clipping and fail only after sequence ESS approaches
zero.  The absence of clipping is therefore not itself the instability.

  \caption{Empirical motivation in Qwen3-30B-A3B RLVR.  With TIS caps of 3
  and 5, AIME-2024 mean@16 initially improves without PPO clipping but later
  collapses as normalized sequence ESS vanishes.  The dotted line marks the
  prespecified $0.1$ threshold studied below.  Each panel shows the available
  training trajectory; multi-seed uncertainty is not shown.}
  \label{fig:llm-motivation}
\end{figure}

Existing approaches mainly change the intervention rather than explain this
change of regime.  A clipping threshold specifies how to alter an individual
sample, an update count measures time spent on a rollout, and a divergence
summarizes policy movement \citep{qi2026dppo}.  None directly answers whether
the fixed rollout still supports an accurate gradient for the current policy.
Without that reliability variable, clipped and permissive objectives remain a
collection of useful but objective-specific heuristics.

This paper identifies sequence effective sample size (ESS) as the missing
reliability variable.  ESS is high when importance weight is distributed across
many responses and low when a few responses dominate the update.  Our central
claim is that a permissive gradient remains statistically reliable while this
effective support is broad.  As support concentrates, its gradient error grows,
and clipping can become preferable when the variance it removes exceeds the
bias it introduces.  This view explains both sides of the empirical pattern:
unclipped optimization can be efficient before it becomes unreliable.

We establish this explanation through one theoretical chain.  First, we derive
the exact mean-squared error of the permissive gradient in terms of ESS and a
weighted gradient scale.  Second, we show that this error controls one-step
policy improvement and finite-time convergence.  Third, we differentiate the
PPO surrogate and show that clipping is an advantage-sign gradient mask, not
global ratio truncation.  The resulting bias--variance condition states exactly
when the clipped estimator has lower gradient error.  ESS is therefore not a
new objective; it is the bridge that connects rollout coverage to the changing
role of clipping.

An exactly evaluable contextual bandit tests each link without relying on an
LLM-scale proxy.  Its RLVR-style rollout loop preserves sequence ratios and
group-relative advantages, while its finite population exposes the true
gradient and reward.  Across 100 paired runs, lower ESS predicts larger
permissive-gradient error and harmful policy steps.  Independent redraws at
frozen policies then recover the predicted crossover under a fixed diagnostic
construction: clipping has lower MSE when effective support is scarce, whereas
the permissive estimator is favored when support is broad.  These results
validate the explanation rather than benchmark a new bandit algorithm.

The theory also has a simple operational implication.  If clipping is useful
only after effective support deteriorates, a conservative ESS threshold should
preserve permissive learning in the reliable regime and activate protection in
the unreliable regime.  We use this minimal intervention as a falsifiable test,
not as the paper's main innovation.  In the controlled study it selects a sparse
set of high-error updates and improves lower-tail optimization.  In
Qwen3-30B-A3B RLVR, the same prespecified trigger improves the reported final
AIME-2024 score under GRPO, Clip-Higher, DPPO, and TIS comparisons, and prevents
the late TIS collapse shown in Figure~\ref{fig:llm-motivation}.  These
trajectory-level results show that the theoretical regime change remains useful
when the true gradient is no longer observable.

\subsection{Related perspectives}

\paragraph{Clipping in RLVR.}
PPO introduced clipping as a local policy-update mechanism
\citep{schulman2017ppo}, and GRPO carried this surrogate into RLVR
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}.  Recent methods expose the cost of applying the
same constraint throughout training.  DAPO relaxes the upper boundary,
Clip-Low changes entropy behavior, CISPO retains gradients beyond the PPO
boundary, and DPPO replaces ratio clipping with a divergence-based constraint
\citep{yu2025dapo,park2025clipping,minimax2025m1,qi2026dppo}.  These methods
change the intervention.  Our analysis explains the common regime change that
determines when any such intervention is statistically warranted.

\paragraph{Reliability under policy mismatch.}
Importance-sampling analyses use second moments and ESS to quantify coverage
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  Recent LLM methods likewise adapt to
weight concentration, ESS, or sequence-level mismatch
\citep{zheng2025m2po,shen2026vespo,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}.  Our analysis
provides the missing theoretical link: sequence ESS controls gradient MSE, and
that MSE determines both finite-time convergence and the clipping crossover.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item We identify effective support as the missing link between policy mismatch
and update reliability, and derive its exact connection to the mean-squared
error of a fixed-rollout policy-gradient estimator.
\item We connect gradient error to finite-time convergence and derive the exact
bias--variance condition that determines when clipping helps, while separating
PPO masking from numerical ratio truncation.
\item We validate the predicted ESS--error relationship and
clipped-versus-permissive crossover with an exact contextual-bandit oracle, then
use a minimal ESS-triggered intervention to test the theory in language-model
RLVR.
\end{enumerate}

The next section states the fixed-rollout problem in which this reliability
question can be analyzed exactly.

\section{Preliminaries: fixed-rollout policy optimization}
\label{sec:setting}

RLVR alternates between data collection and optimization.  A rollout policy
generates a batch of complete responses, and a changing current policy is then
updated on that fixed batch.  We analyze one such optimization phase and keep
the rollout policy explicit throughout.

\subsection{Population target and sampled update}

Let $\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb R^d$ denote the policy parameter. Let
$X\sim\nu$ be a prompt, let $Q(\cdot\mid X)$ be the rollout policy, and let
$R(X,Y)$ be the terminal verifier reward of a complete response.  During the
optimization phase, the policy of interest is $P_\theta$, with objective
$J(\theta):=\E_{P_\theta}[R]$.  Assuming $P_\theta\ll Q$, this target can be
evaluated from rollout data using the sequence likelihood ratio
\begin{equation}
  W_\theta(X,Y)
  :=\frac{P_\theta(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)},
  \qquad
  \E_Q[W_\theta\mid X]=1.
  \label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}

The policy gradient separates the response-level learning signal from the
distribution mismatch.  Let $b(X)$ be a detached prompt-only baseline, set
$A=R-b(X)$, and define the current-policy score and gradient contribution by
\begin{equation}
  S_\theta:=\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y\mid X),
  \qquad
  H_\theta:=A S_\theta.
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
Assuming that differentiation and expectation can be interchanged, the target
gradient is
\begin{align}
  g(\theta):=\nabla J(\theta)
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[R S_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-1}\\
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[(R-b(X))S_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-2}\\
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[H_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-3}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta].
  \label{eq:true-gradient}
\end{align}
The first line is the score-function identity.  The second uses
$\E_{P_\theta}[b(X)S_\theta\mid X]
=b(X)\nabla_\theta\sum_yP_\theta(y\mid X)=0$.  The third substitutes
\eqref{eq:score}, and the fourth changes measure from $P_\theta$ to $Q$.
Given $N$ independent prompt-response pairs from the rollout distribution, the
permissive importance-weighted estimator is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_W(\theta)
  :=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N W_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}.
  \label{eq:raw-estimator}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:true-gradient} identifies the desired update, whereas
Equation~\eqref{eq:raw-estimator} describes what the fixed batch can estimate.
The estimator is unbiased at fixed $\theta$, but its accuracy can deteriorate as
$P_\theta$ moves into responses that are poorly represented under $Q$.  We next
quantify this loss of coverage.

\subsection{Effective support of the rollout distribution}

Sequence ESS summarizes how much support the rollout distribution provides for
the current policy.  Assuming $\E_Q[W_\theta^2]<\infty$, define the normalized
population ESS by
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\theta:=\frac{1}{\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
  =\exp\{-D_2(P_\theta\|Q)\}.
  \label{eq:population-ess}
\end{equation}
Because $\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$, its relation to ratio dispersion is exact:
\begin{align}
  \E_Q[(W_\theta-1)^2]
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta^2]-2\E_Q[W_\theta]+1
    \label{eq:weight-variance-proof-1}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta^2]-1
    \label{eq:weight-variance-proof-2}\\
  &=\rho_\theta^{-1}-1.
    \label{eq:weight-variance}
\end{align}
The first line expands the square, the second uses $\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$, and the
third uses Equation~\eqref{eq:population-ess}.  Hence $N\rho_\theta$ is the
population effective sequence count, consistent with the second-moment
coverage principle used in importance-sampling policy optimization
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  When $\rho_\theta$ is near one, the batch
retains nearly all of its nominal support.  When it approaches zero, a batch of
$N$ responses behaves as if it contained far fewer effective observations.

ESS measures ratio concentration, not the size of the gradient contributions
attached to those ratios.  The theoretical analysis begins by separating these
two quantities and then asks whether their joint effect is large enough to
change the preferred policy update.

\section{Theoretical analysis}
\label{sec:theory}

Section~\ref{sec:setting} reduced fixed-rollout optimization to a sampled
importance-weighted gradient and a measure of its effective support.  This
section determines when that gradient is reliable, how its error controls
one-step improvement and finite-time convergence, and when clipping can reduce
that error.  The
argument follows one causal chain: poor coverage raises gradient error, gradient
error enlarges the certified stationary region, and clipping becomes preferable
only when its variance reduction repays its bias.  This chain gives ESS an
operational role rather than treating it as another diagnostic of policy drift.
All proofs are given line by line in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} so that the main
argument remains visible.

\subsection{When the permissive gradient is reliable}
\label{sec:raw}

The permissive estimator targets the desired on-policy gradient without bias,
but unbiasedness alone does not make a fixed batch reliable.  A small number of
large ratios can dominate the average.  ESS measures this loss of coverage, but
the resulting error also depends on the size of the associated gradient
contributions.  We isolate that second factor through
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}
  :=\frac{\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]}
          {\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}.
  \label{eq:gradient-scale}
\end{equation}
The quantity $G_{2,\theta}$ is the squared gradient scale under the part of the
rollout distribution emphasized by large ratios.  Separating this scale from
coverage yields the paper's reliability bridge.

\begin{theorem}[ESS reliability bridge]
\label{thm:raw-mse}
Assume $\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]<\infty$.  For fixed $\theta$,
$\widehat g_W$ is unbiased and
\begin{align}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  &=\frac1N\left\{
    \E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right\}
  \label{eq:raw-mse}\\
  &=\frac1N\left(
    \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{\rho_\theta}-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right)
  \label{eq:ess-factorization}\\
  &\le \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}.
  \label{eq:ess-bridge}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

The factorization identifies $N\rho_\theta$ as the effective support of the
gradient estimate.  Within a fixed update construction, declining ESS therefore
weakens the reliability bound in direct proportion.  The exact expression keeps
$N$ because it is part of any sample average, but the operational comparison in
this paper holds that construction fixed and uses ESS to locate the reliability
regime.  The next question is whether this statistical error has an optimization
consequence.

\subsection{From gradient MSE to finite-time convergence}
\label{sec:mse-improvement}

Smoothness turns gradient reliability into an optimization guarantee.
Theorem~7 of \citet{papini2022smoothing} shows that an $L$-smooth policy
objective satisfies the following lower bound for any feasible parameter step
$d$:
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta+d)-J(\theta)
  \ge g(\theta)^\top d-\frac{L}{2}\|d\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:smooth-policy-improvement}
\end{equation}
The linear term rewards alignment with the true gradient, while the quadratic
term penalizes an inaccurate or excessively large step.  Completing the square
combines both effects into gradient error, so permissive, clipped, and truncated
estimators can be compared on the same optimization scale.

\begin{theorem}[Gradient error controls one-step policy improvement]
\label{thm:mse-improvement}
Fix $\theta$ and let $g=g(\theta)$.  Let $\widehat g$ be any, possibly biased,
gradient estimate.  Suppose $J$ is $L$-smooth on the line segment from
$\theta$ to $\theta+\eta\widehat g$, and that the update is feasible.  For
$0<\eta\le 1/L$,
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta+\eta\widehat g)-J(\theta)
  \ge \frac{\eta}{2}
  \left\{\|g\|_2^2-\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2\right\}.
  \label{eq:pointwise-improvement}
\end{equation}
If $\widehat g$ is random with finite second moment, then
\begin{equation}
  \E\!\left[J(\theta+\eta\widehat g)-J(\theta)\right]
  \ge \frac{\eta}{2}
  \left\{\|g\|_2^2-\E\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2\right\}.
  \label{eq:mse-improvement}
\end{equation}
Consequently, $\E\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2<\|g\|_2^2$ is sufficient for positive
expected policy improvement.
\end{theorem}

The one-step statement extends to a finite-time convergence result.  Since the
policy objective is nonconcave in general, the standard target is first-order
stationarity rather than a global optimum: a random iterate $\theta_U$ is
$\varepsilon$-stationary in mean square when
$\E\|\nabla J(\theta_U)\|_2^2\le\varepsilon^2$.  This is the convergence notion
used in established vanilla and off-policy policy-gradient analyses
\citep{yuan2022general,kallus2020efficient}.  The next result states convergence
directly and isolates gradient MSE as the only estimator-dependent term.

\begin{theorem}[Finite-time convergence under gradient MSE]
\label{thm:finite-time-mse}
Fix $\theta_0$, and let $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t+\eta\widehat g_t$ for
$t=0,\ldots,T-1$.  Suppose the conditions of
Theorem~\ref{thm:mse-improvement} hold at every update with a common
$0<\eta\le1/L$, suppose $J(\theta)\le J^\star$ on the feasible domain, and suppose
$\E\|\widehat g_t-\nabla J(\theta_t)\|_2^2<\infty$.  Let $U$ be uniform on
$\{0,\ldots,T-1\}$ independently of the optimization path, and define
\begin{equation}
  \overline e_T
  :=\frac1T\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
    \E\|\widehat g_t-\nabla J(\theta_t)\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:average-gradient-error}
\end{equation}
There exists a finite constant $C_{\mathrm{init}}$, independent of $T$ and the
gradient estimator, such that
\begin{equation}
  \E\|\nabla J(\theta_U)\|_2^2
  \le \frac{C_{\mathrm{init}}}{T}+\overline e_T.
  \label{eq:finite-time-convergence}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
  \overline e_T\longrightarrow0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \E\|\nabla J(\theta_U)\|_2^2\longrightarrow0.
  \label{eq:vanishing-mse-convergence}
\end{equation}
More quantitatively, if $\overline e_T\le\varepsilon^2/2$ and
$T\ge2C_{\mathrm{init}}/\varepsilon^2$, then $\theta_U$ is
$\varepsilon$-stationary in mean square.  If instead
$\limsup_{T\to\infty}\overline e_T\le\sigma^2$, then
$\limsup_{T\to\infty}\E\|\nabla J(\theta_U)\|_2^2\le\sigma^2$.
\end{theorem}

Theorem~\ref{thm:finite-time-mse} states precisely what converges.  Exact
gradients recover the usual $O(1/T)$ rate for the average squared gradient, and
vanishing average MSE implies convergence to first-order stationarity.  A
persistent MSE level instead certifies only a stationary neighborhood.  The
exact initialization constant is given in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}; it affects
the finite optimization horizon but not the estimator comparison.  The theorem
does not claim convergence of the parameter sequence or convergence to a
global optimum.

This distinction also clarifies what is inherited from prior work.  Corollary
4.6 of \citet{yuan2022general} proves finite-time stationarity for vanilla policy
gradient under smoothness and a second-moment condition.  Theorem~11 of
\citet{kallus2020efficient} gives the analogous average-gradient structure with
off-policy estimation error.  Theorem~20 and Remark~21 of
\citet{driggs2022biased} show more generally that biased-gradient convergence
can depend on bias through MSE.  Our contribution is not a new generic
nonconvex convergence theorem.  It preserves the full gradient MSE in the
standard argument so that clipping rules can be compared and ESS can enter the
estimator-dependent term.

No bounded parameter set or projection is required by
Theorem~\ref{thm:finite-time-mse}.  It instead assumes that every update is
feasible, that $J$ is smooth along the realized update segments, that the
gradient MSE is finite, and that $J$ is upper bounded on the feasible domain.  In RLVR
with rewards in $[0,1]$, the last condition holds automatically.  Bounded
rewards alone do not guarantee policy smoothness or finite importance-weight
moments.  The smoothing-policy conditions of
\citet{papini2022smoothing}, or the expected score and log-policy-Hessian bounds
in Assumption~4.1 of \citet{yuan2022general}, are sufficient routes to the
required smoothness.  A projection can make such a bound uniform by keeping the
iterates in a controlled region, but the proof does not require a bound on
$\|\theta_t\|_2$ when pathwise smoothness already holds.  By contrast, if an
active projection onto a constrained parameter set is part of the algorithm,
the appropriate convergence measure is a projected gradient mapping.  The
Euclidean-gradient statement above does not cover that case; projected policy
gradient requires a separate analysis such as \citet{xiao2022convergence}.
The finite-MSE condition concerns the estimator rather than the parameter
domain.  If the raw importance-weighted gradient has an infinite second moment,
the expected convergence certificate is vacuous; Section~\ref{sec:ratio-truncation}
explains when upper-ratio truncation repairs this problem and what bias it costs.

The ESS factorization now supplies a coverage-dependent convergence guarantee.
To state it without hiding a dependence assumption, let each gradient batch be
drawn after $\theta_t$ is fixed.

\begin{corollary}[Finite-time ESS convergence certificate]
\label{cor:ess-convergence}
In the setting of Theorem~\ref{thm:finite-time-mse}, let $\mathcal F_t$
contain the history before update $t$, with $\theta_t$ measurable with respect
to $\mathcal F_t$.  Suppose that, conditional on $\mathcal F_t$,
$\widehat g_t$ is the raw estimator formed from $N$ independent draws from the
rollout distribution $Q_t$.  Let $\rho_t$ and $G_{2,t}$ denote the corresponding
population ESS and weighted gradient scale.  Then
\begin{equation}
  \E\|\nabla J(\theta_U)\|_2^2
  \le
  \frac{C_{\mathrm{init}}}{T}
  +\frac1T\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
    \E\!\left[\frac{G_{2,t}}{N\rho_t}\right].
  \label{eq:ess-convergence}
\end{equation}
If $G_{2,t}\le\overline G_2$ and $\rho_t\ge\underline\rho>0$ almost surely
along the path, then
\begin{equation}
  \E\|\nabla J(\theta_U)\|_2^2
  \le
  \frac{C_{\mathrm{init}}}{T}
  +\frac{\overline G_2}{N\underline\rho}.
  \label{eq:ess-convergence-uniform}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

The result gives effective support a finite-time convergence meaning.  At
controlled gradient scale, weak coverage enlarges the certified stationary
neighborhood for a permissive policy-gradient path.  With fixed $N$ and a
nonzero bound $\overline G_2/(N\underline\rho)$, the certificate does not imply
exact stationarity as $T\to\infty$.  For this certificate to imply exact
convergence, the average estimation term must vanish, for example through
growing effective support or a decreasing weighted gradient scale.  For a single update,
the exact MSE identity
also shows that
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}<(N+1)\rho_\theta\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \label{eq:positive-ess-update}
\end{equation}
is sufficient for positive expected improvement.  Neither statement produces
a universal normalized-ESS threshold because the guarantee also depends on
gradient scale and true-gradient magnitude.  Moreover, the ESS substitution in
Corollary~\ref{cor:ess-convergence} requires conditional sampling.  Under
repeated optimization of one fixed rollout, the pathwise inequality underlying
Theorem~\ref{thm:finite-time-mse} remains valid, but replacing its realized
error by the fixed-$\theta$ ESS identity requires additional adaptive-data control.  The
contextual-bandit study therefore measures this relationship directly along a
reused-rollout path.  Before that comparison, we examine why the remaining
scale term is particularly manageable in RLVR.

\subsection{Why RLVR makes the scale term manageable}

The weighted gradient scale combines two factors: the advantage and the policy
score.  RLVR naturally controls the first factor because verifier rewards are
bounded and group-normalized advantages have finite range.  A score bound or an
empirical scale estimate is still required for the second factor.

\begin{proposition}[A sufficient bound on $G_{2,\theta}$]
\label{prop:g2-bound}
If $|A|\le B_A$ and $\|S_\theta\|_2\le B_S$ almost surely under $Q$, then
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}\le B_A^2B_S^2.
  \label{eq:g2-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

Common RLVR constructions make the advantage factor small.  If the verifier
reward is binary and a prompt-only baseline satisfies $b(X)\in[0,1]$, then
$|A|=|R-b(X)|\le1$.  Outcome-level GRPO instead standardizes rewards within a
group of $K$ responses \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}.  Its standardized
advantages also have a deterministic finite-group bound whenever their sample
variance is nonzero.

\begin{proposition}[Standardized group-advantage bound]
\label{prop:grpo-bound}
Let $d_i=r_i-\bar r$, let $v=K^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^K d_j^2>0$, and let
$A_i=d_i/\sqrt v$.  Then $|A_i|\le\sqrt{K-1}$ for every $i$.
\end{proposition}

These results clarify both the value and the limit of advantage normalization.
It controls one factor in $G_{2,\theta}$, while the score still requires either a
bound or an empirical estimate.  Group normalization also couples the $K$
responses, so Theorem~\ref{thm:raw-mse} cannot be applied response by response
without a group-level sampling argument.  The binary-reward,
prompt-baseline case satisfies the theorem directly; the GRPO result controls
scale but does not supply an independence justification.

The analysis has now linked effective coverage to gradient error and gradient
error to finite-time policy optimization.  This link explains why a permissive
update can work while support remains broad.  It does not yet show that clipping
is the right response when support deteriorates.  That comparison requires the
exact gradient induced by the clipped objective.

\subsection{What clipping changes}
\label{sec:clipping}

Low ESS indicates that an intervention may be useful, but it does not establish
that a particular intervention is better.  PPO clipping can remove unstable
sample contributions, yet it changes the target gradient at the same time.  We
derive that change explicitly because clipping is often confused with globally
truncating the importance ratio.

\subsubsection{PPO clipping as an advantage-sign gradient mask}

For a detached advantage $A$, the per-sample PPO surrogate is
\begin{equation}
  \ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
  :=\min\left\{
    W_\theta A,
    \operatorname{clip}(W_\theta,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:ppo-objective}
\end{equation}
Clipping this scalar objective does not cap the gradient coefficient.  Its
derivative instead masks samples according to advantage sign.

\begin{proposition}[PPO clipping is advantage-sign gradient masking]
\label{prop:ppo-mask}
Away from the two clipping boundaries, define
\begin{equation}
  M_\epsilon
  :=\mathbf{1}\{A\ge0, W_\theta\le1+\epsilon\}
   +\mathbf{1}\{A<0, W_\theta\ge1-\epsilon\}.
  \label{eq:ppo-mask}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_\theta\ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
  =W_\theta M_\epsilon H_\theta.
  \label{eq:ppo-gradient}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

PPO therefore removes an upper-ratio gradient only for nonnegative advantages
and a lower-ratio gradient only for negative advantages.  In particular, a
large ratio with negative advantage remains active.  PPO clipping is a
directional mask, not a global bound on importance coefficients.

This distinction reveals the relevant trade-off.  The mask is useful only when
the variance it removes is worth the bias it introduces.  To express that
comparison once for PPO and other interventions, let
$C_\theta$ be any scalar coefficient for which $C_\theta H_\theta$ has a finite
second moment.  Define
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_C:=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N C_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i},
  \qquad
  b_C:=\E_Q[(C_\theta-W_\theta)H_\theta],
  \label{eq:modified-estimator}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma_W:=\Cov_Q(W_\theta H_\theta),
  \qquad
  \Sigma_C:=\Cov_Q(C_\theta H_\theta).
  \label{eq:bias-covariance}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{The estimator crossover}

The next result gives the exact risk of the modified coefficient $C_\theta$
relative to the permissive coefficient $W_\theta$.

\begin{theorem}[Exact estimator crossover]
\label{thm:crossover}
For fixed $\theta$ and finite second moments,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  =\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  =\|b_C\|_2^2+\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C).
  \label{eq:two-risks}
\end{equation}
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  <\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  \iff
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C)
  >N\|b_C\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Bias--variance crossover]
\label{cor:batch-crossover}
Let
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_C
  :=\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C)>0.
  \label{eq:covariance-reduction}
\end{equation}
If $b_C\ne0$, then
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  <\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  \iff
  N<N_C^*:=\frac{\Delta_C}{\|b_C\|_2^2}.
  \label{eq:batch-crossover}
\end{equation}
Thus the modified estimator is preferred below its critical batch size, while
the unbiased estimator is preferred above it.  If $b_C=0$, the modified
estimator has lower MSE for every finite $N$ whenever $\Delta_C>0$.
\end{corollary}

The crossover condition states the entire reason to clip: the removed
covariance must exceed the squared bias of the modified gradient at the given
update construction.  The corollary records that covariance averages away but
clipping bias does not.  This distinction is retained for theoretical
completeness; the practical rule compares updates under the same construction.
For PPO, the coefficient is
$C_\theta^{\mathrm{PPO}}=W_\theta M_\epsilon$.
Its exact bias is
\begin{align}
  b_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}
  &=\E_Q[(W_\theta M_\epsilon-W_\theta)H_\theta]
    \label{eq:ppo-bias-1}\\
  &=-\E_Q[W_\theta(1-M_\epsilon)H_\theta].
    \label{eq:ppo-bias-2}
\end{align}
Theorem~\ref{thm:crossover} therefore compares the covariance removed by PPO's
sign-specific mask with the squared bias of the masked gradient.  Combining the theorem with
Theorem~\ref{thm:mse-improvement}, the difference between the modified and raw
MSE-based improvement certificates is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\eta}{2}\left\{
    \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
    -\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:improvement-crossover}
\end{equation}
Thus Equation~\eqref{eq:crossover} determines which estimator has the stronger
one-step certificate, not merely which estimator has lower gradient risk.

The oracle comparison in Equation~\eqref{eq:crossover} cannot be evaluated
during ordinary training because it requires the true gradient and population
moments.  To make the theory falsifiable with observable quantities, consider
the simplest proxy: switch estimators only on a low-ESS event.  The next result
states the conditional risk implication of that test.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional risk of an ESS-indexed switch]
\label{prop:ess-gate-guarantee}
Let $D_\tau=\{\widehat\rho<\tau\}$, assume that $D_\tau$ and $D_\tau^c$
have positive probability, and define
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_\tau
  :=\mathbf{1}_{D_\tau}\widehat g_C
   +\mathbf{1}_{D_\tau^c}\widehat g_W.
  \label{eq:ess-gated-estimator}
\end{equation}
For $j\in\{C,W\}$ and any event $D$, write
$R_j(D):=\E[\|\widehat g_j-g\|_2^2\mid D]$.  Then
\begin{align}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_\tau)
  -\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  &=\Pr(D_\tau)\{R_C(D_\tau)-R_W(D_\tau)\},
  \label{eq:gate-versus-raw}\\
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_\tau)
  -\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  &=\Pr(D_\tau^c)\{R_W(D_\tau^c)-R_C(D_\tau^c)\}.
  \label{eq:gate-versus-clipped}
\end{align}
Hence the switch has no larger MSE than either static estimator whenever
$R_C(D_\tau)\le R_W(D_\tau)$ and
$R_W(D_\tau^c)\le R_C(D_\tau^c)$.  Under the assumptions of
Theorem~\ref{thm:mse-improvement}, the same ordering holds for the associated
MSE-based policy-improvement certificates.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Because the two events partition the sample space and the switch equals one
estimator on each event,
\begin{align}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_\tau)
  &=\E[\|\widehat g_\tau-g\|_2^2\mathbf{1}_{D_\tau}]
    +\E[\|\widehat g_\tau-g\|_2^2\mathbf{1}_{D_\tau^c}]\\
  &=\Pr(D_\tau)R_C(D_\tau)
    +\Pr(D_\tau^c)R_W(D_\tau^c).
  \label{eq:gate-mse-decomposition}
\end{align}
Similarly,
\begin{align}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  &=\Pr(D_\tau)R_W(D_\tau)
    +\Pr(D_\tau^c)R_W(D_\tau^c),\\
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  &=\Pr(D_\tau)R_C(D_\tau)
    +\Pr(D_\tau^c)R_C(D_\tau^c).
\end{align}
Subtracting each static risk from the conditional risk gives
Equations~\eqref{eq:gate-versus-raw} and
\eqref{eq:gate-versus-clipped}.  The risk ordering follows term by term.
The final claim follows by substituting each MSE into
Equation~\eqref{eq:mse-improvement} at the same learning rate.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:ess-gate-guarantee} turns the theoretical explanation into
a falsifiable empirical condition.  Any threshold must identify a region in
which masking lowers conditional gradient MSE; outside that region, the
permissive estimator should retain its advantage.  The contextual-bandit study
first verifies the oracle crossover and only then tests $\tau=0.1$ as a
conservative observable approximation.
 
\subsubsection{Ratio truncation bias trade-off}
\label{sec:ratio-truncation}

Ratio truncation changes the importance coefficient after the gradient has been
specified, so it is distinct from the derivative of the PPO objective.  For
$c>0$, define
\begin{equation}
  C_c^{\mathrm{tr}}:=\min(W_\theta,c),
  \qquad
  T_c:=(W_\theta-c)_+,
  \qquad
  \tau_c:=\E_Q[T_c].
  \label{eq:upper-truncation}
\end{equation}
The contribution above $c$ is then $cH_\theta$, not zero.  This is a detached
importance-weight estimator, not the derivative of the PPO surrogate.

\begin{corollary}[Bias and MSE of upper-ratio truncation]
\label{cor:upper-truncation}
Suppose $\|H_\theta\|_2\le H_{\max}$ almost surely.  The detached estimator
$\widehat g_c=N^{-1}\sum_i C_{c,i}^{\mathrm{tr}}H_{\theta,i}$ satisfies
\begin{align}
  b_c&=-\E_Q[T_cH_\theta],
  &\|b_c\|_2&\le H_{\max}\tau_c,
  \label{eq:cap-bias}\\
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_c)
  &\le H_{\max}^2\left(\tau_c^2+\frac{c}{N}\right).
  \label{eq:cap-mse-bound}
\end{align}
\end{corollary}

The result isolates the appeal of upper truncation.  If
$\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]=\infty$ while $H_\theta$ is bounded, the raw
expected convergence bound is vacuous but the truncated estimator has finite MSE.  More generally,
substituting either estimator into Theorem~\ref{thm:finite-time-mse} shows that
truncation tightens the finite-time guarantee exactly when its full MSE is lower
than the raw MSE.  Finite variance alone is therefore not sufficient: a fixed
cap can leave persistent bias and hence only a stationary neighborhood.  Exact
stationarity requires the average truncated-estimator MSE, including its bias,
to vanish along the optimization path.

Upper truncation replaces the raw second-moment term by $c/N$, while its bias
remains small when the tail excess $\tau_c$ is small.  The cap is therefore a
tunable estimator safeguard, not part of the paper's central claim.  Truncated importance ratios also appear in ACER with
bias correction \citep{wang2017acer} and in the V-trace correction used by
IMPALA \citep{espeholt2018impala}; recent LLM methods likewise control extreme
weight moments \citep{zheng2025m2po,fakoor2026p3o}.  These precedents motivate
truncation as a separate importance-sampling device.  They do not make it
equivalent to PPO clipping.  In particular, PPO's advantage-sign mask does not
bound every retained ratio contribution and therefore does not by itself ensure
finite estimator variance.

The theory now explains the observed regime change.  When $G_{2,\theta}$ is
controlled, effective support governs permissive-gradient reliability and its
policy-improvement certificate.  Clipping becomes preferable only when its
covariance reduction exceeds the bias penalty.  Thus ESS locates the statistical
transition between permissive and safeguarded updates.  We next define a minimal
observable intervention only to test this prediction outside the oracle setting.

\subsection{An observable test of the theory}
\label{sec:practice}

Population ESS is unavailable during training, but its plug-in estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho
  :=\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^N W_i)^2}{N\sum_{i=1}^N W_i^2}.
  \label{eq:sample-ess}
\end{equation}
The statistic is computed from raw sequence ratios before clipping or
truncation.  We keep the update construction and number of valid sequences
fixed within each reported comparison, so $\widehat\rho$ directly orders the
reliability of the competing updates.  Appendix~\ref{app:bandit} reports the
sensitivity to estimator construction.

The theory does not determine a universal numerical threshold.  The crossover
also depends on clipping bias, gradient scale, and signal, while sample ESS can
miss unobserved tail mass.  Moreover, the same batch usually determines both the
event and the gradient, and a token-local LLM surrogate is not algebraically
identical to Equation~\eqref{eq:raw-estimator}.  We therefore use
$\widehat\rho=0.1$ only as a prespecified conservative intervention.  It is not
presented as a theoretical constant or a new optimization objective.  Recent
LLM RL methods likewise adapt updates using ESS, weight moments, or sequence
reshaping \cite{zheng2025m2po,shen2026vespo,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}.

Section~\ref{sec:simulation} first tests the theoretical quantities with an
exact population oracle.  Only after those tests does an RLVR-style loop ask
whether the simple observable intervention behaves as the theory predicts.

\section{Contextual-bandit validation of the theory}
\label{sec:simulation}

This study tests the explanation developed in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.  It asks
whether ESS predicts permissive-gradient error, whether that error changes
policy improvement, and whether clipped and permissive estimators exchange
their MSE ordering as effective support deteriorates.  A threshold intervention
is examined only after these three predictions are verified.

\subsection{Testbed and oracle}

We use the standard reduction from classification to a contextual bandit on Optdigits
\citep{wang2017adaptive,alpaydin1998optdigits}.  Each handwritten-digit image is
a context, the policy emits a 16-token binary encoding of the digit, and an
exact-match verifier supplies reward.  Because the dataset is a finite
population, we can enumerate the exact reward and policy gradient.  The
sequence action still produces the product importance ratios that drive ESS in
RLVR, so the testbed removes language-model complexity without removing the
statistical mechanism of interest.

The optimization loop follows grouped RLVR
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath,yu2025dapo}.  Each rollout samples 128 prompts and
16 responses per prompt, computes leave-one-out advantages, and supplies 16
sequential minibatch updates.  Ratios and ESS are recomputed before every update,
and a new rollout is collected only afterward.  We run 100 paired replications;
Appendix~\ref{app:bandit} gives the compact protocol and full result tables.

\subsection{The predicted regime change appears}

ESS first predicts the reliability of the permissive gradient.  Its MSE rises
from $1.6\times10^{-2}$ above ESS $0.8$ to $1.31\times10^{-1}$ between $0.03$
and $0.1$, and to $4.77\times10^{-1}$ below $0.03$.  In the same two low-ESS
regions, permissive steps reduce population reward by $4.0\%$ and $13.3\%$,
while matched oracle-gradient steps improve reward.  ESS therefore identifies
estimation failure rather than the absence of an improving direction.

Frozen-policy redraws then verify the bias--variance crossover in
Theorem~\ref{thm:crossover}.  With the fixed $N=512$ diagnostic, PPO masking has
lower MSE below population ESS $0.4$, while the permissive estimator has lower
MSE above it.  A disjoint calibration split places the crossover at $0.42$ and
reduces held-out MSE by $4.3\%\pm1.1\%$ relative to the better static estimator.
Thus ESS predicts not only raw-gradient failure but also which estimator has
lower error under a fixed construction.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/ess_formula_oracle.pdf}
  \caption{Controlled tests of the theoretical regime change.  (a) The
  clipped-versus-permissive MSE preference changes sign with population ESS.
  (b) A conservative $0.1$ event selects updates on which PPO masking usually

Finally, we use $\widehat\rho<0.1$ as a deliberately conservative operational
check, not as the oracle crossover.  It selects only $3.3\%$ of updates; within
that event, PPO has lower MSE on $83.7\%\pm1.4\%$ of updates and reduces
conditional MSE by $53.3\%\pm2.9\%$.  In the four-epoch test, the resulting
trajectory reaches $0.4940\pm0.0050$ final reward, compared with
$0.4606\pm0.0098$ for always permissive and $0.4038\pm0.0005$ for always
masked.  This minimal intervention is evidence for the theory's operational
consequence, not a claim that $0.1$ is universal or that the paper introduces a
new optimizer.

The controlled study therefore establishes the paper's main empirical result:
ESS tracks the reliability transition that changes the role of clipping.  The
next section asks whether the same transition is visible in language-model RLVR,
where the population gradient cannot be enumerated.

\section{Language-model evidence for the regime change}
\label{sec:llm-experiments}

The contextual-bandit study establishes the mechanism under an exact oracle.
This section tests its operational implication in language-model training,
where neither the population gradient nor its MSE is observable.  If ESS marks
the predicted reliability transition, a conservative intervention should retain
permissive updates while ESS is high and prevent their late failure after ESS
falls.  This is a test of the explanation, not a new optimizer benchmark.

\subsection{A minimal intervention}

At every policy update, we compute sequence ESS from the raw ratios before any
masking or truncation.  All methods use the same valid sequences and minibatch
construction.  We then evaluate $0.1$ as a prespecified heuristic:
when $\widehat\rho\ge0.1$, the method retains the permissive update; when
$\widehat\rho<0.1$, it activates the safeguard associated with the comparison.
We test conventional GRPO clipping, Clip-Higher \citep{yu2025dapo}, the
divergence constraint of DPPO \citep{qi2026dppo}.  We also pair conventional
clipping with a permissive TIS update whose ratio cap is 3.

\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{LLM}}
  =\begin{cases}
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{permissive}},&\widehat\rho\ge0.1,\\
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{safeguard}},&\widehat\rho<0.1.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:llm-gate}
\end{equation}
Each conditional run is compared with its always-permissive and
always-protected counterparts.  This pairing tests the regime prediction while
holding the safeguard itself fixed.  The threshold is set before the reported
runs and is not retuned by update rule.  It is a practical probe, not an
estimate of the theoretical crossover.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

We optimize Qwen3-30B-A3B for 200 training steps and evaluate on AIME-2024 with
mean@16.  The primary comparison is the complete evaluation trajectory and its
step-200 endpoint.  We also align normalized sequence ESS and the fraction of
clipped updates to the training axis to verify that the rule responds to a loss
of effective support.  The supplied artifacts report one trajectory per
configuration, so we report endpoint differences without uncertainty and treat
them as trajectory-level evidence.

\subsection{Finite population and initial policy}

Optdigits is a handwritten-digit classification benchmark with 5,620 labeled
examples
\citep{alpaydin1998optdigits}.  Each original $32\times32$ binary image is
partitioned into $4\times4$ blocks; foreground counts produce 64 integer
features in $\{0,\ldots,16\}$.  We concatenate the supplied 3,823 training and
1,797 test examples, divide each feature by 16, and append an intercept.  This
gives a finite population $\{(x_j,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{M}$ with $M=5{,}620$ and
$x_j\in\mathbb{R}^{65}$.  The original split is not used because the estimand
is finite-population policy optimization, not classification generalization.

Each label $y\in\{0,\ldots,9\}$ has a four-bit binary representation.  Let
$c(y)\in\{0,1\}^{16}$ repeat this code four times.  The repetition changes only
the response length, not which answer is correct, and creates a sequence ratio
that compounds over 16 token probabilities.  The policy has one Bernoulli head
per token position,
\begin{align}
  p_{\theta,\ell}(x)&=\sigma(\theta_\ell^\top x),\nonumber\\
  P_\theta(a_{1:16}\mid x)
  &=\prod_{\ell=1}^{16}
    p_{\theta,\ell}(x)^{a_\ell}
    \{1-p_{\theta,\ell}(x)\}^{1-a_\ell},
  \qquad \theta\in\mathbb{R}^{16\times65}.
  \label{eq:optdigits-policy}
\end{align}
The verifier reward is
$R(x_j,a_{1:16})=\mathbf{1}\{a_{1:16}=c(y_j)\}$.

Initialization is deterministic and uses no experimental outcomes.  Starting
from zero, we fit all token heads for 500 full-population logistic-regression
steps with step size $0.8$ and no regularization.  We then multiply the fitted
weights by $0.55$ to keep the initial policy stochastic, which gives exact
initial reward $V(\theta_0)=0.095766$.  This procedure uses no data split,
minibatching, early stopping, or model selection.

For compactness, define
\begin{equation}
  q_j(\theta)
  :=P_\theta(c(y_j)\mid x_j)
  =\prod_{\ell=1}^{16}
  \left[c_{j\ell}p_{j\ell}
  +(1-c_{j\ell})(1-p_{j\ell})\right],
  \label{eq:correct-sequence-probability}
\end{equation}
where $c_{j\ell}=c(y_j)_\ell$ and
$p_{j\ell}=p_{\theta,\ell}(x_j)$.  The finite-population objective is
\begin{equation}
  V(\theta)=\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^{M}q_j(\theta).
  \label{eq:optdigits-value}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Exact gradient and group-relative advantage}

The Bernoulli score identity gives the exact finite-population gradient
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_{\theta_\ell}V(\theta)
  =\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^{M}
    q_j(\theta)\{c_{j\ell}-p_{j\ell}\}x_j.
  \label{eq:optdigits-gradient}
\end{equation}
where $q_j(\theta)$ is the correct-sequence probability,
$c_{j\ell}$ is its bit at position $\ell$, and $p_{j\ell}$ is the policy
probability of bit one.  The oracle enumerates all 5,620 contexts at the current
pre-update policy.  We measure a sampled estimator by
\begin{equation}
  E_t=\|\widehat g_t-\nabla V(\theta_t)\|_F^2,
  \label{eq:optdigits-gradient-error}
\end{equation}
and report its mean within each fixed ESS interval.

For response $g$ to prompt $i$, we use the leave-one-out advantage
\begin{equation}
  A_{ig}=R_{ig}-\frac1{G-1}\sum_{h\ne g}R_{ih}
  =\frac{G}{G-1}(R_{ig}-\bar R_i).
  \label{eq:loo-group-advantage}
\end{equation}
It has the sign of the group-centered GRPO advantage
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}; standard-deviation scaling is omitted so every
minibatch targets the same population gradient.  The baseline excludes the
focal response and is therefore independent of it conditional on the prompt.
Importance weighting and the zero-mean score identity then preserve the target
in Equation~\eqref{eq:optdigits-gradient}.

\small
\textbf{Input:} initial policy $\theta_0$; eight rollouts; 128 prompts per
rollout; $G=16$ responses per prompt; 16 minibatches; step size $3$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Set the rollout policy $Q\leftarrow P_\theta$.  Sample prompts and
  grouped responses, then compute verifier rewards and leave-one-out advantages.
  \item Randomly partition the intact prompt groups into 16 minibatches, each
  containing eight groups and $N=128$ complete responses.
  \item For each minibatch, recompute
  $W=P_\theta(Y\mid x)/Q(Y\mid x)$, policy scores, and sample ESS.  Form the
  designated raw, PPO-masked, or conditional estimator and update
  $\theta\leftarrow\theta+3\widehat g$.
  \item After all 16 updates, discard the rollout and repeat from the new policy.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}}
\end{algorithm}

Every response enters one update.  Within a rollout, responses, rewards,
advantages, and rollout probabilities remain fixed, whereas current
probabilities, ratios, scores, and ESS are recomputed before each update.
Sixteen minibatches are used because the first four never reach ESS below $0.1$
in the diagnostic runs; a shorter traversal would not expose the regime under
study.

\subsection{Estimators and diagnostics}

Let $S_{ig,t}=\nabla_\theta\log
P_{\theta_{r,t-1}}(Y_{ig}\mid x_i)$.  For each $N=128$ minibatch, the
permissive and PPO-masked gradients are
\begin{align}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{raw},t}
  &=\frac1N\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t}A_{ig}S_{ig,t},
    \label{eq:simulation-raw-gradient}\\
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{PPO},t}
  &=\frac1N\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t}M_{ig,t}A_{ig}S_{ig,t},
    \label{eq:simulation-ppo-gradient}
\end{align}
where $M_{ig,t}$ is the advantage-sign mask in
Proposition~\ref{prop:ppo-mask} with $\epsilon=0.2$.  This is gradient masking,
not numerical ratio truncation.  The third method uses
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{cond},t}
  =\begin{cases}
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{raw},t},&\widehat\rho_t\ge0.1,\\
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{PPO},t},&\widehat\rho_t<0.1,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad
  \widehat\rho_t
  =\frac{(\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t})^2}
  {N\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t}^2}.
  \label{eq:simulation-gate}
\end{equation}
The $0.1$ threshold is fixed before the runs and is not selected from a reported
outcome.  All methods share the initialization, prompt draws, response-sampling
uniforms, and group permutations within each of 100 paired replications; the
master seed is 73.

The first 20 conditional replications provide 2,560 pre-update diagnostics.  At
each state, we evaluate the raw gradient against
Equation~\eqref{eq:optdigits-gradient} and compare its counterfactual reward
step with the exact-gradient step.  Results use seven prespecified ESS intervals:
\[
  \begin{gathered}
    \relax[0,0.03),\ [0.03,0.1),\ [0.1,0.2),\ [0.2,0.4),\\
    [0.4,0.6),\ [0.6,0.8),\ [0.8,1].
  \end{gathered}
\]
Within-bin error bars are standard errors.  Optimization curves use all 100
replications and report relative population-reward improvement from the common
initial policy with 95\% confidence intervals.  One rollout costs 2,048
verifier responses.

\subsection{Fixed-checkpoint crossover diagnostic}

To estimate conditional MSE rather than one realized error, we freeze the
rollout and current policies at selected pre-update checkpoints and redraw
independent minibatches.  This diagnostic does not change training.

The finite Bernoulli policy also makes population sequence ESS exact.  For
prompt $x$ and token position $\ell$, let $q_{x\ell}$ and $p_{x\ell}$ denote
the rollout and current probabilities of token one.  Independence across the
16 response positions gives
\begin{align}
  \E_Q[W^2\mid x]
  &=\prod_{\ell=1}^{16}
    \left\{
      \frac{p_{x\ell}^2}{q_{x\ell}}
      +\frac{(1-p_{x\ell})^2}{1-q_{x\ell}}
    \right\},
    \label{eq:simulation-weight-second-moment}\\
  \rho_{\mathrm{pop}}
  &=\left\{
    \frac1{5620}\sum_{x=1}^{5620}\E_Q[W^2\mid x]
  \right\}^{-1}.
    \label{eq:simulation-population-ess}
\end{align}
These equations use $\E_Q[W]=1$ and contain no minibatch noise.  We evaluate
eight checkpoints from each of the seven ESS intervals, giving 56 frozen states
selected from the 2,560 trajectory checkpoints.  At each state, 32 paired
redraws compare raw and PPO-masked estimates.  One independent block contains
eight prompts and 16 responses per prompt, hence $N=128$ sequences; the
$N=512$ estimate averages four blocks.  The block is the independent unit
because responses within a prompt share an advantage.  For checkpoint $c$,
estimator $e$, and size $N$, we report
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{c,e,N}
  =\frac1{32}\sum_{s=1}^{32}
    \left\|
      \widehat g_{c,e,N}^{(s)}-\nabla V(\theta_c)
    \right\|_F^2.
  \label{eq:simulation-crossover-mse}
\end{equation}
We average redraws within a state and states within an ESS interval; standard
errors use the eight states as independent units.  PPO reduction is
$100(\widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\mathrm{raw}}-
\widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\mathrm{PPO}})/
\widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\mathrm{raw}}$.  Positive values favor masking.
State selection and redraw seeds are 99,246 and 271,901, respectively.

\subsection{Formula calibration and held-out decision test}

We split the eight states in each ESS interval into four calibration and four
test states, yielding 28 of each; seed 161,876 fixes the split.  At every state,
100 paired $N=128$ blocks estimate the raw covariance trace $\widehat V_W$, the
PPO covariance trace $\widehat V_C$, and the PPO squared bias $\widehat B_C$.
The squared-bias estimate subtracts the sampling variance of the estimated mean
and is truncated at zero.  Averaging $K\in\{1,2,4,8\}$ independent blocks gives
\begin{equation}
  \widehat R_W(K):=\frac{\widehat V_W}{K},
  \qquad
  \widehat R_C(K):=\widehat B_C+\frac{\widehat V_C}{K}.
  \label{eq:formula-risks}
\end{equation}
These are the two sides of Theorem~\ref{thm:crossover} at block level.  For each
$K$, we choose the ESS midpoint that minimizes fitted risk while applying PPO
below the threshold and raw above it.  The only interior calibration solution is
$K=4$, or $N=512$, with threshold $0.417$.

Held-out evaluation uses 100 paired redraws at each test state and seed 271,974.
The fitted threshold never sees test-state errors; standard errors use the 28
states as independent units.  The subsequent optimization study freezes that
threshold, uses 512 sequences per update, traverses each rollout for four epochs,
and runs eight rollout cycles in 100 paired replications.  Sample ESS, rather
than population ESS, determines the training intervention; master seed 424,315
fixes the optimization stream.

The fixed-$0.1$ trajectory also records shadow raw and PPO gradients whenever
sample ESS is below $0.417$.  Comparing each with the exact gradient yields the
three intervals $[0,0.1)$, $[0.1,0.25)$, and $[0.25,0.417)$.  These shadow
calculations do not alter training.  Means and standard errors use the 100
replications as independent units.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to estimator construction.}
Table~\ref{tab:rlvr-crossover} reports the direct paired comparison.  With one
training-size minibatch, PPO masking has lower estimated MSE in every ESS
interval.  Averaging four minibatches reduces the permissive estimator's
variance without removing the PPO estimator's bias.  At $N=512$, PPO masking
has lower mean MSE below population ESS $0.4$, while the permissive estimator has
lower mean MSE in the three intervals above $0.4$.  The crossover therefore appears
only after averaging more independent blocks.  This is the batch-size effect
predicted by the factor $N\|b_C\|_2^2$ in
Equation~\eqref{eq:crossover}, applied to the block-level estimator described
above.  The result supports ESS as a crossover coordinate within a fixed update
construction and confirms that the exact oracle boundary is
configuration-specific.  This sensitivity check is not a batch-size
prescription for RLVR: an LLM minibatch count need not equal the number of
independent sampling blocks in the theorem, and the practical $0.1$ rule is
evaluated through its one-sided low-ESS condition instead.

The shadow PPO step has lower immediate reward despite its lower MSE.  This is

\paragraph{Reliability conditioning improves repeated optimization.}
Table~\ref{tab:n512-optimization} reports the four-epoch $N=512$ study.  The
fitted ESS reference has the highest final reward and the smallest uncertainty.  It
uses the raw estimator for 450.3 of 512 updates on average, which explains its
fast progress, while its 61.7 PPO-masked updates prevent the late loss observed
under the static raw estimator.  The fixed-$0.1$ rule gives a complementary
operating point.  It masks only $3.3\%$ of updates, matches the raw median, and
raises the tenth-percentile endpoint from $0.352$ to $0.495$.  It ends above
the fitted reference in 90 of 100 paired runs, although the fitted reference
retains the higher mean by eliminating a few severe failures that remain under
the sparse rule.  The comparison supports $0.1$ as a practical heuristic
without presenting it as the risk-minimizing boundary or a new optimizer.

\paragraph{The conditional intervention preserves early progress.}
Figure~\ref{fig:ess-validation}(c) shows that the intervention follows the
permissive trajectory while ESS is high, masks $5.17$ of 128 updates on average,
and reaches twice the initial reward after 6,144 verifier responses, compared
with 8,192 for raw and 10,240 for PPO masking.  Final exact rewards are
$0.2826\pm0.0109$, $0.2952\pm0.0014$, and $0.2954\pm0.0098$, respectively.
The endpoint is not statistically resolved above always masking; the result is
that reliability conditioning recovers its endpoint after following the faster
permissive path through the high-ESS regime.
