\title{\bf Rethinking Clipping in RLVR:\\
Sequence ESS Governs the Reliability of Unclipped Updates}
\author{Anonymous authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards often takes several optimization
steps on fixed rollout data.  Clipping is commonly treated as necessary once
the policy moves away from those data, but update count alone does not determine
whether an unclipped update is reliable.  We show that reliability depends on
both policy coverage and the influence of poorly covered samples.  Effective
sample size connects the first quantity to gradient-estimation error, while the
second determines when that connection is informative.  This view separates
diagnosis from intervention: clipping helps only when the variance it removes
exceeds the bias it introduces.  On an established classification-based
contextual-bandit testbed, observed error follows this joint prediction, whereas
effective sample size alone does not order estimator quality.  A clipping rule
calibrated on separate data helps only in the regimes predicted by the
bias--variance comparison.  Validation in language-model training remains open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

RLVR improves data efficiency by taking several policy updates on fixed rollout
data.  As the policy changes, however, those responses become less
representative of its current behavior.  The central practical question is
therefore not how many optimization steps have elapsed, but whether the rollout
distribution still supports a reliable update.

Ratio clipping is the standard answer in PPO-style training
\citep{schulman2017ppo}.  Its usual justification treats an unclipped update as
increasingly unstable as the policy moves away from the rollout distribution.
Recent work has questioned the ratio itself as a measure of harmful policy
change \citep{qi2026dppo}, while
adaptive methods track how concentrated off-policy weights have become
\citep{zheng2025m2po,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}.  These methods expose a
logically prior question.  Before choosing how to regulate an update, one must
determine whether the unclipped update has actually become unreliable.

The optimization step count cannot answer that question.  Two rollout datasets
observed after the same number of updates can differ sharply in how well they
represent the current policy.
One may remain broadly informative, while the other may be dominated by only a
few responses.  Sequence effective sample size (ESS) captures this difference
and provides a more meaningful notion of coverage than update count alone.

We show that ESS supplies the missing theoretical bridge, but not the whole
answer.  It connects policy coverage to estimation error after accounting for
the scale of the gradient contributions attached to large ratios.  Data
collected many steps earlier can therefore remain reliable, while more recent
data can already be poor.  Conversely, high ESS does not guarantee low error if
the influential samples carry unusually large gradients.  This qualification
is essential to both the theorem and its empirical test.

This diagnosis changes the role of clipping.  Clipping is not what defines a
stable update.  It limits the influence of extreme responses, but also changes
the update in a systematic way.  We make this tradeoff explicit: clipping is
preferred exactly when its covariance reduction exceeds its squared bias.
Specific cap values have no theorem-level status and must be calibrated rather
than treated as a contribution.  Verifier rewards remain helpful because they
bound one source of gradient scale.

We test these claims using the classification-to-contextual-bandit protocol of
\citet{wang2017adaptive} on the UCI Optdigits data
\citep{alpaydin1998optdigits}.  This established construction supplies real
contexts, ten actions, bounded rewards, and a known finite population, so the
true gradient and every theoretical moment can be calculated.  Two logger
families vary coverage and gradient scale separately.  Across 100 independent
batches per condition, we report predicted and observed raw-gradient MSE, the
gradient scale against ESS, and held-out clipped-to-raw MSE.  The study tests the
theory directly without claiming to settle language-model training.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item We identify sequence ESS as the bridge between policy coverage and the
reliability of an unclipped update.
\item We characterize when clipping is worth the distortion it introduces and
show that the decision cannot be reduced to a universal ESS or cap.
\item We validate the quantitative prediction on a contextual-bandit benchmark
adopted from prior off-policy evaluation work.
\end{enumerate}

The next section makes the setting precise and separates population correctness
from finite-sample reliability.

\section{Problem formulation and sequence coverage}
\label{sec:setting}

We first separate the target of an update from the accuracy with which it is
estimated.  Let $X\sim\nu$ be a prompt, let $Y$ be a complete response drawn
from the rollout policy $Q$, and let $R=R(X,Y)$ be a terminal verifier reward.
The current policy is $P_\theta$, with $P_\theta\ll Q$.  Their sequence
likelihood ratio is
\begin{equation}
  W_\theta(X,Y)
  :=\frac{P_\theta(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)},
  \qquad
  \E_Q[W_\theta\mid X]=1.
  \label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}

Let $b(X)$ be a detached prompt-only baseline and set $A=R-b(X)$.  Define the
score and the corresponding sequence contribution by
\begin{equation}
  S_\theta:=\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y\mid X),
  \qquad
  H_\theta:=A S_\theta.
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
For $J(\theta):=\E_{P_\theta}[R]$, assume that differentiation and expectation
can be interchanged.  The population gradient follows one identity per line:
\begin{align}
  g(\theta):=\nabla J(\theta)
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[R S_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-1}\\
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[(R-b(X))S_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-2}\\
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[H_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-3}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta].
  \label{eq:true-gradient}
\end{align}
The first line is the score-function identity.  The second uses
$\E_{P_\theta}[b(X)S_\theta\mid X]
=b(X)\nabla_\theta\sum_yP_\theta(y\mid X)=0$.  The third substitutes
\eqref{eq:score}, and the fourth changes measure from $P_\theta$ to $Q$.
Given $N$ independent responses from $Q$, the unclipped estimator is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_W(\theta)
  :=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N W_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}.
  \label{eq:raw-estimator}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:true-gradient} establishes the correct population target.
It does not establish that a finite rollout sample estimates that target
reliably, which is the question addressed next.

\subsection{Sequence coverage}

The second moment of the likelihood ratio measures how well $Q$ covers
$P_\theta$.  Assuming $\E_Q[W_\theta^2]<\infty$, define normalized sequence ESS
by
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\theta:=\frac{1}{\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
  =\exp\{-D_2(P_\theta\|Q)\}.
  \label{eq:population-ess}
\end{equation}
Because $\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$, its relation to ratio dispersion is exact:
\begin{align}
  \E_Q[(W_\theta-1)^2]
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta^2]-2\E_Q[W_\theta]+1
    \label{eq:weight-variance-proof-1}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta^2]-1
    \label{eq:weight-variance-proof-2}\\
  &=\rho_\theta^{-1}-1.
    \label{eq:weight-variance}
\end{align}
The first line expands the square, the second uses $\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$, and the
third uses \eqref{eq:population-ess}.  Thus $N\rho_\theta$ is the population
effective sequence count, consistent with the second-moment coverage principle
used in importance-sampling policy optimization
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  Coverage is scalar, whereas the update
is a vector, so the next section establishes the exact bridge between them.

\section{Main result: ESS governs finite-sample reliability}
\label{sec:raw}

Population correctness does not prevent a small number of responses from
dominating a finite-sample update.  To separate ratio concentration from the
scale of the associated gradient contributions, define
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}
  :=\frac{\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]}
          {\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}.
  \label{eq:gradient-scale}
\end{equation}
The quantity $G_{2,\theta}$ is a weighted gradient scale.  It records whether
large likelihood ratios are attached to large gradient contributions.  With
this qualifier explicit, the main theorem states the reliability bridge in one
step.

\begin{theorem}[ESS reliability bridge]
\label{thm:raw-mse}
Assume $\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]<\infty$.  For fixed $\theta$,
$\widehat g_W$ is unbiased and
\begin{align}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  &=\frac1N\left\{
    \E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right\}
  \label{eq:raw-mse}\\
  &=\frac1N\left(
    \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{\rho_\theta}-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right)
  \label{eq:ess-factorization}\\
  &\le \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}.
  \label{eq:ess-bridge}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $Z_i=W_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}$ and let $g=g(\theta)$.  Unbiasedness follows
line by line from identical sampling:
\begin{align}
  \E[\widehat g_W]
  &=\E\left[\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N Z_i\right]
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-1}\\
  &=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\E[Z_i]
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-2}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta]
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-3}\\
  &=g.
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-4}
\end{align}
For the MSE, center each contribution before expanding the squared norm:
\begin{align}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  &=\E\left\|
    \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N(Z_i-g)
    \right\|_2^2
    \label{eq:raw-proof-1}\\
  &=\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i=1}^N\E\|Z_i-g\|_2^2
    +\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i\ne j}
      \E[(Z_i-g)^\top(Z_j-g)]
    \label{eq:raw-proof-2}\\
  &=\frac1N\E\|Z_1-g\|_2^2
    \label{eq:raw-proof-3}\\
  &=\frac1N\left\{
    \E\|Z_1\|_2^2-2g^\top\E[Z_1]+\|g\|_2^2
    \right\}
    \label{eq:raw-proof-4}\\
  &=\frac1N\left\{
    \E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]-\|g\|_2^2
    \right\}
    \label{eq:raw-proof-5}\\
  &=\frac1N\left(
    \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{\rho_\theta}-\|g\|_2^2
    \right)
    \label{eq:raw-proof-6}\\
  &\le\frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}.
    \label{eq:raw-proof-7}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:unbiased-proof-1} substitutes the estimator definition.
Equation~\eqref{eq:unbiased-proof-2} uses linearity of expectation, and
\eqref{eq:unbiased-proof-3} uses identical sampling.  The last line substitutes
\eqref{eq:true-gradient}.  For the MSE, \eqref{eq:raw-proof-1} uses
$\widehat g_W-g=N^{-1}\sum_i(Z_i-g)$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:raw-proof-2} expands
the squared norm.  Independence and $\E[Z_i-g]=0$ make every cross term vanish;
identical sampling then gives \eqref{eq:raw-proof-3}.  Equation
\eqref{eq:raw-proof-4} expands the remaining squared norm.  Finally,
\eqref{eq:raw-proof-5} substitutes $\E[Z_1]=g$ and
$\|Z_1\|_2^2=W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:raw-proof-6} uses \eqref{eq:gradient-scale} and
\eqref{eq:population-ess}.  Equation~\eqref{eq:raw-proof-7} drops the
nonpositive term $-\|g\|_2^2/N$.
\end{proof}

Equation~\eqref{eq:ess-bridge} is the main theoretical bridge.  Sequence ESS
governs the error contributed by ratio concentration, while $G_{2,\theta}$
records whether large ratios coincide with large gradient contributions.  When
$G_{2,\theta}$ is controlled and $N\rho_\theta$ is large, the upper bound remains
small.  Policy drift or update count alone therefore does not imply estimator
failure.  Falling ESS instead identifies the regime in which a
variance-reducing estimator may become worth considering.

\subsection{Controlling the weighted gradient scale in RLVR}

The main theorem is informative only when its remaining factor is controlled.
Advantage bounds are
useful here, but they cannot control $G_{2,\theta}$ without a corresponding
condition on the score.

\begin{proposition}[A sufficient bound on $G_{2,\theta}$]
\label{prop:g2-bound}
If $|A|\le B_A$ and $\|S_\theta\|_2\le B_S$ almost surely under $Q$, then
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}\le B_A^2B_S^2.
  \label{eq:g2-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $H_\theta=A S_\theta$, substitute its squared norm into
\eqref{eq:gradient-scale} and apply one bound per line:
\begin{align}
  G_{2,\theta}
  &=\frac{\E_Q[W_\theta^2 A^2\|S_\theta\|_2^2]}
          {\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
    \label{eq:g2-proof-1}\\
  &\le B_A^2
    \frac{\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|S_\theta\|_2^2]}
         {\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
    \label{eq:g2-proof-2}\\
  &\le B_A^2B_S^2.
    \label{eq:g2-proof-3}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:g2-proof-2} uses $A^2\le B_A^2$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:g2-proof-3} uses $\|S_\theta\|_2^2\le B_S^2$ under the same
nonnegative weight $W_\theta^2$, then cancels $\E_Q[W_\theta^2]$.
\end{proof}

The advantage side is favorable in common RLVR constructions.  If the verifier
reward is binary and a prompt-only baseline satisfies $b(X)\in[0,1]$, then
$|A|=|R-b(X)|\le1$.  Outcome-level GRPO instead standardizes rewards within a
group of $K$ responses \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}.  Its standardized
advantages also have a deterministic finite-group bound whenever their sample
variance is nonzero.

\begin{proposition}[Standardized group-advantage bound]
\label{prop:grpo-bound}
Let $d_i=r_i-\bar r$, let $v=K^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^K d_j^2>0$, and let
$A_i=d_i/\sqrt v$.  Then $|A_i|\le\sqrt{K-1}$ for every $i$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The centered deviations satisfy $d_i=-\sum_{j\ne i}d_j$.  Therefore,
\begin{align}
  d_i^2
  &=\left(\sum_{j\ne i}d_j\right)^2
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-1}\\
  &\le (K-1)\sum_{j\ne i}d_j^2
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-2}\\
  &=(K-1)\left(\sum_{j=1}^K d_j^2-d_i^2\right).
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-3}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:grpo-proof-1} uses the zero-sum identity for centered
deviations.  Equation~\eqref{eq:grpo-proof-2} is Cauchy--Schwarz, and
\eqref{eq:grpo-proof-3} restores the omitted term.  Rearranging gives
\begin{align}
  Kd_i^2
  &\le (K-1)\sum_{j=1}^K d_j^2
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-4}\\
  \frac{d_i^2}{v}
  &\le K-1,
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-5}
\end{align}
where the second line uses $Kv=\sum_jd_j^2$.  Taking square roots proves the
claim.  Adding a positive stabilizer to the denominator can only decrease the
magnitude.
\end{proof}

These bounds isolate what advantage normalization can and cannot establish.
They control the advantage factor, while a bound or empirical estimate of the
score factor remains necessary.  Moreover, group normalization couples the $K$
responses, so Theorem~\ref{thm:raw-mse} does not apply response by response without a
group-level sampling analysis.  The binary-reward, prompt-baseline case obeys
the theorem directly; the GRPO bound supplies scale information rather than an
i.i.d. justification.

\begin{remark}[Pointwise scope]
The expectation is over a fresh batch at fixed $\theta$.  During same-batch
optimization, $\theta$ depends on the fixed rollout data.  Extending
\eqref{eq:ess-bridge} uniformly over that adaptive path requires additional
stability or complexity control.
\end{remark}

The raw risk is now explicit, but an estimator error matters only through its
effect on policy optimization.  The next section supplies that link before we
compare unclipped and clipped estimators.

\section{Why gradient reliability matters for policy optimization}
\label{sec:optimization}

The main theorem characterizes gradient error.  The next result shows how that
error enters a standard first-order optimization guarantee.

\begin{proposition}[MSE-controlled stationarity]
\label{prop:stationarity}
Suppose $J$ is $L$-smooth and bounded above by $J_{\max}$.  For
$\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k+\eta\widehat g_k$ with $0<\eta\le 1/L$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac1T\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\E\|\nabla J(\theta_k)\|_2^2
  \le
  \frac{2\{J_{\max}-\E J(\theta_0)\}}{\eta T}
  +\frac1T\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}
   \E\|\widehat g_k-\nabla J(\theta_k)\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:stationarity}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $g_k:=\nabla J(\theta_k)$ and $e_k:=\widehat g_k-g_k$.  The ascent form of
$L$-smoothness gives
\begin{align}
  J(\theta_{k+1})-J(\theta_k)
  &\ge g_k^\top(\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k)
    -\frac L2\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k\|_2^2
    \label{eq:stationarity-proof-1}\\
  &=\eta g_k^\top\widehat g_k
    -\frac{L\eta^2}{2}\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2
    \label{eq:stationarity-proof-2}\\
  &=\frac\eta2\|g_k\|_2^2
    +\frac\eta2\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2
    -\frac\eta2\|e_k\|_2^2
    -\frac{L\eta^2}{2}\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2
    \label{eq:stationarity-proof-3}\\
  &=\frac\eta2\|g_k\|_2^2
    +\frac\eta2(1-L\eta)\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2
    -\frac\eta2\|e_k\|_2^2
    \label{eq:stationarity-proof-4}\\
  &\ge\frac\eta2\|g_k\|_2^2
    -\frac\eta2\|e_k\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:stationarity-proof-5}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:stationarity-proof-2} substitutes the update.  Equation
\eqref{eq:stationarity-proof-3} uses
$2g_k^\top\widehat g_k=\|g_k\|_2^2+\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2-
\|e_k\|_2^2$.  The last line uses $\eta\le1/L$.

Rearranging \eqref{eq:stationarity-proof-5}, taking expectations, and summing
over $k$ gives
\begin{align}
  \frac\eta2\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\E\|g_k\|_2^2
  &\le \E[J(\theta_T)-J(\theta_0)]
    +\frac\eta2\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\E\|e_k\|_2^2
    \label{eq:stationarity-proof-6}\\
  &\le J_{\max}-\E J(\theta_0)
    +\frac\eta2\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\E\|e_k\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:stationarity-proof-7}
\end{align}
The second line uses the upper bound on $J$.  Dividing by $\eta T/2$ yields
\eqref{eq:stationarity}.
\end{proof}

Lower gradient MSE therefore tightens the statistical term in
\eqref{eq:stationarity}.  The proposition does not analyze Adam or imply a
global ordering of training trajectories.  It supplies the limited bridge
needed here: if clipping reduces MSE, it improves this first-order guarantee;
if clipping increases MSE, it worsens it.  This use of MSE is consistent with
analyses of biased stochastic gradients \cite{driggs2022biased,tang2022biased},
while persistent clipping bias can still create an error floor
\cite{ajalloeian2020biased,koloskova2023clipping}.

The optimization consequence reduces the intervention question to an estimator
comparison.  We now ask exactly when the variance removed by clipping is worth
the bias it introduces.

\section{When clipping improves the estimator}
\label{sec:clipping}

Let $U_\theta=U(W_\theta)$ be a bounded, detached sequence coefficient.  The
modified estimator
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_U:=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N U_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}
  \label{eq:clipped-estimator}
\end{equation}
has gradient bias and one-sample covariances
\begin{equation}
  b_U:=\E_Q[(U_\theta-W_\theta)H_\theta],
  \quad
  \Sigma_W:=\Cov_Q(W_\theta H_\theta),
  \quad
  \Sigma_U:=\Cov_Q(U_\theta H_\theta).
  \label{eq:bias-covariance}
\end{equation}
Detachment matters: differentiating a scalar expression containing
$U(W_\theta)$ generally produces additional chain-rule terms.

\begin{theorem}[Exact clipping crossover]
\label{thm:crossover}
For fixed $\theta$ and finite second moments,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  =\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_U)
  =\|b_U\|_2^2+\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U).
  \label{eq:two-risks}
\end{equation}
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_U)
  <\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  \iff
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U)
  >N\|b_U\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $\mu_U:=\E[\widehat g_U]$.  Its displacement from the target is
\begin{align}
  \mu_U-g
  &=\E_Q[U_\theta H_\theta]-\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta]
    \label{eq:modified-bias-proof-1}\\
  &=\E_Q[(U_\theta-W_\theta)H_\theta]
    \label{eq:modified-bias-proof-2}\\
  &=b_U.
    \label{eq:modified-bias-proof-3}
\end{align}
Centering the modified estimator at $\mu_U$ now yields
\begin{align}
  \E\|\widehat g_U-g\|_2^2
  &=\E\|\widehat g_U-\mu_U+b_U\|_2^2
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-1}\\
  &=\E\|\widehat g_U-\mu_U\|_2^2
    +2b_U^\top\E[\widehat g_U-\mu_U]
    +\|b_U\|_2^2
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-2}\\
  &=\E\|\widehat g_U-\mu_U\|_2^2+\|b_U\|_2^2
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-3}\\
  &=\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U)+\|b_U\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-4}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:modified-bias-proof-1}--\eqref{eq:modified-bias-proof-3}
substitute $g=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta]$, use linearity, and invoke the definition
of $b_U$, respectively.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-1} adds and
subtracts $\mu_U$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-2} expands the
squared norm.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-3} uses
$\E[\widehat g_U-\mu_U]=0$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-4} uses
independence of the $N$ sampled responses, exactly as in
\eqref{eq:raw-proof-2}--\eqref{eq:raw-proof-3}.  The raw estimator is unbiased by
Theorem~\ref{thm:raw-mse}, so the same covariance calculation gives
\begin{equation}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  =\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W).
  \label{eq:raw-risk-crossover-proof}
\end{equation}
Subtracting the two risks gives
\begin{align}
  &\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_U)
    -\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
    \label{eq:crossover-proof-1}\\
  &\quad=\|b_U\|_2^2
    -\frac1N\left\{
      \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U)
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:crossover-proof-2}
\end{align}
The left side is negative exactly when the covariance reduction exceeds
$N\|b_U\|_2^2$, which is \eqref{eq:crossover}.
\end{proof}

The exact crossover still contains estimator-specific moments.  One-sided
upper truncation makes those moments interpretable.  For $c>0$, define
\begin{equation}
  U_c:=\min(W_\theta,c),
  \qquad
  T_c:=(W_\theta-c)_+,
  \qquad
  \tau_c:=\E_Q[T_c].
  \label{eq:upper-truncation}
\end{equation}
The cap changes only ratios above $c$, while $\tau_c$ measures how much target
mass those changes remove.

\begin{corollary}[Bias and MSE of upper ratio truncation]
\label{cor:upper-truncation}
Suppose $\|H_\theta\|_2\le H_{\max}$ almost surely.  The detached estimator
$\widehat g_c=N^{-1}\sum_i U_{c,i}H_{\theta,i}$ satisfies
\begin{align}
  b_c&=-\E_Q[T_cH_\theta],
  &\|b_c\|_2&\le H_{\max}\tau_c,
  \label{eq:cap-bias}\\
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_c)
  &\le H_{\max}^2\left(\tau_c^2+\frac{c}{N}\right).
  \label{eq:cap-mse-bound}
\end{align}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The coefficient difference is $U_c-W_\theta=-T_c$.  Substitution into
\eqref{eq:bias-covariance} gives
\begin{align}
  b_c
  &=\E_Q[(U_c-W_\theta)H_\theta]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-1}\\
  &=-\E_Q[T_cH_\theta].
    \label{eq:cap-proof-2}
\end{align}
The triangle inequality and the bound on $H_\theta$ then give
\begin{align}
  \|b_c\|_2
  &\le\E_Q[T_c\|H_\theta\|_2]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-3}\\
  &\le H_{\max}\E_Q[T_c]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-4}\\
  &=H_{\max}\tau_c.
    \label{eq:cap-proof-5}
\end{align}
For the covariance term, use the trace identity and remove its nonnegative
mean-squared term:
\begin{align}
  \operatorname{tr}\{\Cov_Q(U_cH_\theta)\}
  &=\E_Q[U_c^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]
    -\|\E_Q[U_cH_\theta]\|_2^2
    \label{eq:cap-proof-6}\\
  &\le H_{\max}^2\E_Q[U_c^2]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-7}\\
  &\le cH_{\max}^2\E_Q[W_\theta]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-8}\\
  &=cH_{\max}^2.
    \label{eq:cap-proof-9}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cap-proof-7} uses the bound on $H_\theta$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:cap-proof-8} uses $U_c^2\le cW_\theta$, which holds both
when $W_\theta\le c$ and when $W_\theta>c$.  The final line uses
$\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$.  Combining \eqref{eq:two-risks},
\eqref{eq:cap-proof-5}, and \eqref{eq:cap-proof-9} proves
\eqref{eq:cap-mse-bound}.
\end{proof}

The bound explains when upper truncation can be useful.  It replaces the raw
second-moment dependence by $c/N$, while its bias is small when the tail excess
$\tau_c$ is small.  The cap is therefore an estimator parameter to calibrate,
not a scientific claim.  Truncated importance ratios also appear in ACER with
bias correction \citep{wang2017acer} and in the V-trace correction used by
IMPALA \citep{espeholt2018impala}; recent LLM methods likewise control extreme
weight moments \citep{zheng2025m2po,fakoor2026p3o}.  These precedents motivate
the intervention, while Corollary~\ref{cor:upper-truncation} states the bias quantity that
must be measured for this estimator.

The ESS identity and crossover produce a conditional conclusion.  When $G_{2,\theta}$ is
controlled, high ESS keeps the raw ratio-driven error small and leaves little
variance for clipping to remove.  Falling ESS enlarges the ratio second moment
and can make variance reduction worth its bias.  But ESS cannot determine the
sign of \eqref{eq:crossover}, because the decision also depends on
$G_{2,\theta}$ and on what the clipping rule removes.
The claim is therefore not ``clip below a universal ESS,'' but ``ESS locates
the regime in which the clipping MSE comparison becomes consequential.''

\section{From theory to an ESS-guided estimator rule}
\label{sec:practice}

Population ESS is unavailable during training.  Its plug-in estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho
  :=\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^N W_i)^2}{N\sum_{i=1}^N W_i^2}.
  \label{eq:sample-ess}
\end{equation}
It should be computed from raw, pre-clipping sequence ratios.  A candidate gate
uses the low-bias objective while $\widehat\rho$ is high and switches to a
bounded objective when it falls below a calibrated threshold.

This rule is a hypothesis, not a consequence of
Theorem~\ref{thm:crossover}.  First, sample ESS can miss unobserved tail mass.  Second,
the same batch usually determines both the gate and the gradient.  Third,
a token-local surrogate is not algebraically equal to
\eqref{eq:raw-estimator}.  Recent LLM RL methods
already adapt updates using ESS, weight moments, or sequence reshaping
\cite{zheng2025m2po,shen2026vespo,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}; our distinct
empirical question is whether their relevant regime change coincides with the
gradient-MSE crossover in \eqref{eq:crossover}.

\paragraph{Candidate rule.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Aggressive estimator: use the detached raw sequence ratio $W_i$ in
\eqref{eq:raw-estimator}.
\item Conservative estimator: replace $W_i$ by the detached coefficient
$\min(W_i,c)$, with $c$ selected from a prespecified grid on held-out data.
\item Calibration: choose a gate on held-out runs by locating the measured MSE
crossover, then monitor raw $\widehat\rho$, estimated tail excess, and a
weighted score-scale proxy.  Freeze both the gate and cap before evaluation.
\end{itemize}

The rule makes the estimator choice reproducible without claiming a universal
ESS threshold.  The controlled study below tests the identities that motivate
it before considering an LLM-scale gate.

\section{Empirical validation of the theory}
\label{sec:experiments}

The experiment must validate the quantities in the theory rather than an
arbitrary bandit outcome.  It therefore asks three questions in order.  First,
does observed raw-gradient MSE match the exact factorization in
\eqref{eq:ess-factorization}?  Second, can two logging regimes with similar ESS
have different error because their weighted gradient scales differ?  Third,
after choosing a cap on separate calibration batches, does clipping reduce MSE
only when variance reduction pays for its bias?  These questions test the
reliability bridge, its qualification, and the clipping crossover, respectively.

We adopt the classification-to-contextual-bandit protocol used by
\citet{wang2017adaptive} for off-policy evaluation.  Each Optdigits image is a
context, the ten labels are actions, and choosing the correct label yields unit
reward \citep{alpaydin1998optdigits}.  This is useful here because the empirical
distribution over all 5,620 labeled examples is a known finite population.
Consequently, the target gradient, ESS, weighted gradient scale, clipping bias,
and predicted MSE can all be computed exactly before drawing any batches.  We
compare aligned loggers with loggers trained under covariate shift, then vary
their temperatures to change coverage.  Prior importance-sampling policy
optimization studies likewise report ESS and weight dispersion when diagnosing
off-policy reliability \citep{metelli2018pois}; our test adds the gradient-scale
and MSE measurements required by the present theorem.

For every logger, we report predicted raw MSE, empirical raw MSE, weighted
gradient scale, and the ratio of calibrated clipped MSE to raw MSE.  Results use
100 independent batches of 256 interactions.  The first 40 batches select a cap
from a fixed grid, and the remaining 60 evaluate it.  Thus no particular cap is
part of the hypothesis, and the evaluation data do not choose the estimator.
Full policy construction, estimators, and numerical results appear in
Appendix~\ref{app:bandit}.

\section{Optdigits contextual-bandit protocol}
\label{app:bandit}

\subsection{Established testbed and data}

The protocol follows the classification reduction used by
\citet{dudik2011doubly,wang2017adaptive}.  A labeled example is a context, the
class labels are actions, and the reward equals one exactly when the selected
action is the true label.  These studies use the construction because logged
bandit feedback can be sampled while the full labeled data still reveal the
target value.  We use the same advantage for gradient evaluation: the full
empirical distribution also reveals the target gradient and all
importance-weight moments.  The 2011 study included Optdigits in its UCI
classification benchmark and used 30 repeated policy-learning runs; it also
used 100 repetitions in a separate covariate-shift study
\citep{dudik2011doubly}.  Our 100-repetition limit therefore remains consistent
with established contextual-bandit practice.

The Optdigits data contain $M=5{,}620$ examples, 64 pixel-aggregation features,
and ten digit classes \citep{alpaydin1998optdigits}.  Features are divided by 16
and augmented with an intercept.  We combine the original training and test
files and place uniform mass on the resulting empirical population.  This step
does not estimate predictive performance.  It defines a finite contextual
bandit on which population quantities are exactly computable.

\subsection{Target and logging policies}

We fit a ten-class linear softmax classifier to the finite population using 500
full-batch gradient steps with learning rate $0.8$.  Temperature $0.75$ defines
the stochastic target policy $P_\theta$.  For feature vector $x$ and action $a$,
its score with respect to the temperature-scaled classifier parameters is
\begin{equation}
  S_\theta(x,a)=\{e_a-P_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\}\otimes x.
  \label{eq:optdigits-score}
\end{equation}
The reward is $R(x,a)=\mathbb{1}\{a=y(x)\}$, and the prompt-only baseline is the
target policy's probability of the correct label.  Hence $|A|\le1$, matching the
bounded-reward regime discussed in Proposition~\ref{prop:g2-bound}.

The aligned logger uses the same classifier at temperatures
$0.75,1,1.5,2.5,$ and $4$.  The shifted logger follows the covariate-shift idea
in \citet{wang2017adaptive}: a second classifier is fit after weighting each
example by
\begin{equation}
  0.1+0.9\{1+\exp(-2z)\}^{-1},
  \label{eq:shift-weight}
\end{equation}
where $z$ is a standardized random projection of its features.  The same five
temperatures are applied to this classifier.  Every logger is mixed with five
percent uniform exploration to guarantee support.  The aligned family mainly
varies concentration around the target decision boundary, while the shifted
family also changes which contexts carry large ratios.  Comparing them prevents
the experiment from building the conclusion into a one-dimensional ESS sweep.

\subsection{Population quantities and sampled estimators}

For each logger $Q$, contexts are uniform over the $M$ examples and actions are
drawn from $Q(\cdot\mid x)$.  We enumerate all $M\times10$ context-action pairs
to calculate
\begin{align}
  g
  &=\frac1M\sum_{x=1}^M\sum_{a=1}^{10}
    P_\theta(a\mid x)H_\theta(x,a),
    \label{eq:optdigits-gradient}\\
  \rho_\theta^{-1}
  &=\frac1M\sum_{x=1}^M\sum_{a=1}^{10}
    Q(a\mid x)W_\theta(x,a)^2,
    \label{eq:optdigits-rho}\\
  G_{2,\theta}
  &=\rho_\theta\frac1M\sum_{x=1}^M\sum_{a=1}^{10}
    Q(a\mid x)W_\theta(x,a)^2\|H_\theta(x,a)\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:optdigits-g2}
\end{align}
Substitution into \eqref{eq:ess-factorization} gives the predicted raw MSE
without Monte Carlo approximation.

We then draw 100 independent batches with $N=256$.  For each batch we compute
the squared error of the raw gradient and of upper-truncated estimators with
caps in the prespecified grid
\begin{equation}
  \{1.25,1.5,2,3,5,10,20,\infty\}.
  \label{eq:cap-grid}
\end{equation}
The first 40 batches select the lowest-MSE cap separately for each logger.  The
remaining 60 batches estimate raw MSE and selected-cap MSE, with standard errors
computed across batches.  The grid includes the raw estimator, so calibration
can decline to clip.  The numerical cap is not compared across conditions and
is not interpreted as a result.

Table~\ref{tab:bandit-full-results} reports representative conditions.  The first

shifted logger has a much larger weighted gradient scale and 65 percent higher
raw MSE.  The calibrated estimator is unchanged for the aligned logger but
