\title{\bf When Should We Clip in RLVR?\\
Effective Sample Size as a Reliability Criterion}
\author{Dongkyu Derek Cho, Thomas Voice}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Clipping is widely used to stabilize reinforcement learning from fixed rollout
data, but it can also discard useful learning signal.  This tension is
especially visible in language-model training, where permissive updates often
learn faster but can fail after the policy moves too far from its data.  We
argue that clipping should therefore be triggered by the reliability of the
data, not applied throughout training.  Effective sample size provides this
reliability criterion by measuring how broadly the current policy is supported
by the rollout batch.  Our analysis connects effective sample size to gradient
error, connects gradient error to policy improvement, and casts clipping as a
bias--variance trade-off rather than a universal stability requirement.  This
view leads to a simple rule: retain the efficient update while the data remain
reliable, and clip only after reliability deteriorates.  A controlled
contextual-bandit study validates the full chain and shows that a prespecified
gate improves over both always clipping and never clipping.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Clipping is treated as a basic safeguard in policy optimization.  RLVR methods
draw an expensive rollout batch, take several updates on that batch, and use a
clipped objective to limit the effect of the resulting policy drift
\citep{schulman2017ppo,shao2024deepseekmath}.  The usual intuition is simple:
once likelihood ratios move away from one, an unrestricted update is unsafe.
That intuition has made clipping nearly synonymous with stable training.

Language-model training exposes a conflict with this conventional view.
Clipping can mask precisely the low-probability tokens that carry new reasoning
behavior, which slows early learning and can accelerate entropy collapse
\citep{yu2025dapo,park2025clipping}.  More permissive objectives respond by
raising the upper clipping threshold or by retaining gradients outside the PPO
interval; CISPO, for example, clips importance weights without zeroing those
gradients \citep{minimax2025m1}.  These methods can learn efficiently, yet a
fully permissive run may later deteriorate when a small fraction of responses
dominates the update.  Clipping is therefore neither always necessary nor
always harmful.  The unresolved question is when its bias becomes worth paying.

Existing clipping rules do not answer this question because they monitor the
intervention rather than the reliability problem.  A token ratio says how one
sample changed, and an update count says how long a batch has been optimized.
Neither says whether the batch still contains enough independent support for
the current gradient.  Divergence-based constraints improve how policy change
is measured \citep{qi2026dppo}, while recent adaptive methods monitor weight
concentration \citep{zheng2025m2po,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}.  What is still
missing is a direct bridge from that concentration to optimization quality.

We provide this bridge through sequence effective sample size (ESS).  ESS is
high when the update is supported across many responses and low when a few
responses carry most of the importance weight.  Our central claim is that this
quantity governs when an unclipped update is statistically reliable.  High ESS
supports the unbiased and efficient update.  Low ESS raises its gradient error,
creating the regime in which a biased clipping mechanism can improve the
bias--variance trade-off.

The theory turns this claim into a decision chain.  First, we factor the exact
mean-squared error of the unclipped gradient into sequence ESS and a weighted
gradient scale.  Second, we show that lower gradient error strengthens a
one-step policy-improvement guarantee.  Third, we differentiate the PPO
objective and show that clipping is an advantage-sign gradient mask, whereas
ratio truncation is a different estimator.  This distinction lets us compare
unclipped and clipped updates by their gradient MSE and yields the practical
rule suggested by Figure~\ref{fig:llm-motivation}: do not clip while ESS is high;
introduce the chosen safeguard only after ESS falls.

We test the mechanism before returning to large language models.  An
enumerable contextual bandit lets us vary coverage while calculating the exact
gradient and exact population reward.  Across 100 batches per condition, high
ESS predicts low gradient error, low error predicts a better policy step, and a
prespecified $0.1$ gate improves over both always-clipped and always-unclipped
updates.  Section~\ref{sec:llm-experiments} then specifies the decisive RLVR
test: apply the same gate to conventional PPO clipping, Clip-Higher, and a DPPO
constraint, and report whether each reliability-gated variant improves the
corresponding always-constrained baseline.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item We identify sequence ESS as the missing reliability criterion for fixed
rollout optimization and derive its exact connection to gradient MSE.
\item We connect gradient MSE to policy improvement and characterize clipping
as a biased variance-reduction decision rather than an unconditional safeguard.
\item We validate the complete mechanism in a controlled bandit and formulate a
matched LLM evaluation across conventional clipping, Clip-Higher, and DPPO.
\end{enumerate}

The paper follows the same argument in order.  Section~\ref{sec:setting}
defines the fixed-rollout gradient and sequence ESS.  Section~\ref{sec:theory}
develops the reliability and clipping analysis.  Section~\ref{sec:simulation}
isolates the predicted mechanism, and Section~\ref{sec:llm-experiments} tests
whether that mechanism improves RLVR training.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:setting}

\subsection{Fixed-rollout policy gradient}

We first separate the target of an update from the accuracy with which it is
estimated.  Let $X\sim\nu$ be a prompt, let $Y$ be a complete response drawn
from the rollout policy $Q$, and let $R=R(X,Y)$ be a terminal verifier reward.
The current policy is $P_\theta$, with $P_\theta\ll Q$.  Their sequence
likelihood ratio is
\begin{equation}
  W_\theta(X,Y)
  :=\frac{P_\theta(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)},
  \qquad
  \E_Q[W_\theta\mid X]=1.
  \label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}

Let $b(X)$ be a detached prompt-only baseline and set $A=R-b(X)$.  Define the
score and the corresponding sequence contribution by
\begin{equation}
  S_\theta:=\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y\mid X),
  \qquad
  H_\theta:=A S_\theta.
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
For $J(\theta):=\E_{P_\theta}[R]$, assume that differentiation and expectation
can be interchanged.  The population gradient follows one identity per line:
\begin{align}
  g(\theta):=\nabla J(\theta)
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[R S_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-1}\\
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[(R-b(X))S_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-2}\\
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[H_\theta]
    \label{eq:policy-gradient-3}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta].
  \label{eq:true-gradient}
\end{align}
The first line is the score-function identity.  The second uses
$\E_{P_\theta}[b(X)S_\theta\mid X]
=b(X)\nabla_\theta\sum_yP_\theta(y\mid X)=0$.  The third substitutes
\eqref{eq:score}, and the fourth changes measure from $P_\theta$ to $Q$.
Given $N$ independent responses from $Q$, the unclipped estimator is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_W(\theta)
  :=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N W_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}.
  \label{eq:raw-estimator}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:true-gradient} establishes the correct population target.
It does not establish that a finite rollout sample estimates that target
reliably, which is the question addressed next.

\subsection{Sequence coverage}

The second moment of the likelihood ratio measures how well $Q$ covers
$P_\theta$.  Assuming $\E_Q[W_\theta^2]<\infty$, define normalized sequence ESS
by
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\theta:=\frac{1}{\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
  =\exp\{-D_2(P_\theta\|Q)\}.
  \label{eq:population-ess}
\end{equation}
Because $\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$, its relation to ratio dispersion is exact:
\begin{align}
  \E_Q[(W_\theta-1)^2]
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta^2]-2\E_Q[W_\theta]+1
    \label{eq:weight-variance-proof-1}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta^2]-1
    \label{eq:weight-variance-proof-2}\\
  &=\rho_\theta^{-1}-1.
    \label{eq:weight-variance}
\end{align}
The first line expands the square, the second uses $\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$, and the
third uses \eqref{eq:population-ess}.  Thus $N\rho_\theta$ is the population
effective sequence count, consistent with the second-moment coverage principle
used in importance-sampling policy optimization
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  Coverage is scalar, whereas the update
is a vector, so the next section establishes the exact bridge between them.

\section{Theoretical analysis}
\label{sec:theory}

The analysis answers three questions in the order required by the practical
decision.  We first ask when the fixed-rollout gradient is reliable, then ask
why that reliability matters for policy improvement, and finally ask when
clipping improves the resulting estimator.  These steps turn ESS from a
descriptive statistic into a criterion for choosing the update.

\subsection{ESS and finite-sample reliability}
\label{sec:raw}

Population correctness does not prevent a small number of responses from
dominating a finite-sample update.  To separate ratio concentration from the
scale of the associated gradient contributions, define
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}
  :=\frac{\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]}
          {\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}.
  \label{eq:gradient-scale}
\end{equation}
The quantity $G_{2,\theta}$ is a weighted gradient scale.  It records whether
large likelihood ratios are attached to large gradient contributions.  With
this qualifier explicit, the main theorem states the reliability bridge in one
step.

\begin{theorem}[ESS reliability bridge]
\label{thm:raw-mse}
Assume $\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]<\infty$.  For fixed $\theta$,
$\widehat g_W$ is unbiased and
\begin{align}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  &=\frac1N\left\{
    \E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right\}
  \label{eq:raw-mse}\\
  &=\frac1N\left(
    \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{\rho_\theta}-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right)
  \label{eq:ess-factorization}\\
  &\le \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}.
  \label{eq:ess-bridge}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $Z_i=W_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}$ and let $g=g(\theta)$.  Unbiasedness follows
line by line from identical sampling:
\begin{align}
  \E[\widehat g_W]
  &=\E\left[\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N Z_i\right]
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-1}\\
  &=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\E[Z_i]
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-2}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta]
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-3}\\
  &=g.
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-4}
\end{align}
For the MSE, center each contribution before expanding the squared norm:
\begin{align}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  &=\E\left\|
    \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N(Z_i-g)
    \right\|_2^2
    \label{eq:raw-proof-1}\\
  &=\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i=1}^N\E\|Z_i-g\|_2^2
    +\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i\ne j}
      \E[(Z_i-g)^\top(Z_j-g)]
    \label{eq:raw-proof-2}\\
  &=\frac1N\E\|Z_1-g\|_2^2
    \label{eq:raw-proof-3}\\
  &=\frac1N\left\{
    \E\|Z_1\|_2^2-2g^\top\E[Z_1]+\|g\|_2^2
    \right\}
    \label{eq:raw-proof-4}\\
  &=\frac1N\left\{
    \E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]-\|g\|_2^2
    \right\}
    \label{eq:raw-proof-5}\\
  &=\frac1N\left(
    \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{\rho_\theta}-\|g\|_2^2
    \right)
    \label{eq:raw-proof-6}\\
  &\le\frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}.
    \label{eq:raw-proof-7}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:unbiased-proof-1} substitutes the estimator definition.
Equation~\eqref{eq:unbiased-proof-2} uses linearity of expectation, and
\eqref{eq:unbiased-proof-3} uses identical sampling.  The last line substitutes
\eqref{eq:true-gradient}.  For the MSE, \eqref{eq:raw-proof-1} uses
$\widehat g_W-g=N^{-1}\sum_i(Z_i-g)$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:raw-proof-2} expands
the squared norm.  Independence and $\E[Z_i-g]=0$ make every cross term vanish;
identical sampling then gives \eqref{eq:raw-proof-3}.  Equation
\eqref{eq:raw-proof-4} expands the remaining squared norm.  Finally,
\eqref{eq:raw-proof-5} substitutes $\E[Z_1]=g$ and
$\|Z_1\|_2^2=W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:raw-proof-6} uses \eqref{eq:gradient-scale} and
\eqref{eq:population-ess}.  Equation~\eqref{eq:raw-proof-7} drops the
nonpositive term $-\|g\|_2^2/N$.
\end{proof}

Equation~\eqref{eq:ess-bridge} is the main theoretical bridge.  Sequence ESS
governs the error contributed by ratio concentration, while $G_{2,\theta}$
records whether large ratios coincide with large gradient contributions.  When
$G_{2,\theta}$ is controlled and $N\rho_\theta$ is large, the upper bound remains
small.  Policy drift or update count alone therefore does not imply estimator
failure.  Gradient error would still be only a statistical diagnostic, however,
unless it implied a concrete optimization consequence.  The next result makes
that implication explicit.

\subsection{Gradient MSE and policy improvement}
\label{sec:mse-improvement}

Policy-gradient theory supplies the missing optimization link.  Theorem~7 of
\citet{papini2022smoothing} states that an $L$-smooth policy objective satisfies
the following lower bound for any feasible parameter step $d$:
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta+d)-J(\theta)
  \ge g(\theta)^\top d-\frac{L}{2}\|d\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:smooth-policy-improvement}
\end{equation}
That work uses the bound to construct safe policy-gradient schedules.  Broader
analyses establish convergence from smoothness and gradient second moments
\citep{yuan2022general}.  Off-policy work also uses gradient MSE to assess
efficiency \citep{kallus2020efficient}, while biased-gradient theory shows why
bias and variance must be assessed jointly \citep{driggs2022biased}.  The result below applies
\eqref{eq:smooth-policy-improvement} directly to the possibly biased update
directions created by clipping or truncation.

\begin{theorem}[Gradient MSE controls certified policy improvement]
\label{thm:mse-improvement}
Fix $\theta$ and let $g=g(\theta)$.  Let $\widehat g$ be any, possibly biased,
gradient estimator with finite second moment.  Suppose $J$ is $L$-smooth on
every line segment from $\theta$ to $\theta+\eta\widehat g$, and that these
updates are feasible.  For $0<\eta\le 1/L$,
\begin{equation}
  \E\!\left[J(\theta+\eta\widehat g)-J(\theta)\right]
  \ge \frac{\eta}{2}
  \left\{\|g\|_2^2-\E\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2\right\}.
  \label{eq:mse-improvement}
\end{equation}
Consequently, $\E\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2<\|g\|_2^2$ is sufficient for positive
expected policy improvement.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply \eqref{eq:smooth-policy-improvement} with
$d=\eta\widehat g$ and take expectation:
\begin{align}
  &\E\!\left[J(\theta+\eta\widehat g)-J(\theta)\right]
    \label{eq:mse-improvement-proof-1}\\
  &\quad\ge
    \eta\E[g^\top\widehat g]
    -\frac{L\eta^2}{2}\E\|\widehat g\|_2^2
    \label{eq:mse-improvement-proof-2}\\
  &\quad\ge
    \eta\E[g^\top\widehat g]
    -\frac{\eta}{2}\E\|\widehat g\|_2^2
    \label{eq:mse-improvement-proof-3}\\
  &\quad=
    \frac{\eta}{2}\E\!\left[
      2g^\top\widehat g-\|\widehat g\|_2^2
    \right]
    \label{eq:mse-improvement-proof-4}\\
  &\quad=
    \frac{\eta}{2}\E\!\left[
      \|g\|_2^2-\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2
    \right]
    \label{eq:mse-improvement-proof-5}\\
  &\quad=
    \frac{\eta}{2}
    \left\{\|g\|_2^2-\E\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2\right\}.
    \label{eq:mse-improvement-proof-6}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:mse-improvement-proof-2} uses the cited smoothness bound.
Equation~\eqref{eq:mse-improvement-proof-3} uses $L\eta\le1$ and the
nonnegativity of $\|\widehat g\|_2^2$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:mse-improvement-proof-4} collects the two terms under one
expectation.  Equation~\eqref{eq:mse-improvement-proof-5} uses
$2g^\top\widehat g-\|\widehat g\|_2^2
=\|g\|_2^2-\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2$.  The final line moves the deterministic
quantity $\|g\|_2^2$ outside the expectation.  If the MSE is below
$\|g\|_2^2$, the right side is positive.
\end{proof}

This theorem provides a direct, nonasymptotic meaning for low gradient MSE.
For two estimators used with the same learning rate, reducing MSE by $\delta$
raises the certified improvement lower bound by $\eta\delta/2$.  This statement
orders their guarantees, not necessarily their realized improvements, because
the smoothness lower bound need not be tight.

\begin{corollary}[ESS certificate for an unclipped update]
\label{cor:ess-improvement}
Under Theorems~\ref{thm:raw-mse} and \ref{thm:mse-improvement},
\begin{equation}
  \E\!\left[J(\theta+\eta\widehat g_W)-J(\theta)\right]
  \ge \frac{\eta}{2}
  \left\{
    \left(1+\frac1N\right)\|g\|_2^2
    -\frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:ess-improvement-certificate}
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}<(N+1)\rho_\theta\|g\|_2^2
  \label{eq:positive-ess-update}
\end{equation}
is sufficient for positive expected policy improvement.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Substitute the exact MSE from \eqref{eq:ess-factorization} into
\eqref{eq:mse-improvement}:
\begin{align}
  &\E\!\left[J(\theta+\eta\widehat g_W)-J(\theta)\right]
    \label{eq:ess-improvement-proof-1}\\
  &\quad\ge\frac{\eta}{2}\left[
    \|g\|_2^2
    -\frac1N\left(
      \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{\rho_\theta}-\|g\|_2^2
    \right)
  \right]
    \label{eq:ess-improvement-proof-2}\\
  &\quad=\frac{\eta}{2}\left\{
    \left(1+\frac1N\right)\|g\|_2^2
    -\frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}
  \right\}.
    \label{eq:ess-improvement-proof-3}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ess-improvement-proof-2} applies
Theorem~\ref{thm:mse-improvement}; Equation
\eqref{eq:ess-improvement-proof-3} collects the two gradient-norm terms.  Its
right side is positive exactly when
$G_{2,\theta}<(N+1)\rho_\theta\|g\|_2^2$.
\end{proof}

Corollary~\ref{cor:ess-improvement} closes the theoretical chain.  At fixed
gradient signal and weighted gradient scale, higher ESS strengthens the
one-step improvement certificate.  The certificate does not create a universal
ESS threshold because its boundary also depends on the gradient signal and
$G_{2,\theta}$.  The remaining task is therefore to show when the latter scale
is controlled in RLVR.

\subsection{Controlling the weighted gradient scale in RLVR}

The main theorem is informative only when its remaining factor is controlled.
Advantage bounds are
useful here, but they cannot control $G_{2,\theta}$ without a corresponding
condition on the score.

\begin{proposition}[A sufficient bound on $G_{2,\theta}$]
\label{prop:g2-bound}
If $|A|\le B_A$ and $\|S_\theta\|_2\le B_S$ almost surely under $Q$, then
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}\le B_A^2B_S^2.
  \label{eq:g2-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $H_\theta=A S_\theta$, substitute its squared norm into
\eqref{eq:gradient-scale} and apply one bound per line:
\begin{align}
  G_{2,\theta}
  &=\frac{\E_Q[W_\theta^2 A^2\|S_\theta\|_2^2]}
          {\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
    \label{eq:g2-proof-1}\\
  &\le B_A^2
    \frac{\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|S_\theta\|_2^2]}
         {\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
    \label{eq:g2-proof-2}\\
  &\le B_A^2B_S^2.
    \label{eq:g2-proof-3}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:g2-proof-2} uses $A^2\le B_A^2$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:g2-proof-3} uses $\|S_\theta\|_2^2\le B_S^2$ under the same
nonnegative weight $W_\theta^2$, then cancels $\E_Q[W_\theta^2]$.
\end{proof}

The advantage side is favorable in common RLVR constructions.  If the verifier
reward is binary and a prompt-only baseline satisfies $b(X)\in[0,1]$, then
$|A|=|R-b(X)|\le1$.  Outcome-level GRPO instead standardizes rewards within a
group of $K$ responses \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}.  Its standardized
advantages also have a deterministic finite-group bound whenever their sample
variance is nonzero.

\begin{proposition}[Standardized group-advantage bound]
\label{prop:grpo-bound}
Let $d_i=r_i-\bar r$, let $v=K^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^K d_j^2>0$, and let
$A_i=d_i/\sqrt v$.  Then $|A_i|\le\sqrt{K-1}$ for every $i$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The centered deviations satisfy $d_i=-\sum_{j\ne i}d_j$.  Therefore,
\begin{align}
  d_i^2
  &=\left(\sum_{j\ne i}d_j\right)^2
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-1}\\
  &\le (K-1)\sum_{j\ne i}d_j^2
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-2}\\
  &=(K-1)\left(\sum_{j=1}^K d_j^2-d_i^2\right).
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-3}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:grpo-proof-1} uses the zero-sum identity for centered
deviations.  Equation~\eqref{eq:grpo-proof-2} is Cauchy--Schwarz, and
\eqref{eq:grpo-proof-3} restores the omitted term.  Rearranging gives
\begin{align}
  Kd_i^2
  &\le (K-1)\sum_{j=1}^K d_j^2
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-4}\\
  \frac{d_i^2}{v}
  &\le K-1,
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-5}
\end{align}
where the second line uses $Kv=\sum_jd_j^2$.  Taking square roots proves the
claim.  Adding a positive stabilizer to the denominator can only decrease the
magnitude.
\end{proof}

These bounds isolate what advantage normalization can and cannot establish.
They control the advantage factor, while a bound or empirical estimate of the
score factor remains necessary.  Moreover, group normalization couples the $K$
responses, so Theorem~\ref{thm:raw-mse} does not apply response by response without a
group-level sampling analysis.  The binary-reward, prompt-baseline case obeys
the theorem directly; the GRPO bound supplies scale information rather than an
i.i.d. justification.

\begin{remark}[Pointwise scope]
The expectation is over a fresh batch at fixed $\theta$.  During same-batch
optimization, $\theta$ depends on the fixed rollout data.  Extending
\eqref{eq:ess-bridge} uniformly over that adaptive path requires additional
stability or complexity control.
\end{remark}

The raw risk is now explicit.  The next question is what PPO clipping actually
does to this gradient and how that operation differs from ratio truncation.

\subsection{Clipping trades bias for variance}
\label{sec:clipping}

The reliability result identifies when the raw estimator becomes noisy, but it
does not yet establish that clipping is preferable.  That comparison requires
the exact gradient induced by clipping and the bias it introduces.  We begin
with PPO because its gradient is often confused with a globally capped ratio.

\subsubsection{PPO clipping as an advantage-sign gradient mask}

For a detached advantage $A$, the per-sample PPO surrogate is
\begin{equation}
  \ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
  :=\min\left\{
    W_\theta A,
    \operatorname{clip}(W_\theta,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:ppo-objective}
\end{equation}
Clipping this scalar objective does not cap the gradient coefficient.  Its
derivative instead masks samples according to advantage sign.

\begin{proposition}[PPO clipping is advantage-sign gradient masking]
\label{prop:ppo-mask}
Away from the two clipping boundaries, define
\begin{equation}
  M_\epsilon
  :=\mathbb{1}\{A\ge0, W_\theta\le1+\epsilon\}
   +\mathbb{1}\{A<0, W_\theta\ge1-\epsilon\}.
  \label{eq:ppo-mask}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_\theta\ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
  =W_\theta M_\epsilon H_\theta.
  \label{eq:ppo-gradient}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
When $A\ge0$, multiplication by $A$ preserves order.  Hence
\begin{align}
  \ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}
  &=A\min\{W_\theta,1+\epsilon\}
    \label{eq:ppo-positive-proof-1}\\
  \nabla_\theta\ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}
  &=A\nabla_\theta W_\theta
    \mathbb{1}\{W_\theta<1+\epsilon\}
    \label{eq:ppo-positive-proof-2}\\
  &=AW_\theta S_\theta
    \mathbb{1}\{W_\theta<1+\epsilon\}.
    \label{eq:ppo-positive-proof-3}
\end{align}
The second line differentiates the active branch.  The third uses
$\nabla_\theta W_\theta=W_\theta S_\theta$.

When $A<0$, multiplication reverses order.  Therefore
\begin{align}
  \ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}
  &=A\max\{W_\theta,1-\epsilon\}
    \label{eq:ppo-negative-proof-1}\\
  \nabla_\theta\ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}
  &=A\nabla_\theta W_\theta
    \mathbb{1}\{W_\theta>1-\epsilon\}
    \label{eq:ppo-negative-proof-2}\\
  &=AW_\theta S_\theta
    \mathbb{1}\{W_\theta>1-\epsilon\}.
    \label{eq:ppo-negative-proof-3}
\end{align}
Combining the two cases and substituting $H_\theta=AS_\theta$ gives
\eqref{eq:ppo-gradient}.  When $A=0$, the gradient is zero.  At the two
boundaries, any fixed valid subgradient convention preserves the distinction.
\end{proof}

PPO therefore removes an upper-ratio gradient only for nonnegative advantages
and a lower-ratio gradient only for negative advantages.  In particular, a
large ratio with negative advantage remains active.  PPO clipping is a
directional mask, not a global bound on importance coefficients.

To compare this mask with other estimators, let $C_\theta$ be any scalar
coefficient for which $C_\theta H_\theta$ has a finite second moment.  Define
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_C:=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N C_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i},
  \qquad
  b_C:=\E_Q[(C_\theta-W_\theta)H_\theta],
  \label{eq:modified-estimator}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma_W:=\Cov_Q(W_\theta H_\theta),
  \qquad
  \Sigma_C:=\Cov_Q(C_\theta H_\theta).
  \label{eq:bias-covariance}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{The estimator crossover}

\begin{theorem}[Exact estimator crossover]
\label{thm:crossover}
For fixed $\theta$ and finite second moments,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  =\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  =\|b_C\|_2^2+\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C).
  \label{eq:two-risks}
\end{equation}
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  <\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  \iff
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C)
  >N\|b_C\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $\mu_C:=\E[\widehat g_C]$.  Its displacement from the target is
\begin{align}
  \mu_C-g
  &=\E_Q[C_\theta H_\theta]-\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta]
    \label{eq:modified-bias-proof-1}\\
  &=\E_Q[(C_\theta-W_\theta)H_\theta]
    \label{eq:modified-bias-proof-2}\\
  &=b_C.
    \label{eq:modified-bias-proof-3}
\end{align}
Centering the modified estimator at $\mu_C$ now yields
\begin{align}
  \E\|\widehat g_C-g\|_2^2
  &=\E\|\widehat g_C-\mu_C+b_C\|_2^2
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-1}\\
  &=\E\|\widehat g_C-\mu_C\|_2^2
    +2b_C^\top\E[\widehat g_C-\mu_C]
    +\|b_C\|_2^2
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-2}\\
  &=\E\|\widehat g_C-\mu_C\|_2^2+\|b_C\|_2^2
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-3}\\
  &=\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C)+\|b_C\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-4}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:modified-bias-proof-1}--\eqref{eq:modified-bias-proof-3}
substitute $g=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta]$, use linearity, and invoke the definition
of $b_C$, respectively.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-1} adds and
subtracts $\mu_C$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-2} expands the
squared norm.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-3} uses
$\E[\widehat g_C-\mu_C]=0$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-4} uses
independence of the $N$ sampled responses, exactly as in
\eqref{eq:raw-proof-2}--\eqref{eq:raw-proof-3}.  The raw estimator is unbiased by
Theorem~\ref{thm:raw-mse}, so the same covariance calculation gives
\begin{equation}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  =\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W).
  \label{eq:raw-risk-crossover-proof}
\end{equation}
Subtracting the two risks gives
\begin{align}
  &\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
    -\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
    \label{eq:crossover-proof-1}\\
  &\quad=\|b_C\|_2^2
    -\frac1N\left\{
      \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C)
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:crossover-proof-2}
\end{align}
The left side is negative exactly when the covariance reduction exceeds
$N\|b_C\|_2^2$, which is \eqref{eq:crossover}.
\end{proof}

For PPO, the coefficient is $C_\theta^{\mathrm{PPO}}=W_\theta M_\epsilon$.
Its exact bias is
\begin{align}
  b_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}
  &=\E_Q[(W_\theta M_\epsilon-W_\theta)H_\theta]
    \label{eq:ppo-bias-1}\\
  &=-\E_Q[W_\theta(1-M_\epsilon)H_\theta].
    \label{eq:ppo-bias-2}
\end{align}
Theorem~\ref{thm:crossover} compares the covariance removed by this
sign-specific mask with its squared bias.  Combining the theorem with
Theorem~\ref{thm:mse-improvement}, the difference between the modified and raw
MSE-based improvement certificates is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\eta}{2}\left\{
    \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
    -\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:improvement-crossover}
\end{equation}
Thus \eqref{eq:crossover} identifies when masking or truncation has the larger
one-step certificate, not only when it has lower estimator risk.

\subsubsection{Ratio truncation bias trade-off}

Ratio truncation is different.  It changes the importance coefficient after
the gradient form has been specified.  For $c>0$, define
\begin{equation}
  C_c^{\mathrm{tr}}:=\min(W_\theta,c),
  \qquad
  T_c:=(W_\theta-c)_+,
  \qquad
  \tau_c:=\E_Q[T_c].
  \label{eq:upper-truncation}
\end{equation}
The contribution above $c$ is then $cH_\theta$, not zero.  This is a detached
importance-weight estimator, not the derivative of the PPO surrogate.

\begin{corollary}[Bias and MSE of upper-ratio truncation]
\label{cor:upper-truncation}
Suppose $\|H_\theta\|_2\le H_{\max}$ almost surely.  The detached estimator
$\widehat g_c=N^{-1}\sum_i C_{c,i}^{\mathrm{tr}}H_{\theta,i}$ satisfies
\begin{align}
  b_c&=-\E_Q[T_cH_\theta],
  &\|b_c\|_2&\le H_{\max}\tau_c,
  \label{eq:cap-bias}\\
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_c)
  &\le H_{\max}^2\left(\tau_c^2+\frac{c}{N}\right).
  \label{eq:cap-mse-bound}
\end{align}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The coefficient difference is $C_c^{\mathrm{tr}}-W_\theta=-T_c$.  Substitution into
\eqref{eq:bias-covariance} gives
\begin{align}
  b_c
  &=\E_Q[(C_c^{\mathrm{tr}}-W_\theta)H_\theta]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-1}\\
  &=-\E_Q[T_cH_\theta].
    \label{eq:cap-proof-2}
\end{align}
The triangle inequality and the bound on $H_\theta$ then give
\begin{align}
  \|b_c\|_2
  &\le\E_Q[T_c\|H_\theta\|_2]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-3}\\
  &\le H_{\max}\E_Q[T_c]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-4}\\
  &=H_{\max}\tau_c.
    \label{eq:cap-proof-5}
\end{align}
For the covariance term, use the trace identity and remove its nonnegative
mean-squared term:
\begin{align}
  \operatorname{tr}\{\Cov_Q(C_c^{\mathrm{tr}}H_\theta)\}
  &=\E_Q[(C_c^{\mathrm{tr}})^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]
    -\|\E_Q[C_c^{\mathrm{tr}}H_\theta]\|_2^2
    \label{eq:cap-proof-6}\\
  &\le H_{\max}^2\E_Q[(C_c^{\mathrm{tr}})^2]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-7}\\
  &\le cH_{\max}^2\E_Q[W_\theta]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-8}\\
  &=cH_{\max}^2.
    \label{eq:cap-proof-9}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cap-proof-7} uses the bound on $H_\theta$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:cap-proof-8} uses $(C_c^{\mathrm{tr}})^2\le cW_\theta$,
which holds both
when $W_\theta\le c$ and when $W_\theta>c$.  The final line uses
$\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$.  Combining \eqref{eq:two-risks},
\eqref{eq:cap-proof-5}, and \eqref{eq:cap-proof-9} proves
\eqref{eq:cap-mse-bound}.
\end{proof}

The bound explains when upper truncation can be useful.  It replaces the raw
second-moment dependence by $c/N$, while its bias is small when the tail excess
$\tau_c$ is small.  The cap is therefore an estimator parameter to calibrate,
not a scientific claim.  Truncated importance ratios also appear in ACER with
bias correction \citep{wang2017acer} and in the V-trace correction used by
IMPALA \citep{espeholt2018impala}; recent LLM methods likewise control extreme
weight moments \citep{zheng2025m2po,fakoor2026p3o}.  These precedents motivate
truncation as a separate importance-sampling device.  They do not make it
equivalent to PPO clipping.

The ESS identity and crossover now give the intended decision logic.  When
$G_{2,\theta}$ is controlled, high ESS certifies a small raw-gradient MSE and
therefore a stronger policy-improvement lower bound.  As ESS falls, raw-gradient
MSE grows and an intervention becomes valuable exactly when its variance
reduction repays its squared bias.  The threshold must still be calibrated
because PPO's benefit depends on this sign-specific crossover.

\subsection{The ESS-guided decision rule}
\label{sec:practice}

Population ESS is unavailable during training.  Its plug-in estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho
  :=\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^N W_i)^2}{N\sum_{i=1}^N W_i^2}.
  \label{eq:sample-ess}
\end{equation}
It should be computed from raw, pre-clipping sequence ratios.  The ESS gate uses
the raw gradient while $\widehat\rho$ is high and the PPO masked gradient once
$\widehat\rho$ falls below a calibrated threshold.

This rule is a hypothesis, not a consequence of
Theorem~\ref{thm:crossover}.  First, sample ESS can miss unobserved tail mass.  Second,
the same batch usually determines both the gate and the gradient.  Third,
a token-local surrogate is not algebraically equal to
\eqref{eq:raw-estimator}.  Recent LLM RL methods
already adapt updates using ESS, weight moments, or sequence reshaping
\cite{zheng2025m2po,shen2026vespo,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}; our distinct
empirical question is whether their relevant regime change coincides with the
gradient-MSE crossover in \eqref{eq:crossover}.

The rule is simple.  Compute raw sequence ESS, use the unmasked update above a
threshold, and use PPO masking below it.  The threshold is an application-level
choice because the theorem also depends on gradient scale and signal.  In the
controlled study below, we prespecify $0.1$ rather than choosing it from the
bandit outcomes, then test the full chain from ESS to gradient error, policy
improvement, and the gated update decision.

\section{Simulation: validating the decision mechanism}
\label{sec:simulation}

The simulation evaluates three empirical implications of the analysis.  First,
normalized ESS should order raw-gradient MSE when the gradient scale is
controlled.  Second, this MSE ordering should determine the quality of a policy
update.  Third, thresholding sample ESS should improve multi-update optimization
relative to always applying or never applying PPO masking.

We use a standard classification-to-bandit construction
\citep{dudik2011doubly,wang2017adaptive}.  Intuitively, the policy sees a digit
image, chooses one of ten labels, and observes only whether that choice was
correct.  Optdigits supplies 5,620 such examples in a compact 64-feature form
\citep{alpaydin1998optdigits}.  Although the simulated learner receives only
the reward for its chosen label, the experimenter retains the labels.  We can
therefore enumerate the exact population gradient and reward against which the
logged-data estimate is judged.

We first fix a stochastic digit policy and progressively make the logging
policy less representative of it.  This produces six coverage conditions while
leaving the underlying learning problem unchanged.  In each condition, 100
independent logged batches of 256 decisions are used to estimate the gradient.
We then ask two direct questions: does lower ESS predict larger error relative
to the enumerated gradient, and does that error reduce the reward obtained by
one policy update?  On the same batches, we also compare always using the raw
gradient, always applying the PPO mask from
Proposition~\ref{prop:ppo-mask}, and using the raw gradient only when
$\widehat\rho\ge0.1$.  The threshold is specified before observing the bandit
results.

The multi-update study asks whether the one-step mechanism matters over a full
optimization epoch.  Under the lowest-coverage logger, each method reuses the
same logged batch for 16 updates, as in fixed-rollout policy optimization.
Ratios and sample ESS are recomputed after every update, so the gate can switch
between raw and masked gradients as the policy moves.  Panel (c) reports
relative population-reward improvement after 4, 8, 12, and 16 updates.  We
evaluate step sizes $2$ and $5$, with the more aggressive setting shown in the
figure.  Appendix~\ref{app:bandit} defines every quantity, sampling step,
hyperparameter, and uncertainty calculation.

ESS accurately orders estimator reliability and update quality in the
controlled sweep.  As population ESS decreases from $1$ to $0.0025$,
raw-gradient MSE increases from $0.0105$ to $0.918$, while mean one-step reward
gain decreases from $9.39\times10^{-3}$ to $-5.70\times10^{-3}$.  Across all
600 one-step decisions, mean gain is $(6.39\pm0.56)\times10^{-3}$ for the ESS
gate, $(5.35\pm0.71)\times10^{-3}$ for the unclipped estimator, and
$(5.82\pm0.56)\times10^{-3}$ for PPO.  Paired advantages of the gate are
$(1.04\pm0.41)\times10^{-3}$ over the unclipped estimator and
$(0.56\pm0.06)\times10^{-3}$ over PPO.  All reported uncertainty values are
standard errors.

The multi-update results establish an optimization consequence.  At step size
$5$, relative reward improvement after 16 updates is $30.18\pm2.36\%$ for the
ESS gate, $26.87\pm2.28\%$ for PPO, and $23.73\pm3.94\%$ for the unclipped
estimator.  Paired advantages of the gate are $3.31\pm0.51$ percentage points
over PPO and $6.45\pm3.34$ percentage points over the unclipped estimator.  The
gate selects $4.93$ raw and $11.07$ masked updates on average, confirming that
it does not reproduce either static baseline.  At step size $2$, the gate also
has the highest mean improvement, although its advantage over the unclipped
estimator is not statistically resolved.  These results support ESS as a
criterion for selecting the update in this controlled setting; the next section
tests whether the same criterion transfers to RLVR.

\section{Main experiments: ESS-gated RLVR}
\label{sec:llm-experiments}

The simulation establishes that ESS can identify the lower-MSE update when the
target gradient is observable.  The main experiment asks whether the same
criterion improves language-model training, where neither the true gradient nor
its MSE can be measured.  The test must therefore compare final performance,
learning efficiency, and collapse behavior under matched training runs.

\subsection{Decision rule and comparison set}

At every policy update, compute sequence ESS from the raw ratios before any
masking or truncation.  When $\widehat\rho\ge0.1$, use a permissive update that
retains the available learning signal, instantiated as either the fully
unclipped estimator or the CISPO-style weight-clipped estimator
\citep{minimax2025m1}.  When $\widehat\rho<0.1$, activate one of three safeguards:
conventional PPO clipping, Clip-Higher \citep{yu2025dapo}, or the divergence
constraint of DPPO \citep{qi2026dppo}.  Each gated method must be compared with
its always-active counterpart under identical rollouts, optimizer settings,
token budgets, and evaluation checkpoints.  This matched design isolates the
value of the ESS decision from the value of the underlying safeguard.

\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{LLM}}
  =\begin{cases}
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{permissive}},&\widehat\rho\ge0.1,\\
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{safeguard}},&\widehat\rho<0.1.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:llm-gate}
\end{equation}
The threshold is fixed before the reported runs.  It is not retuned for each
safeguard, model, or benchmark, because per-setting tuning would no longer test
ESS as a transferable reliability criterion.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

permissive baseline, each always-active safeguard, and its ESS-gated version.
A companion plot should align sequence ESS with the same horizontal axis and
mark every switch of Equation~\eqref{eq:llm-gate}.  Report at least three seeds,
the mean and confidence interval, peak reward, final reward, samples or tokens
to a fixed reward target, and a prespecified collapse statistic.  The collapse
statistic should capture the motivating CISPO failure directly, for example the
largest sustained reward decline after the peak, and should be accompanied by
entropy and response-length diagnostics when available.

\section{Optdigits contextual-bandit protocol}
\label{app:bandit}

\subsection{What classification-to-bandit means}

The experiment follows the classification-to-bandit reduction used by
\citet{dudik2011doubly} and subsequently by \citet{wang2017adaptive}.  Start
with a supervised example $(x,y)$, where $x$ is an input and
$y\in\{0,\ldots,9\}$ is its class.  The bandit interaction is generated as
follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item present only $x$ to the policy;
  \item draw one action $A\in\{0,\ldots,9\}$ from a logging policy
  $Q(\cdot\mid x)$;
  \item reveal only the selected-action reward
  $R(x,A)=\mathbb{1}\{A=y\}$.
\end{enumerate}
Thus the logged learner observes $(x,A,Q(A\mid x),R(x,A))$, not the rewards of
the nine unselected actions.  The simulator retains $y$ only for population
evaluation.  This construction produces genuine partial feedback while making
the counterfactual rewards available to the experimenter.  Dud\'{i}k et al.
used it to turn classification benchmarks, including Optdigits, into contextual
bandits.  Wang et al. later used the same class-to-action and
correct-class-to-unit-reward construction to evaluate off-policy estimators
against known finite-population truth.  Our experiment adapts that established
evaluation device to policy-gradient estimation; it is not a claim about image
classification generalization.

\subsection{Optdigits data and preprocessing}

Optdigits is the UCI Optical Recognition of Handwritten Digits data set
\citep{alpaydin1998optdigits}.  The original examples are $32\times32$ binary
images.  Each image is partitioned into $4\times4$ blocks, and the foreground
pixels in each block are counted, yielding an $8\times8$ representation with
64 integer features in $\{0,\ldots,16\}$.  The response is one of ten digits.
The supplied files contain 3,823 training examples and 1,797 test examples,
for $M=5{,}620$ examples in total.

We download the two official UCI files, concatenate them, divide all 64 features
by 16, and append a constant feature equal to one.  Every context is therefore
$x_j\in\mathbb{R}^{65}$.  We place probability $1/M$ on each context.  The
original train-test distinction is deliberately not used because predictive
generalization is not the estimand.  The concatenated data define a finite
population whose policy value, gradient, and importance-weight moments can all
be enumerated exactly.

\subsection{Initial policy, reward, baseline, and objective}

The policy is a ten-class linear softmax with parameter
$\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{10\times65}$:
\begin{equation}
  P_\theta(a\mid x)
  =\frac{\exp(\theta_a^\top x)}
  {\sum_{c=0}^{9}\exp(\theta_c^\top x)}.
  \label{eq:optdigits-policy}
\end{equation}
To construct a nontrivial but stochastic initial policy, we first fit a softmax
classifier to all $M$ examples.  Its weights start at zero and receive 400
full-batch gradient-descent steps on multiclass cross-entropy, with learning
rate $0.8$, no regularization, no minibatching, and no early stopping.  If
$\theta_{\mathrm{fit}}$ denotes the result, every simulation starts at
$\theta_0=0.3\theta_{\mathrm{fit}}$.  This scaling prevents the policy from
becoming nearly deterministic before logging begins.

The finite-population objective is the probability of choosing the correct
digit under a uniformly drawn context:
\begin{equation}
  V(\theta)
  =\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^{M}
    P_\theta(y_j\mid x_j).
  \label{eq:optdigits-value}
\end{equation}
The reward is $R_{j,a}=\mathbb{1}\{a=y_j\}$.  We use the fixed baseline
$b_j=P_{\theta_0}(y_j\mid x_j)$ and define
$A_{j,a}=R_{j,a}-b_j$.  Consequently $|A_{j,a}|\le1$.  The baseline is known
from the initial policy and never fitted from the sampled bandit batch.  Its
being fixed during the 16-update experiment does not change the population
gradient because, for every context,
$\sum_aP_\theta(a\mid x)\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(a\mid x)=0$.

For this policy, the score is the $10\times65$ matrix
\begin{equation}
  S_\theta(x_j,a)
  =\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(a\mid x_j)
  =\{e_a-P_\theta(\cdot\mid x_j)\}x_j^\top,
  \label{eq:optdigits-score}
\end{equation}
and one unweighted gradient contribution is
$H_\theta(x_j,a)=A_{j,a}S_\theta(x_j,a)$.

\subsection{Logging policies and the controlled coverage sweep}

We vary only the mismatch between the fixed initial policy and the logger.  With
random seed 38, draw one array
$Z\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times10}$ with independent standard-normal entries and
center every row so that $\sum_aZ_{j,a}=0$.  The six logging policies are
\begin{equation}
  Q_\alpha(a\mid x_j)
  =\operatorname{softmax}_a
    \left(\log P_{\theta_0}(a\mid x_j)+\alpha Z_{j,a}\right),
  \qquad
  \alpha\in\{0,0.5,1,1.5,2,2.5\}.
  \label{eq:logger-perturbation}
\end{equation}
The same realized $Z$ is multiplied by each value of $\alpha$; it is not
redrawn across conditions.  At $\alpha=0$, $Q_\alpha=P_{\theta_0}$.  Increasing
$\alpha$ progressively changes which actions the logger favors.  Softmax gives
strictly positive probability to every action, so all importance ratios are
defined and the true raw importance-weighted gradient is unchanged across
loggers.  Since $Z$ is sampled without using rewards, advantages, or scores,
coverage is not deliberately aligned with large gradient contributions.  In
the realized sweep, $G_{2,\theta_0}$ varies only from $2.65$ to $3.12$, whereas
$\rho_{\theta_0}$ varies from $1$ to $0.002531$.

\subsection{Exact gradient, exact MSE, and empirical MSE}

For a logger $Q=Q_\alpha$, a logged observation is generated by drawing
$J\sim\operatorname{Uniform}\{1,\ldots,M\}$, then
$A\sim Q(\cdot\mid x_J)$.  Define
\begin{equation}
  W_\theta(x_j,a)
  =\frac{P_\theta(a\mid x_j)}{Q(a\mid x_j)}.
  \label{eq:optdigits-weight}
\end{equation}
The phrase \emph{true gradient} refers to an exact finite sum, not to a larger
Monte Carlo batch.  We evaluate all $M\times10=56{,}200$ context-action pairs:
\begin{align}
  g(\theta)
  &=\nabla_\theta V(\theta)
    =\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=0}^{9}
      P_\theta(a\mid x_j)H_\theta(x_j,a),
    \label{eq:optdigits-gradient}\\
  \rho_\theta^{-1}
  &=\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=0}^{9}
    Q(a\mid x_j)W_\theta(x_j,a)^2,
    \label{eq:optdigits-rho}\\
  G_{2,\theta}
  &=\rho_\theta\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=0}^{9}
    Q(a\mid x_j)W_\theta(x_j,a)^2
    \|H_\theta(x_j,a)\|_F^2.
    \label{eq:optdigits-g2}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:optdigits-gradient} is independent of $Q$ because the factor
$QW_\theta$ equals $P_\theta$.  The program forms every score matrix in
\eqref{eq:optdigits-score}, weights it by $P_\theta A_{j,a}/M$, and adds the
result.  No sampled action enters this calculation.

For one logged batch $\mathcal{B}_r=\{(J_i,A_i,R_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, the raw
estimate and normalized sample ESS are
\begin{align}
  \widehat g_r
  &=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}
    W_\theta(x_{J_i},A_i)H_\theta(x_{J_i},A_i),
    \label{eq:optdigits-sample-gradient}\\
  \widehat\rho_r
  &=\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_i)^2}
    {N\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_i^2}.
    \label{eq:optdigits-sample-ess}
\end{align}
We use $N=256$ and $R=100$ independently sampled batches per logger.  Each
batch samples contexts with replacement and then samples one action from its
logger.  The reported empirical gradient MSE is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}
  =\frac1R\sum_{r=1}^{R}
    \|\widehat g_r-g(\theta_0)\|_F^2.
  \label{eq:optdigits-empirical-mse}
\end{equation}
This is the squared Euclidean error after vectorizing all $10\times65=650$
gradient coordinates.  It is neither a per-coordinate average nor a root MSE.
The exact finite-population MSE shown by the dashed curve in
Figure~\ref{fig:ess-validation}(a) is
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_r)
  =\frac1N\left[
    \frac{G_{2,\theta_0}}{\rho_{\theta_0}}
    -\|g(\theta_0)\|_F^2
  \right],
  \label{eq:optdigits-exact-mse}
\end{equation}
where every expectation is evaluated by the same 56,200-term sum.  At very low
ESS, rare actions with extremely large weights contribute strongly to the exact
second moment but may not occur in 100 batches.  The empirical average can
therefore lie below the exact MSE even though it is an unbiased Monte Carlo
estimate of that MSE.  This explains the visible gap at the lowest-coverage
conditions.

\subsection{One-step update and ESS decision}

For each of the six loggers, the 100 batches are generated with seed 39.  A
single batch is passed to all three methods, making every comparison paired.
The raw estimator is given in \eqref{eq:optdigits-sample-gradient}.  The masked
estimator and the ESS decision are
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{PPO}}
  =\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N W_iM_{1,i}H_i,
  \qquad
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{gate}}
  =\begin{cases}
    \widehat g_r,&\widehat\rho_r\ge0.1,\\
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{PPO}},&\widehat\rho_r<0.1.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:experimental-estimators}
\end{equation}
Here $M_{1,i}$ is exactly the advantage-sign mask in
Proposition~\ref{prop:ppo-mask} with $\epsilon=1$.  Thus a nonnegative-advantage
sample is retained when $W_i\le2$, and a negative-advantage sample is retained
when $W_i\ge0$.  Since ratios are positive, the latter condition is always
satisfied.  In this testbed the PPO rule therefore removes only high-ratio,
nonnegative-advantage contributions.  It does not numerically truncate a ratio
that it retains.

The threshold $0.1$ is fixed before examining any bandit outcome to match the
motivating LLM rule; it is not tuned on these simulations and is not presented
as universal.  Each method updates
$\theta_1=\theta_0+0.8\widehat g$.  We then enumerate
$V(\theta_1)$ using \eqref{eq:optdigits-value} and report
$V(\theta_1)-V(\theta_0)$.  Replacing $\widehat g$ by the enumerated
$g(\theta_0)$ gives the exact-gradient reference in
Figure~\ref{fig:ess-validation}(b).

\subsection{Sixteen-update fixed-rollout experiment}

The optimization experiment uses $Q_{2.5}$, whose population ESS at
$\theta_0$ is $0.002531$.  With seed 40, we independently sample 100 fixed
rollouts of $N=256$ interactions.  For each rollout and each method, set
$\theta^{(0)}=\theta_0$ and repeat the following steps for
$t=0,\ldots,15$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item recompute $P_{\theta^{(t)}}$, every ratio
  $P_{\theta^{(t)}}(A_i\mid x_{J_i})/Q_{2.5}(A_i\mid x_{J_i})$, and
  $\widehat\rho^{(t)}$ on the same 256 interactions;
  \item compute the raw and PPO-masked gradients using the current policy
  score and the fixed initial-policy baseline $b_j$;
  \item select the raw gradient, the masked gradient, or the
  $\widehat\rho^{(t)}\ge0.1$ decision according to the method;
  \item update $\theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^{(t)}+\eta\widehat g^{(t)}$;
  \item enumerate $V(\theta^{(t+1)})$ over all 5,620 contexts.
\end{enumerate}
The logger, actions, rewards, and contexts stay fixed within a rollout.  Only
the target policy, ratios, scores, ESS, and selected update change.  All three
methods use the same rollout, and the same 100 rollouts are used for step sizes
$\eta=2$ and $\eta=5$.  The moderate setting reports behavior under smaller
updates; the aggressive setting tests whether unreliable gradients accumulate
over an epoch and is the setting displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:ess-validation}(c).
No learning rate is selected separately for any method.

At checkpoint $t$, relative improvement is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_t^{\mathrm{rel}}
  =100\frac{V(\theta^{(t)})-V(\theta^{(0)})}
  {V(\theta^{(0)})}.
  \label{eq:relative-improvement}
\end{equation}
The initial value is $V(\theta_0)=0.42154$.  Text and tables report the mean and
standard error $s/\sqrt{100}$ across rollouts.  Figure error bars and bands are
the mean plus or minus $1.96s/\sqrt{100}$.  Method differences are first taken
within each paired rollout and then summarized, which is why their standard
errors need not equal the sum of the marginal standard errors.
