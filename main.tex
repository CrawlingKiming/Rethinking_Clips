\title{\bf When Does Clipping Help?\\
Effective Sample Size and Reliable Policy Improvement}
\author{Dongkyu Derek Cho, Thomas Voice}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Clipping is usually treated as an always-on safeguard for policy optimization,
yet permissive updates often learn faster and fail only late in training.  We
study this tension as a problem of statistical reliability.  For a current
policy evaluated on a fixed rollout distribution, normalized sequence effective
sample size (ESS) exactly controls the second moment of the centered importance
ratio.  With bounded verifier rewards, this gives an ESS-dependent mean-squared
error and lower confidence bound for the unclipped estimate of population
policy improvement.  A bounded full-sequence cap gives a conservative but
biased alternative.  Our main theorem gives a direct answer to when clipping
helps: at a common confidence level, the clipped certificate is stronger
exactly when the uncertainty removed by clipping exceeds the empirical reward
signal it discards.  Thus high ESS favors permissive estimation, whereas low
ESS makes clipping potentially useful but does not make it automatically
optimal.  We distinguish this sequence-level result from token-local PPO/GRPO
masking and CISPO coefficient capping.  A separate smoothness proposition
connects gradient-estimation error to one-step population progress without
treating ESS as sufficient for gradient reliability.  An exactly evaluable
contextual bandit verifies the predicted reliability transition, and
ESS-triggered safeguards prevent late collapse in language-model RLVR.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Policy optimization on a fixed rollout batch faces a simple tension.  Reusing
the batch makes learning efficient, but the current policy gradually moves away
from the policy that generated the data.  PPO addresses this mismatch by
suppressing selected token gradients after their likelihood ratios cross fixed
boundaries \citep{schulman2017ppo}; GRPO inherits the same mechanism in RLVR
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}.  This motivates the common view that clipping
should remain active throughout optimization.

The empirical behavior is less simple.  Clipping can remove useful gradients
and slow reasoning improvement, while permissive objectives can learn quickly
and remain stable for many updates before suddenly failing
\citep{yu2025dapo,park2025clipping,minimax2025m1}.  Figure~\ref{fig:llm-motivation}
shows this pattern for two CISPO-style detached token-coefficient caps.  Both
improve without PPO masking and collapse only after normalized sequence ESS
nearly vanishes.  The absence of masking is therefore not itself the failure
mode.

The missing question is not whether an individual ratio has crossed a
boundary.  It is whether the fixed rollout batch still provides reliable
evidence about the current policy.  We use sequence effective sample size
(ESS) to measure this effective support.  If
$W=dP/dQ$ is the complete-response likelihood ratio, then normalized population
ESS is
\[
  \rho=\{\E_Q[W^2]\}^{-1},
\]
and $\rho^{-1}-1=\E_Q[(W-1)^2]$ is exactly the ratio-dispersion term that drives
importance-sampling uncertainty \citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.

Our theoretical result is deliberately direct.  For bounded verifier rewards,
the unclipped estimator of the population improvement
$J(P)-J(Q)$ has error of order
\[
  \sqrt{\frac{\rho^{-1}-1}{N}}.
\]
A bounded full-sequence cap reduces this uncertainty but discards reward-bearing
tail mass.  The main theorem compares the two valid lower confidence bounds and
shows that the capped bound is stronger exactly when the uncertainty it removes
is larger than the empirical signal it deletes.  This yields the regime change
seen in practice: high ESS supports permissive estimation; low ESS makes a
conservative intervention worth considering, but does not by itself prove that
clipping is preferable.

The theorem is sequence-level.  Token-local PPO/GRPO clipping is an
advantage-sign gradient mask, while CISPO caps a detached token coefficient.
Neither operation is the bounded sequence estimator in the theorem.  We keep
these objects separate: the theorem supplies the reliability diagnosis, and
the experiments test whether token-level safeguards are useful in the
diagnosed low-support regime.  A short smoothness argument
\citep{pirotta2013adaptive,papini2022smoothing} separately explains why reducing
the full MSE of an applied gradient can improve a one-step population guarantee.

An exactly evaluable contextual bandit tests the theory without an LLM-scale
proxy.  It exposes the true population reward and gradient, allowing us to
measure the relation among ESS, estimator error, and harmful policy steps.
Language-model experiments then test the operational implication: preserve
permissive updates while ESS is high and activate a safeguard only after
effective support deteriorates.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item We give a concise ESS-dependent certificate for the true policy
improvement $J(P)-J(Q)$ and an exact lower-bound crossover condition:
clipping helps only when uncertainty reduction exceeds discarded empirical
signal.
\item We separate full-sequence truncation, PPO/GRPO gradient masking, and
CISPO coefficient capping, and use a secondary bias--variance and smoothness
analysis to explain the gradient diagnostics without claiming that ESS alone
determines gradient quality.
\item We verify the predicted reliability transition in a contextual bandit
with an exact oracle and evaluate sparse ESS-triggered safeguards in
language-model RLVR.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Related perspectives}

Importance-sampling policy optimization uses second moments, Renyi divergence,
and ESS to quantify effective coverage
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  Recent language-model methods likewise
adapt updates using weight concentration, ESS, divergence, or sequence
reshaping
\citep{zheng2025m2po,shen2026vespo,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o,qi2026dppo}.
Our contribution is not another clipping boundary.  It is a statistical
criterion that states when the rollout data cease to support a reliable
unclipped improvement estimate.

Recent RLVR methods expose the cost of uniform clipping.  DAPO relaxes the
upper boundary, Clip-Low changes entropy behavior, CISPO retains gradients
beyond PPO boundaries, and DPPO uses a divergence-based rule
\citep{yu2025dapo,park2025clipping,minimax2025m1,qi2026dppo}.  These methods
modify different mathematical objects.  Our analysis makes that distinction
explicit and identifies the sequence-level reliability variable shared by
their empirical failure regimes.

\section{Preliminaries: fixed-rollout policy optimization}
\label{sec:setting}

Let $X\sim\nu$ be a prompt, let $Q(\cdot\mid X)$ be the rollout policy, and let
$P(\cdot\mid X)$ be a current policy evaluated on data from $Q$.  Write
$Z=(X,Y)$ and assume $P\ll Q$.  The complete-response likelihood ratio is
\begin{equation}
  W(Z):=\frac{P(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)},
  \qquad \E_Q[W\mid X]=1.
  \label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}
For a verifier reward $0\le R(Z)\le R_{\max}$, define
\begin{equation}
  J(P):=\E_P[R],
  \qquad
  \Delta(P,Q):=J(P)-J(Q)
  =\E_Q[(W-1)R].
  \label{eq:population-improvement}
\end{equation}
Thus importance weighting introduces no population surrogate: it only creates
a finite-sample reliability problem.

The normalized population sequence ESS is
\begin{equation}
  \rho(P,Q):=\frac{1}{\E_Q[W^2]}
  =\exp\{-D_2(P\|Q)\}.
  \label{eq:current-ess}
\end{equation}
Because $\E_Q[W]=1$,
\begin{equation}
  \E_Q[(W-1)^2]=\E_Q[W^2]-1=\rho(P,Q)^{-1}-1.
  \label{eq:weight-variance}
\end{equation}
Hence $N\rho$ is the population effective sequence count and
$\rho^{-1}-1=\chi^2(P\|Q)$ is the exact sequence-ratio dispersion.

We state the result for $N$ independent prompt-response units
$Z_1,\ldots,Z_N\sim Q$.  In a one-pass disjoint traversal, the theorem is
applied conditionally on the previous updates: the current policy is then
fixed, while the unused minibatch is fresh.  Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} gives
this conditional form and the extension to intact prompt groups.

Define the unclipped improvement estimator and a full-sequence upper-capped
estimator, with $c\ge1$ fixed before observing the minibatch,
\begin{align}
  \widehat\Delta_W
  &:=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N(W_i-1)R_i,
  \label{eq:raw-improvement-estimator}\\
  \widehat\Delta_c
  &:=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\{\min(W_i,c)-1\}R_i.
  \label{eq:capped-improvement-estimator}
\end{align}
For $0<\alpha<1$, write
\begin{align}
  \mathfrak r_W(\rho,\alpha)
  &:=R_{\max}
    \sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha N}\{\rho^{-1}-1\}},
  \label{eq:raw-radius}\\
  \mathfrak r_c(c,\alpha)
  &:=cR_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{2N}}.
  \label{eq:capped-radius}
\end{align}

\section{When does clipping help?}
\label{sec:theory}

\begin{theorem}[ESS-controlled reliability and clipping crossover]
\label{thm:ess-clipping}
Assume $0<\rho(P,Q)\le1$, fix $c\ge1$ before observing the batch, and let
$\alpha=\delta/2$ for $0<\delta<1$.  Then
\begin{equation}
  \E\!\left[(\widehat\Delta_W-\Delta(P,Q))^2\right]
  =\frac{1}{N}\Var_Q\!\left((W-1)R\right)
  \le\frac{R_{\max}^2}{N}\{\rho(P,Q)^{-1}-1\}.
  \label{eq:raw-mse}
\end{equation}
Moreover, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta(P,Q)\ge
  \max\left\{
    \widehat\Delta_W-\mathfrak r_W(\rho,\alpha),\,
    \widehat\Delta_c-\mathfrak r_c(c,\alpha)
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:joint-certificate}
\end{equation}
The capped certificate is stronger exactly when
\begin{equation}
  \mathfrak r_W(\rho,\alpha)-\mathfrak r_c(c,\alpha)
  >
  \widehat\Delta_W-\widehat\Delta_c
  =
  \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N(W_i-c)_+R_i.
  \label{eq:certificate-crossover}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Theorem~\ref{thm:ess-clipping} gives the paper's central answer.  The left side
of Equation~\eqref{eq:certificate-crossover} is the uncertainty removed by
bounding the sequence contribution; the right side is the observed reward
signal deleted by the cap.  Clipping helps the certified population floor only
when the former exceeds the latter.

\begin{remark}[Interpretation]
\label{rem:regimes}
When $\rho$ is high, the unclipped radius in
Equation~\eqref{eq:raw-radius} is small, so clipping has little uncertainty to
remove while it may still discard useful signal.  High ESS does not itself
imply $\Delta(P,Q)>0$; positive improvement is certified only when the empirical
margin exceeds the radius.  When $\rho$ is low, the unclipped radius grows and
a bounded estimator can become preferable, but low ESS does not imply that it
must: Equation~\eqref{eq:certificate-crossover} still charges for discarded
signal.  Thus ESS is a reliability coordinate, not a universal clipping rule.
The numerical trigger $\widehat\rho=0.1$ used later is deliberately empirical,
not a constant derived from the theorem.
\end{remark}

The population price of the cap is also explicit:
\begin{equation}
  \E[\widehat\Delta_c]
  =
  \Delta(P,Q)-\E_Q[(W-c)_+R]
  \le \Delta(P,Q).
  \label{eq:cap-bias}
\end{equation}
The cap is conservative because $R\ge0$, and its downward bias is exactly the
reward-bearing tail mass it removes.

\subsection{What the theorem covers}
\label{sec:clipping}

\subsubsection{Full-sequence truncation}
\label{sec:ratio-truncation}

The capped estimator in Theorem~\ref{thm:ess-clipping} acts on the complete
response ratio $W=\prod_t r_t$.  If differentiated as an objective, it masks the
entire response once $W>c$.  This operation has the one-sided population
relation in Equation~\eqref{eq:cap-bias}.

\subsubsection{PPO/GRPO is an advantage-sign gradient mask}

For a detached advantage $A$ and token ratio
$r_t(\theta)=P_\theta(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t})/
Q(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t})$, the token-averaged PPO/GRPO surrogate is
\begin{equation}
  \ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
  :=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\min\left\{
    r_tA,\operatorname{clip}(r_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:ppo-objective}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[PPO clipping is advantage-sign gradient masking]
\label{prop:ppo-mask}
Away from the clipping boundaries, let
\begin{equation}
  M_{t,\epsilon}
  :=\mathbf{1}\{A\ge0,r_t\le1+\epsilon\}
   +\mathbf{1}\{A<0,r_t\ge1-\epsilon\}.
  \label{eq:ppo-mask}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_\theta\ell_\epsilon^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
  =\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T
    M_{t,\epsilon}r_tA
    \nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t}).
  \label{eq:ppo-gradient}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

Thus PPO/GRPO removes upper-ratio tokens only for nonnegative advantages and
lower-ratio tokens only for negative advantages.  It is not numerical
full-sequence truncation.

\subsubsection{CISPO caps a detached token coefficient}

Let $c_{\mathrm C}(r_t)$ denote the CISPO token coefficient and
$\operatorname{sg}$ stop differentiation through it.  Its estimator has the
form
\begin{equation}
  G^{\mathrm{CISPO}}(Z)
  =
  \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T
  \operatorname{sg}\{c_{\mathrm C}(r_t)\}
  A\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t}).
  \label{eq:cispo-gradient}
\end{equation}
The token score remains active beyond the PPO boundary
\citep{minimax2025m1}.  PPO/GRPO, CISPO, and full-sequence truncation therefore
modify different estimators.  The exact certificate above applies only to the
last; the language-model experiments test whether the same ESS diagnosis is
operationally useful for the first two.

\subsection{Why gradient MSE remains a useful diagnostic}

The theorem concerns the true reward difference, where bounded $R$ allows ESS
to control the full estimation error.  Gradient contributions include policy
scores and cannot be controlled by ESS alone.  To compare gradient estimators,
let $X_W$ be an unbiased permissive contribution with
$\E[X_W]=g$, let $X_C$ be a modified contribution, and define
\begin{equation}
  b_C:=\E[X_C-X_W],
  \qquad
  \Sigma_W:=\Cov(X_W),
  \qquad
  \Sigma_C:=\Cov(X_C).
  \label{eq:bias-covariance}
\end{equation}
For $N$ independent units,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  =\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  =\|b_C\|_2^2+\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C).
  \label{eq:two-risks}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_C)
  <\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  \iff
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C)
  >N\|b_C\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
This is the bias--variance comparison measured by the frozen-policy diagnostics.
ESS can predict the ratio-concentration component empirically, but the
gradient contributions attached to the ratio tail remain necessary.

Published policy-gradient analyses use smoothness to turn gradient quality into
a performance statement
\citep{pirotta2013adaptive,papini2022smoothing}.  The required bridge is short.

\begin{proposition}[Gradient error and one-step population progress]
\label{prop:gradient-progress}
Let $g=\nabla J(\theta)$, suppose $J$ is $L$-smooth along the update segment,
and let $\theta^+=\theta+\eta\widehat g$ with $0<\eta\le1/L$.  Then
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta^+)-J(\theta)
  \ge
  \frac{\eta}{2}
  \left\{\|g\|_2^2-\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2\right\}.
  \label{eq:pointwise-gradient-progress}
\end{equation}
If $\widehat g$ is random with finite MSE, then
\begin{equation}
  \E[J(\theta^+)-J(\theta)]
  \ge
  \frac{\eta}{2}
  \left\{\|g\|_2^2-\E\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2\right\}.
  \label{eq:expected-population-gain}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

Proposition~\ref{prop:gradient-progress} explains why the measured MSE
crossover is relevant to stability.  It is not the ESS theorem and does not
claim that lower MSE alone orders complete training trajectories.

\subsection{Observable diagnostic}
\label{sec:practice}

Population ESS is unavailable during training.  We use the standard plug-in
estimate
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho
  :=
  \frac{(\sum_{i=1}^N W_i)^2}
       {N\sum_{i=1}^N W_i^2}.
  \label{eq:sample-ess}
\end{equation}
It is computed from raw sequence ratios before intervention.  A finite batch can
miss rare high-ratio responses and therefore overstate coverage, so substituting
$\widehat\rho$ into Theorem~\ref{thm:ess-clipping} is a diagnostic rather than a
finite-sample confidence guarantee.  We prespecify $0.1$ as a conservative
operational trigger and test it empirically.

\section{Contextual-bandit validation of the theory}
\label{sec:simulation}

The contextual bandit exposes the exact population gradient and reward.  We use
it to test whether ESS predicts finite-batch gradient error, harmful policy
steps, and the MSE crossover between permissive and masked estimators.

\subsection{Testbed and oracle}

We use the standard reduction from classification to a contextual bandit on Optdigits
\citep{wang2017adaptive,alpaydin1998optdigits}.  Each handwritten-digit image is
a context, the policy emits a 16-token binary encoding of the digit, and an
exact-match verifier supplies reward.  Because the dataset is a finite
population, we can enumerate the exact reward and policy gradient.  The
sequence action still produces the product importance ratios that drive ESS in
RLVR, so the testbed removes language-model complexity without removing the
statistical mechanism of interest.

The optimization loop follows grouped RLVR
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath,yu2025dapo}.  Each rollout samples 128 prompts and
16 responses per prompt, computes leave-one-out advantages, and supplies one
disjoint epoch of 16 sequential minibatch updates.  Ratios and ESS are
recomputed before every update, and a new rollout is collected afterward.
Thus all 16 updates avoid response reuse.  The prompts are sampled without
replacement from the finite population, however, so the loop matches the
theorem's disjoint one-pass structure but not its conditional-i.i.d.
assumption literally.  Frozen-policy redraws provide an independent estimator
diagnostic.  A separate scaled trajectory uses 512 prompts per rollout and four
optimization epochs to test repeated-data robustness beyond the theorem.  We
run 100 paired replications; Appendix~\ref{app:bandit} gives the compact
protocols and full result tables.

\subsection{The predicted regime change appears}

ESS first predicts the reliability of the permissive gradient.  Its MSE rises
from $1.6\times10^{-2}$ above ESS $0.8$ to $1.31\times10^{-1}$ between $0.03$
and $0.1$, and to $4.77\times10^{-1}$ below $0.03$.  In the same two low-ESS
regions, permissive steps reduce population reward by $4.0\%$ and $13.3\%$,
while matched oracle-gradient steps improve reward.  ESS therefore identifies
estimation failure rather than the absence of an improving direction.

Frozen-policy redraws then measure the gradient-MSE decomposition in
Equation~\eqref{eq:crossover}.  With the fixed $N=512$ diagnostic, the
sequence-level mask has
lower MSE below population ESS $0.4$, while the permissive estimator has lower
MSE above it.  A disjoint calibration split places the crossover at $0.42$ and
reduces held-out MSE by $4.3\%\pm1.1\%$ relative to the better static estimator.
Thus ESS predicts which estimator has lower gradient error under this fixed
construction; it does not establish which update has greater population gain.

Finally, we evaluate the deliberately conservative rule
$\widehat\rho<0.1$.  It selects only $3.3\%$ of updates; within that event, PPO
has lower MSE on $83.7\%\pm1.4\%$ of updates and reduces
conditional MSE by $53.3\%\pm2.9\%$.  Across eight rollout cycles, each with four
optimization epochs over its fixed batch, the resulting trajectory reaches
$0.4940\pm0.0050$ final reward, compared with
$0.4606\pm0.0098$ for always permissive and $0.4038\pm0.0005$ for always
masked.  The sparse trigger therefore captures the low-ESS side of the
  empirical diagnostic region with an observable training statistic.

The controlled study therefore supports the empirical claim that ESS tracks a
reliability transition relevant to the sequence-level masking analogue.  It
does not by itself establish the one-epoch population bound or transfer the result
to token-local PPO, CISPO, or full-sequence truncation.  The next section asks
whether a related operational transition is visible in language-model RLVR,
where the population gradient cannot be enumerated.

\section{Language-model evidence for the regime change}
\label{sec:llm-experiments}

The contextual-bandit study provides controlled one-epoch evidence under an
exact oracle, together with a separate repeated-epoch stress test outside the
theorem.  It does not directly test the confidence certificates.
This section tests its operational implication in language-model training,
where neither the population gradient nor its MSE is observable.  If ESS marks
the predicted reliability transition, a conservative intervention should retain
permissive updates while ESS is high and prevent their late failure after ESS
falls.  The experiment asks whether this reliability transition remains visible
at language-model scale.

\subsection{A minimal intervention}

The supplied run matrix evaluates $0.1$ as a prespecified heuristic:
when $\widehat\rho\ge0.1$, the method retains the permissive update; when
$\widehat\rho<0.1$, it activates the safeguard associated with the comparison.
The permissive branch in the main hybrids is a CISPO/GPD-style detached token
coefficient with cap 3.  The low-ESS branch applies conventional GRPO masking,
Clip-Higher \citep{yu2025dapo}, or the divergence rule of DPPO
\citep{qi2026dppo}.  This coefficient cap is dense and retains token scores; it
is not the full-sequence scalar truncation analyzed in
Section~\ref{sec:ratio-truncation}.

\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{LLM}}
  =\begin{cases}
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{permissive}},&\widehat\rho\ge0.1,\\
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{safeguard}},&\widehat\rho<0.1.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:llm-gate}
\end{equation}
The run matrix contains two kinds of evidence.  The DPPO and cap-3 panels are
with-versus-without-gate comparisons.  The GRPO and Clip-Higher panels instead
compare an always-masked baseline with a cap-3-based ESS hybrid; no
identical-loss ungated/gated pair was run for those panels.  The trigger is
computed from raw sequence ratios for the GRPO-band and DPPO hybrids, but the
supplied Clip-Higher/cap-3 hybrid uses shaped post-cap ESS.  We report these
implementation differences explicitly because only raw sequence ESS matches
$\widehat\rho$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:sample-ess}.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

We optimize Qwen3-30B-A3B for 200 training steps and evaluate on AIME-2024 with
mean@16.  The primary comparison is the complete evaluation trajectory and its
step-200 endpoint.  We also align normalized sequence ESS and the fraction of
clipped updates to the training axis to verify that the rule responds to a loss
of effective support.  The supplied artifacts report one trajectory per
configuration, so we report endpoint differences without uncertainty and treat
them as trajectory-level evidence.

\section{Conclusion}

The role of conservative intervention changes as fixed rollout data cease to
represent the current learner.  The theory follows one reward objective across
that change.  Raw full-sequence importance sampling satisfies
$L_Q(\theta)=J(\theta)$ exactly, while current sequence ESS controls the gap
between its unused-minibatch estimate and population improvement over the
rollout policy and isolates the sequence-ratio amplification factor in the
singleton raw-gradient MSE and an intact-group upper bound.  Population
smoothness then converts the full current-gradient error into
a finite-update reward bound.  For plain gradient ascent, the resulting
condition is explicit: expected reward progress is positive when current
gradient MSE is smaller than squared population-gradient signal.

The intervention analysis also separates three objects that are often called
clipping.  PPO/GRPO masks selected token gradients, CISPO caps detached token
coefficients while retaining their scores, and full-sequence truncation changes
the scalar importance estimator.  Only full-sequence truncation directly
inherits the bounded conservative objective certificate.  Its comparison with
the raw estimator is explicit: the truncated certificate is larger when the
reduction in confidence radius exceeds the sampled reward signal it discards.
The contextual-bandit and language-model trajectories support sample ESS as an
operational diagnostic.  A direct next test is empirical coverage of the
finite-update bound and the raw-versus-full-sequence-truncated confidence
crossover on a strictly disjoint one-epoch traversal.

\subsection{Task and correspondence to RLVR}

embed this task in the grouped rollout and minibatch structure used by RLVR

The testbed uses a sequence policy, not a language model.  Its token heads are
conditionally independent so that the population gradient remains exact, but
its response ratio is the same product of token ratios that appears in RLVR.
During training, the learner observes only sampled responses, rollout log
probabilities, and verifier rewards.  Labels are used only by the oracle that
evaluates the finite-population reward and gradient.  This separation preserves
the RLVR feedback structure while exposing the quantities needed to test the
theory.

\subsection{Finite population and initial policy}

Optdigits is a handwritten-digit classification benchmark with 5,620 labeled
examples
\citep{alpaydin1998optdigits}.  Each original $32\times32$ binary image is
partitioned into $4\times4$ blocks; foreground counts produce 64 integer
features in $\{0,\ldots,16\}$.  We concatenate the supplied 3,823 training and
1,797 test examples, divide each feature by 16, and append an intercept.  This
gives a finite population $\{(x_j,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{M}$ with $M=5{,}620$ and
$x_j\in\mathbb{R}^{65}$.  The original split is not used because the estimand
is finite-population policy optimization, not classification generalization.

Each label $y\in\{0,\ldots,9\}$ has a four-bit binary representation.  Let
$c(y)\in\{0,1\}^{16}$ repeat this code four times.  The repetition changes only
the response length, not which answer is correct, and creates a sequence ratio
that compounds over 16 token probabilities.  The policy has one Bernoulli head
per token position,
\begin{align}
  p_{\theta,\ell}(x)&=\sigma(\theta_\ell^\top x),\nonumber\\
  P_\theta(a_{1:16}\mid x)
  &=\prod_{\ell=1}^{16}
    p_{\theta,\ell}(x)^{a_\ell}
    \{1-p_{\theta,\ell}(x)\}^{1-a_\ell},
  \qquad \theta\in\mathbb{R}^{16\times65}.
  \label{eq:optdigits-policy}
\end{align}
The verifier reward is
$R(x_j,a_{1:16})=\mathbf{1}\{a_{1:16}=c(y_j)\}$.

Initialization is deterministic and uses no experimental outcomes.  Starting
from zero, we fit all token heads for 500 full-population logistic-regression
steps with step size $0.8$ and no regularization.  We then multiply the fitted
weights by $0.55$ to keep the initial policy stochastic, which gives exact
initial reward $V(\theta_0)=0.095766$.  This procedure uses no data split,
minibatching, early stopping, or model selection.

For compactness, define
\begin{equation}
  q_j(\theta)
  :=P_\theta(c(y_j)\mid x_j)
  =\prod_{\ell=1}^{16}
  \left[c_{j\ell}p_{j\ell}
  +(1-c_{j\ell})(1-p_{j\ell})\right],
  \label{eq:correct-sequence-probability}
\end{equation}
where $c_{j\ell}=c(y_j)_\ell$ and
$p_{j\ell}=p_{\theta,\ell}(x_j)$.  The finite-population objective is
\begin{equation}
  V(\theta)=\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^{M}q_j(\theta).
  \label{eq:optdigits-value}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Exact gradient and group-relative advantage}

The Bernoulli score identity gives the exact finite-population gradient
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_{\theta_\ell}V(\theta)
  =\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^{M}
    q_j(\theta)\{c_{j\ell}-p_{j\ell}\}x_j.
  \label{eq:optdigits-gradient}
\end{equation}
where $q_j(\theta)$ is the correct-sequence probability,
$c_{j\ell}$ is its bit at position $\ell$, and $p_{j\ell}$ is the policy
probability of bit one.  The oracle enumerates all 5,620 contexts at the current
pre-update policy.  We measure a sampled estimator by
\begin{equation}
  E_t=\|\widehat g_t-\nabla V(\theta_t)\|_F^2,
  \label{eq:optdigits-gradient-error}
\end{equation}
and report its mean within each fixed ESS interval.

For response $g$ to prompt $i$, we use the leave-one-out advantage
\begin{equation}
  A_{ig}=R_{ig}-\frac1{G-1}\sum_{h\ne g}R_{ih}
  =\frac{G}{G-1}(R_{ig}-\bar R_i).
  \label{eq:loo-group-advantage}
\end{equation}
It has the sign of the group-centered GRPO advantage
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}; standard-deviation scaling is omitted so every
minibatch targets the same population gradient.  The baseline excludes the
focal response and is therefore independent of it conditional on the prompt.
Importance weighting and the zero-mean score identity then preserve the target
in Equation~\eqref{eq:optdigits-gradient}.

\subsection{Rollout and minibatch procedure}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{RLVR-style OptDigits policy optimization}
\label{alg:bandit-loop}
\centering
\fbox{\begin{minipage}{0.92\linewidth}
\small
\textbf{Input:} initial policy $\theta_0$; eight rollouts; 128 prompts per
rollout; $G=16$ responses per prompt; 16 minibatches; one optimization epoch.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Set the rollout policy $Q\leftarrow P_\theta$.  Sample prompts without
  replacement from the finite population and sample grouped responses, then
  compute verifier rewards and leave-one-out advantages.
  \item Randomly partition the intact prompt groups into 16 minibatches, each
  containing eight groups and $N=128$ complete responses.
  \item Traverse each minibatch once.  Before every update, recompute
  $W=P_\theta(Y\mid x)/Q(Y\mid x)$, policy scores, and sample ESS.  Form the
  designated raw, PPO-masked, or conditional estimator.
  \item After all 16 updates, discard the rollout and repeat from the new policy.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}}
\end{algorithm}

Every response enters exactly one update.  Within a rollout, responses,
rewards, advantages, and rollout probabilities remain fixed, whereas current
probabilities, ratios, scores, and ESS are recomputed before each update.
This realizes the theorem's disjoint no-reuse structure, but prompt sampling
without replacement means that later unused groups are not conditionally
i.i.d. draws from the global prompt population after earlier adaptive updates.
The study therefore tests the theorem's observable quantities rather than
claiming literal coverage of its conditional statement.  Sixteen minibatches
are used because the first four never reach ESS below $0.1$ in the diagnostic
runs; a shorter traversal would not expose the regime under study.  The
separate scaled optimization uses 512 prompts per rollout, the same 16
minibatches (32 prompt groups or 512 sequences per update), and four epochs;
that repeated-data trajectory is an explicit stress test beyond the theorem.

\subsection{Estimators and diagnostics}

Let $S_{ig,t}=\nabla_\theta\log
P_{\theta_{r,t-1}}(Y_{ig}\mid x_i)$.  For each $N=128$ minibatch, the
permissive and PPO-masked gradients are
\begin{align}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{raw},t}
  &=\frac1N\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t}A_{ig}S_{ig,t},
    \label{eq:simulation-raw-gradient}\\
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{PPO},t}
  &=\frac1N\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t}M_{ig,t}^{\mathrm{seq}}A_{ig}S_{ig,t},
    \label{eq:simulation-ppo-gradient}
\end{align}
where the experiment uses the response-level analogue
\begin{equation}
  M_{ig,t}^{\mathrm{seq}}
  =\mathbf{1}\{A_{ig}\ge0,W_{ig,t}\le1+\epsilon\}
   +\mathbf{1}\{A_{ig}<0,W_{ig,t}\ge1-\epsilon\},
  \qquad \epsilon=0.2.
  \label{eq:simulation-sequence-mask}
\end{equation}
This construction preserves the advantage-sign masking mechanism of
Proposition~\ref{prop:ppo-mask}, but applies it once to the complete-response
ratio rather than separately to token ratios.  It is therefore a controlled
sequence-level masking analogue, not the exact token-local PPO/GRPO gradient and
not numerical ratio truncation.  The third method uses
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{cond},t}
  =\begin{cases}
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{raw},t},&\widehat\rho_t\ge0.1,\\
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{PPO},t},&\widehat\rho_t<0.1,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad
  \widehat\rho_t
  =\frac{(\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t})^2}
  {N\sum_{i,g}W_{ig,t}^2}.
  \label{eq:simulation-gate}
\end{equation}
The $0.1$ threshold is fixed before the runs and is not selected from a reported
outcome.  All methods share the initialization, prompt draws, response-sampling
uniforms, and group permutations within each of 100 paired replications.

The first 20 conditional replications provide 2,560 pre-update diagnostics.  At
each state, we evaluate the raw gradient against
Equation~\eqref{eq:optdigits-gradient} and compare its counterfactual reward
step with the exact-gradient step.  Results use seven prespecified ESS intervals:
\[
  \begin{gathered}
    \relax[0,0.03),\ [0.03,0.1),\ [0.1,0.2),\ [0.2,0.4),\\
    [0.4,0.6),\ [0.6,0.8),\ [0.8,1].
  \end{gathered}
\]
Within-bin error bars are standard errors.  Optimization curves use all 100
replications and report relative population-reward improvement from the common
initial policy with 95\% confidence intervals.  One rollout costs 2,048
verifier responses.

\subsection{Fixed-checkpoint crossover diagnostic}

To estimate conditional MSE rather than one realized error, we freeze the
rollout and current policies at selected pre-update checkpoints and redraw
independent minibatches.  This diagnostic does not change training.

The finite Bernoulli policy also makes population sequence ESS exact.  For
prompt $x$ and token position $\ell$, let $q_{x\ell}$ and $p_{x\ell}$ denote
the rollout and current probabilities of token one.  Independence across the
16 response positions gives
\begin{align}
  \E_Q[W^2\mid x]
  &=\prod_{\ell=1}^{16}
    \left\{
      \frac{p_{x\ell}^2}{q_{x\ell}}
      +\frac{(1-p_{x\ell})^2}{1-q_{x\ell}}
    \right\},
    \label{eq:simulation-weight-second-moment}\\
  \rho_{\mathrm{pop}}
  &=\left\{
    \frac1{5620}\sum_{x=1}^{5620}\E_Q[W^2\mid x]
  \right\}^{-1}.
    \label{eq:simulation-population-ess}
\end{align}
These equations use $\E_Q[W]=1$ and contain no minibatch noise.  We evaluate
eight checkpoints from each of the seven ESS intervals, giving 56 frozen states
selected from the 2,560 trajectory checkpoints.  At each state, 32 paired
redraws compare raw and PPO-masked estimates.  One independent block contains
eight prompts and 16 responses per prompt, hence $N=128$ sequences; the
$N=512$ estimate averages four blocks.  The block is the independent unit
because responses within a prompt share an advantage.  For checkpoint $c$,
estimator $e$, and size $N$, we report
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{c,e,N}
  =\frac1{32}\sum_{s=1}^{32}
    \left\|
      \widehat g_{c,e,N}^{(s)}-\nabla V(\theta_c)
    \right\|_F^2.
  \label{eq:simulation-crossover-mse}
\end{equation}
We average redraws within a state and states within an ESS interval; standard
errors use the eight states as independent units.  PPO reduction is
$100(\widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\mathrm{raw}}-
\widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\mathrm{PPO}})/
\widehat{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\mathrm{raw}}$.  Positive values favor masking.

\subsection{Formula calibration and held-out decision test}

We split the eight states in each ESS interval into four calibration and four
test states, yielding 28 of each.  At every state,
100 paired $N=128$ blocks estimate the raw covariance trace $\widehat V_W$, the
PPO covariance trace $\widehat V_C$, and the PPO squared bias $\widehat B_C$.
The squared-bias estimate subtracts the sampling variance of the estimated mean
and is truncated at zero.  Averaging $K\in\{1,2,4,8\}$ independent blocks gives
\begin{equation}
  \widehat R_W(K):=\frac{\widehat V_W}{K},
  \qquad
  \widehat R_C(K):=\widehat B_C+\frac{\widehat V_C}{K}.
  \label{eq:formula-risks}
\end{equation}
These are the two sides of the diagnostic decomposition in
Equation~\eqref{eq:crossover} at block level.  For each
$K$, we choose the ESS midpoint that minimizes fitted risk while applying PPO
below the threshold and raw above it.  The only interior calibration solution is
$K=4$, or $N=512$, with threshold $0.417$.

Held-out evaluation uses 100 paired redraws at each test state.
The fitted threshold never sees test-state errors; standard errors use the 28
states as independent units.  The subsequent optimization study freezes that
threshold, uses 512 sequences per update, traverses each rollout for four epochs,
and runs eight rollout cycles in 100 paired replications.  Sample ESS, rather
than population ESS, determines the training intervention.

The fixed-$0.1$ trajectory also records shadow raw and PPO gradients whenever
sample ESS is below $0.417$.  Comparing each with the exact gradient yields the
three intervals $[0,0.1)$, $[0.1,0.25)$, and $[0.25,0.417)$.  These shadow
calculations do not alter training.  Means and standard errors use the 100
replications as independent units.

\section{Proofs for the theoretical results}
\label{app:proofs}

The main text states one concise reliability theorem.  This appendix proves it
through three supporting lemmas, then records the grouped and adaptive forms
used to interpret one-pass RLVR.

\subsection{Supporting lemmas for Theorem~\ref{thm:ess-clipping}}

\begin{lemma}[Change of measure and ESS identity]
\label{lem:change-measure}
Under the assumptions of Section~\ref{sec:setting},
\[
  \Delta(P,Q)=\E_Q[(W-1)R],
  \qquad
  \E_Q[(W-1)^2]=\rho(P,Q)^{-1}-1.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $W=dP/dQ$ and the prompt marginal is shared,
\[
  J(P)=\E_Q[WR].
\]
Subtracting $J(Q)=\E_Q[R]$ gives the first identity.  Also
$\E_Q[W]=1$, and hence
\[
  \E_Q[(W-1)^2]
  =\E_Q[W^2]-2\E_Q[W]+1
  =\E_Q[W^2]-1
  =\rho(P,Q)^{-1}-1.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Unclipped reliability]
\label{lem:raw-reliability}
Let $\widehat\Delta_W$ be defined by
Equation~\eqref{eq:raw-improvement-estimator}.  Then it is unbiased and
\[
  \E[(\widehat\Delta_W-\Delta(P,Q))^2]
  \le
  \frac{R_{\max}^2}{N}\{\rho(P,Q)^{-1}-1\}.
\]
For any $0<\alpha<1$, with probability at least $1-\alpha$,
\[
  \Delta(P,Q)
  \ge
  \widehat\Delta_W-\mathfrak r_W(\rho,\alpha).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Set $Y=(W-1)R$.  Lemma~\ref{lem:change-measure} gives
$\E_Q[Y]=\Delta(P,Q)$.  Independence therefore yields
\[
  \E[(\widehat\Delta_W-\Delta(P,Q))^2]
  =\frac1N\Var_Q(Y)
  \le\frac1N\E_Q[Y^2].
\]
Since $0\le R\le R_{\max}$,
\[
  \E_Q[Y^2]
  \le
  R_{\max}^2\E_Q[(W-1)^2]
  =
  R_{\max}^2\{\rho(P,Q)^{-1}-1\}.
\]
For the one-sided statement, Cantelli's inequality gives
\[
  \Pr\{\widehat\Delta_W-\Delta(P,Q)\ge a\}
  \le
  \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+a^2},
\]
where
$\sigma^2=\Var(\widehat\Delta_W)$.  Set
$a=\sigma\sqrt{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}$ and upper-bound $\sigma^2$ by the
preceding display.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Capped reliability]
\label{lem:capped-reliability}
Fix $c\ge1$ before observing the batch and let $\widehat\Delta_c$ be defined by
Equation~\eqref{eq:capped-improvement-estimator}.  Then
\[
  \E[\widehat\Delta_c]
  =
  \Delta(P,Q)-\E_Q[(W-c)_+R]
  \le\Delta(P,Q).
\]
For any $0<\alpha<1$, with probability at least $1-\alpha$,
\[
  \Delta(P,Q)
  \ge
  \widehat\Delta_c-\mathfrak r_c(c,\alpha).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Because $W-\min(W,c)=(W-c)_+$,
\[
  \Delta(P,Q)-\E[\widehat\Delta_c]
  =
  \E_Q[(W-c)_+R]\ge0.
\]
Each random variable
$\{\min(W,c)-1\}R$ lies in
$[-R_{\max},(c-1)R_{\max}]$, an interval of width $cR_{\max}$.
Hoeffding's inequality therefore gives
\[
  \E[\widehat\Delta_c]
  \ge
  \widehat\Delta_c
  -
  cR_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{2N}}
\]
with probability at least $1-\alpha$.  Combining the two inequalities proves
the claim.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:ess-clipping}}

\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:raw-mse} is
Lemma~\ref{lem:raw-reliability}.  Apply
Lemmas~\ref{lem:raw-reliability} and
\ref{lem:capped-reliability} with
$\alpha=\delta/2$.  A union bound makes both lower bounds valid
simultaneously with probability at least $1-\delta$, proving
Equation~\eqref{eq:joint-certificate}.

The capped lower bound exceeds the unclipped lower bound exactly when
\[
  \widehat\Delta_c-\mathfrak r_c(c,\alpha)
  >
  \widehat\Delta_W-\mathfrak r_W(\rho,\alpha).
\]
Rearranging gives the first inequality in
Equation~\eqref{eq:certificate-crossover}.  Finally,
\[
  \widehat\Delta_W-\widehat\Delta_c
  =
  \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N
  \{W_i-\min(W_i,c)\}R_i
  =
  \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N(W_i-c)_+R_i.
\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{One-pass conditioning and intact prompt groups}

\begin{lemma}[Conditional one-pass form]
\label{lem:conditional-one-pass}
Let $\mathcal F_{k-1}$ contain all information before update $k$.  If the
current policy $P_k$, cap $c_k$, and confidence allocation are
$\mathcal F_{k-1}$-measurable and the unused minibatch is conditionally sampled
from $Q$, then Theorem~\ref{thm:ess-clipping} holds conditionally on
$\mathcal F_{k-1}$ with $P=P_k$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
After conditioning on $\mathcal F_{k-1}$, the policy and cap are fixed and the
unused observations have the sampling law assumed by the theorem.  Each step
of Lemmas~\ref{lem:change-measure}--\ref{lem:capped-reliability} can therefore
be read as a conditional expectation, variance, and probability statement.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Intact prompt groups]
\label{lem:intact-groups}
Suppose $m$ prompts are independent and each prompt has $G$ conditionally
independent responses.  Form the within-prompt average of either the unclipped
or capped contribution and then average across prompts.  The conclusions of
Theorem~\ref{thm:ess-clipping} remain valid with $N=m$ and with $\rho$ computed
for a uniformly selected response from a fresh prompt group.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For the unclipped contribution write
$Y_{ig}=(W_{ig}-1)R_{ig}$ and
$\bar Y_i=G^{-1}\sum_{g=1}^G Y_{ig}$.  The groups are independent,
$\E[\bar Y_i]=\Delta(P,Q)$, and Jensen's inequality gives
\[
  \E[\bar Y_i^2]
  \le
  \frac1G\sum_{g=1}^G\E[Y_{ig}^2]
  \le
  R_{\max}^2\{\rho(P,Q)^{-1}-1\}.
\]
Averaging the $m$ independent group contributions therefore yields the same
MSE and Cantelli argument with $N=m$.  For the cap, every member contribution
lies in an interval of width $cR_{\max}$, so its group average lies in the same
interval.  Hoeffding's inequality across the $m$ independent groups completes
the capped result.  This bound is conservative because it counts independent
prompts rather than treating all within-prompt responses as independent.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:ppo-mask}}

\begin{proof}
If $A\ge0$, the minimum in Equation~\eqref{eq:ppo-objective} selects
$r_tA$ for $r_t\le1+\epsilon$ and the constant $(1+\epsilon)A$ otherwise.
Its derivative is therefore
$r_tA\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t})$ in the first region and zero
in the second.  If $A<0$, the minimum selects the constant
$(1-\epsilon)A$ for $r_t<1-\epsilon$ and $r_tA$ otherwise.  Summing the two
cases proves Equation~\eqref{eq:ppo-gradient}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Derivation of the gradient-MSE crossover}

The permissive estimator is unbiased, so independence gives
\[
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  =
  \frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W).
\]
For the modified estimator,
$\E[\widehat g_C]-g=b_C$.  Expanding around its own mean eliminates the cross
term:
\[
  \E\|\widehat g_C-g\|_2^2
  =
  \|b_C\|_2^2
  +
  \frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_C).
\]
Subtracting the two risks proves Equation~\eqref{eq:crossover}.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:gradient-progress}}

\begin{proof}
Smoothness gives
\[
  J(\theta+\eta\widehat g)-J(\theta)
  \ge
  \eta g^\top\widehat g
  -
  \frac{L\eta^2}{2}\|\widehat g\|_2^2.
\]
Using
$2g^\top\widehat g
=\|g\|_2^2+\|\widehat g\|_2^2-\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2$
and $L\eta\le1$ yields
\[
  J(\theta+\eta\widehat g)-J(\theta)
  \ge
  \frac{\eta}{2}
  \{\|g\|_2^2-\|\widehat g-g\|_2^2\}.
\]
Taking expectation proves
Equation~\eqref{eq:expected-population-gain}.
\end{proof}
