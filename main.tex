\begin{abstract}
Permissive policy optimization can learn rapidly from rollout data and then
fail abruptly after many successful updates. Policy-deviation bounds explain
why a large change can be unsafe, but they do not say when a finite rollout
stops supporting an accurate update for the current policy. We study this
transition through two ordered questions. First, when is the ordinary
full-trajectory importance-sampling (IS) estimator still reliable? We show
that normalized effective sample size (ESS) controls the policy-mismatch
component of its finite-sample error and, through smoothness, yields a
high-probability one-step improvement guarantee. Second, when does coefficient
control provide a better update? Lower gradient-estimation MSE is not enough:
the controlled estimator must also retain sufficient alignment with the
population gradient. We capture this requirement with a full fixed-step
improvement certificate. A Gaussian bandit instantiates the finite-moment ESS
reliability bound, estimator crossover, and one-step policy improvement.
Although TIS-3 has the lowest MSE along the displayed path, IS gives the better
update at high ESS; only after effective support contracts does TIS-3 overtake
it, while the more aggressive PPO mask never reaches the oracle envelope.
In the reported large-model trajectories, ESS-conditioned safeguards preserve
early learning while preventing late failure.

\end{abstract}

Classical trust-region theory explains why moving too far from the rollout
policy can be unsafe
\citep{schulman2015trpo,schulman2017ppo,qi2026dppo}. It does not, however,
resolve the delayed transition in Figure~\ref{fig:llm-motivation}: failure
follows many apparently successful steps rather than one conspicuous move.
Repeated updates can progressively concentrate complete-trajectory likelihood
weights, reducing the effective support provided by the rollout even when the
empirical update uses factorwise coefficients. A policy-deviation bound
controls a population approximation; it does not track the statistical
adequacy of the available rollout. The practical question is therefore: when
does a finite rollout cease to support a reliable next update?

We answer this question in two stages. We first analyze the ordinary
full-trajectory importance-sampling (IS) estimator, for which normalized
effective sample size (ESS) replaces the nominal batch size $N$ with the
effective count $N\rho$ in the finite-sample error bound. Combined with
smoothness, this yields an ESS-dependent one-step improvement guarantee. We
then compare the factorwise coefficient-controlled estimators used in practice.
Low ESS indicates when the IS guarantee weakens, but it does not determine
which alternative update is better. Clipping or truncation can reduce sampling
variation and MSE while also removing useful population-gradient alignment. We
therefore derive a full fixed-step certificate that determines when the risk
reduction is sufficient to justify the signal removed by control.

An analytically tractable Gaussian bandit then follows the theory in order.
It first checks the finite-moment ESS reliability bound and safe-step guarantee,
then computes the pairwise certificate crossovers of TIS and PPO against IS.
The result
isolates the missing term in an MSE-only comparison: TIS-3 has the lowest MSE
throughout the displayed path, yet IS remains the better update at high ESS
because it retains more useful alignment. Large-model trajectories establish
the operational consequence. Sample ESS exposes support loss under both TIS 3
and an unclipped update, while ESS-conditioned safeguards preserve permissive
learning and activate control only in the low-support regime. On
Qwen3-30B-A3B, ungated TIS 3 collapses to $0.417\%$, whereas the
ESS-conditioned TIS-3/Clip-Higher trajectory retains $44.8\%$ at step 200.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Policy-deviation guarantees.}
Trust-region and proximal methods control policy movement to obtain stable
improvement guarantees or practical update rules
\citep{schulman2015trpo,schulman2017ppo,qi2026dppo}. These analyses provide the
standard explanation for why large policy changes can be unsafe. Our framework
is complementary: it studies when a fixed finite rollout ceases to provide a
reliable estimate of the current policy gradient. The distinction is between a
population-level approximation guarantee and a finite-sample reliability
question.

\paragraph{Importance sampling and effective support.}
Importance-sampling policy optimization uses likelihood-ratio moments and
R\'enyi divergence to quantify the reliability of off-policy estimators
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}. In these results, effective rather than
nominal sample size controls estimation error. We apply the same principle
directly to the vector-valued policy-gradient estimator and use normalized ESS
as the regime coordinate.

\paragraph{Reliable policy-gradient updates.}
Smoothness-based analyses translate the error of a finite gradient estimate into
a lower bound on policy improvement and use this relation to choose step sizes
or sample sizes \citep{pirotta2013adaptive,papini2022smoothing}. We combine
this optimization argument with an ESS-dependent off-policy reliability bound.

\paragraph{Stability in RLVR.}
Recent methods alter ratio boundaries, sequence weights, divergence controls,
or update rules to improve the stability of large-model reinforcement learning
\citep{yu2025dapo,park2025clipping,minimax2025m1,zheng2025m2po,shen2026vespo,
huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}. Rather than proposing another static boundary,
we study the statistical transition that makes a permissive update unreliable
and then ask when an intervention can recover reliability.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:setting}

This section provides the background and notation used in the following
analysis.

\subsection{Policy-gradient estimators from a fixed rollout}

\paragraph{Problem formulation.}
Let $X\sim\nu$ denote a context, $Q(\cdot\mid X)$ the rollout policy, and
$P_\theta(\cdot\mid X)$ the current policy. A complete sample is $Z=(X,Y)$,
where $Y=(Y_1,\ldots,Y_T)$ and $R(Z)$ is its reward. For each factor $t$,
define the likelihood ratio and score
\begin{equation}
 r_{\theta,t}(Z)
 :=\frac{P_\theta(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t})}{Q(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t})},
 \qquad
 \ell_{\theta,t}(Z):=\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y_t\mid X,Y_{<t}).
 \label{eq:factor-ratio-score}
\end{equation}
Here $t$ indexes a policy factor, such as a token or decision step. Because
both policies factorize along the trajectory, the complete-sample likelihood
ratio is
\begin{equation}
 W_\theta(Z):=\frac{P_\theta(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)}
 =\prod_{t=1}^T r_{\theta,t}(Z).
 \label{eq:sequence-ratio}
\end{equation}

The learning objective is to maximize the expected reward
$J(\theta):=\E_{P_\theta}[R]$. Let $A(Z):=R(Z)-b(X)$ be a baseline-centered
reward, where $b(X)$ does not depend on $Y$. Under
$P_\theta(\cdot\mid X)\ll Q(\cdot\mid X)$, change of measure expresses the
population gradient under the rollout distribution:
\begin{equation}
 g(\theta):=\nabla J(\theta)=\E_Q[W_\theta f_\theta],
 \qquad
 f_\theta(Z):=A(Z)\sum_{t=1}^T \ell_{\theta,t}(Z)
 =A(Z)\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y\mid X).
 \label{eq:true-gradient}
\end{equation}
While trust-region arguments motivate safeguards such as TRPO and PPO-style clipping \citep{schulman2015trpo,schulman2017ppo}, these arguments are typically made
at the population level. Practical algorithms, however, do not observe
$g(\theta)$ and must rely on its finite-sample counterpart. Given i.i.d.
rollout samples $Z_i\sim\nu(dx)Q(dy\mid x)$, $i=1,\ldots,N$, the ordinary
full-trajectory importance-sampling (IS) policy-gradient estimator is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat g_N(\theta)
 :=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N W_{\theta,i}f_{\theta,i}.
 \label{eq:is-gradient-estimator}
\end{equation}
The importance-weighted nature of this estimator can produce poor agreement
between the finite-sample update and the population gradient
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}. This exposes an important but often
overlooked question: when is the finite-sample gradient reliable?

\paragraph{Choice of gradient estimator.}
To formalize this comparison, write
$\mathbf r_\theta=(r_{\theta,1},\ldots,r_{\theta,T})$ and let
$u=(u_1,\ldots,u_T)$ be a collection of coefficient rules, with
$u_t:\mathbb R_+^T\times\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$. Define
\begin{equation}
 \psi_\theta(u;Z)
 :=A(Z)\sum_{t=1}^T
 u_t(\mathbf r_\theta,A(Z))\ell_{\theta,t}(Z),
 \qquad
 \widehat g_N(u;\theta):=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\psi_\theta(u;Z_i).
 \label{eq:coefficient-rule-estimator}
\end{equation}

Existing ratio-based policy-gradient estimators can be represented by
particular choices of $u$. The IS estimator sets
$u_{\mathrm{IS},t}=W_\theta$ for every factor. One popular alternative is PPO/GRPO
ratio clipping, whose clipped surrogate induces the following gradient-masking
rule with radius $0<\epsilon<1$
\citep{schulman2017ppo,shao2024deepseekmath}:
\begin{equation}
 u_{\mathrm{P},t}(\mathbf r,A)
 :=r_t\left[
 \mathbf 1\{A\ge0,\ r_t\le1+\epsilon\}
 +\mathbf 1\{A<0,\ r_t\ge1-\epsilon\}
 \right].
 \label{eq:ppo-coefficient-rule}
\end{equation}

Such clipping rules create a conventional bias--variance tradeoff. More
aggressive clipping can reduce sampling variation, but it can also increase
bias by removing gradient terms. This raises the natural question: what governs
the reliability of a gradient estimator, and when is clipping worth the signal
it removes? We answer the first part by introducing R\'enyi divergence and
effective sample size (ESS).

\subsection{Gradient estimators and R\'enyi divergence}

The dispersion of the importance weights indicates how dissimilar $P_\theta$
and $Q$ are. R\'enyi divergence formalizes this notion as an
information-theoretic dissimilarity between probability measures
\citep{renyi1961entropy,vanerven2014renyi}, while importance-sampling analyses
connect it to the finite-sample behavior of importance-weighted means
\citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.

R\'enyi divergence is a family of measures indexed by a moment order
$\alpha>1$. For the current and rollout policies, define
\begin{equation}
 D_\alpha(P_\theta\|Q)
 :=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\E_Q[W_\theta^\alpha],
 \qquad
 d_\alpha(P_\theta\|Q):=\exp\{D_\alpha(P_\theta\|Q)\}.
 \label{eq:renyi-divergence}
\end{equation}
Larger values of $\alpha$ place progressively more emphasis on rare, large
likelihood ratios. The order-two case is central because
$\E_Q[W_\theta^2]$ controls standard MSE bounds for bounded
importance-weighted contributions. Importantly, its reciprocal is the
normalized effective sample size \citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}:
\begin{equation}
 \rho_\theta
 :=\frac{1}{d_2(P_\theta\|Q)}
 =\frac{1}{\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
 =\exp\{-D_2(P_\theta\|Q)\}.
 \label{eq:population-ess}
\end{equation}
Its observable normalized sample analogue is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\rho_\theta
 :=\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^N W_{\theta,i}\right)^2}
 {N\sum_{i=1}^N W_{\theta,i}^2}.
 \label{eq:sample-ess}
\end{equation}
It lies in $[1/N,1]$ and is computed directly from the complete-trajectory
weights in the batch used for the update.
When $\E_Q[W_\theta^2]<\infty$, Jensen's inequality and
$\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$ give $\rho_\theta\in(0,1]$, and $N\rho_\theta$ is the
effective sample count corresponding to nominal batch size $N$. As the second
moment of the likelihood ratio grows, both $\rho_\theta$ and this effective
count contract. The identity
$\E_Q[(W_\theta-1)^2]=\rho_\theta^{-1}-1$ shows that $\rho_\theta$ is exactly
the inverse second-moment measure of ratio dispersion.

Importance-sampling theory makes this quantity operational. If
$\|f_\theta\|_\infty:=\sup_z\|f_\theta(z)\|_2<\infty$, the MSE of the IS estimator is at
most $\|f_\theta\|_\infty^2/(N\rho_\theta)$. Normalized ESS therefore captures
the policy-mismatch component of IS reliability. The next section
determines when that reliability is sufficient for improvement and then asks
whether coefficient control offers a better update.

\section{Theoretical Analysis}
\label{sec:theory}

This section answers the preceding questions in order. We first connect
gradient-estimator reliability to policy improvement, then use normalized ESS
to control the reliability of the IS estimator, and finally ask when clipping
provides the stronger update.

\subsection{Gradient-estimator reliability and policy improvement}

The preceding section distinguishes the finite-sample IS estimate from the
population gradient it targets. Here, we connect estimator reliability to a
lower bound on policy improvement. The following lemma formalizes this
connection for an arbitrary gradient estimator.

\begin{lemma}[A reliable gradient estimate yields policy improvement]
\label{lem:gradient-error-progress}
Let $\widehat g$ be an estimate of $g=\nabla J(\theta)$ satisfying
$\|\widehat g-g\|_2\le\varepsilon$. Suppose $J$ is $L$-smooth along the
update segment for $L>0$, and define
$\gamma=L^{-1}(1-\varepsilon/\|\widehat g\|_2)_+$, with $\gamma=0$ when
$\widehat g=0$. Then
\begin{equation}
 J(\theta+\gamma\widehat g)-J(\theta)
 \ge
 \frac{1}{2L}
 \left(\|\widehat g\|_2-\varepsilon\right)_+^2.
 \label{eq:gradient-error-progress}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

This deterministic lemma uses the same smoothness mechanism as existing
policy-gradient analyses
\citep{pirotta2013adaptive,papini2022smoothing}. Its implication is direct:
the error radius measures how far the finite-sample direction can lie from the
population gradient. When this radius is smaller than
$\|\widehat g\|_2$, the estimator yields a positive lower bound on policy
improvement.

\paragraph{ESS and the IS update.}
However, the population gradient is unknown, so this reliability cannot be
evaluated directly. Normalized ESS provides the bridge: for the IS estimator,
it converts likelihood-ratio dispersion into a bound on gradient-estimation
error.
At update $k$, condition on the preceding updates and write
$W_k:=W_{\theta_k}$, $f_k:=f_{\theta_k}$,
$g_k:=g(\theta_k)$, $\widehat g_k:=\widehat g_N(\theta_k)$, and
$\rho_k:=\rho_{\theta_k}$. Assume that the $N$ rollout samples are independent
draws from $Q$, $d_2(P_{\theta_k}\|Q)<\infty$, and
$\E_Q[W_k^2\|f_k\|_2^2]<\infty$. Define the
importance-weighted contribution scale
\begin{equation}
 M_{2,k}:=
 \frac{\E_Q[W_k^2\|f_k\|_2^2]}{\E_Q[W_k^2]}
 =\rho_k\E_Q[W_k^2\|f_k\|_2^2].
 \label{eq:weighted-contribution-scale}
\end{equation}
This factorizes the weighted second moment as $M_{2,k}/\rho_k$: $\rho_k$
records ratio dispersion, while $M_{2,k}$ also captures its coupling with the
score--advantage magnitude.

Because the IS estimator is unbiased, its exact mean-squared error is
\begin{equation}
 \E\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2
 =\frac1N\left\{\frac{M_{2,k}}{\rho_k}-\|g_k\|_2^2\right\}.
 \label{eq:rho-gradient-mse}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 \E\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2
 \le
 \frac{M_{2,k}}{N\rho_k}.
 \label{eq:rho-gradient-mse-bound}
\end{equation}
Likelihood-ratio dispersion therefore enters through the effective count
$N\rho_k$, replacing the nominal sample size $N$, while $M_{2,k}$ records the
remaining contribution scale. If $f_k$ is bounded, then
$M_{2,k}\le\|f_k\|_\infty^2$, recovering the familiar bounded-integrand form.
Equation~\eqref{eq:rho-gradient-mse-bound} controls the mean-squared error; the
next lemma converts this result into a high-probability error radius for the
realized update.

\begin{lemma}[Normalized ESS controls the gradient-error radius]
\label{lem:rho-gradient-reliability}
Under the preceding setup, for any $0<\delta\le1$, with probability at least
$1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}
 \|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2
 \le
 \sqrt{\frac{M_{2,k}}{\delta N\rho_k}}.
 \label{eq:rho-gradient-concentration}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
Holding the contribution scale fixed, the radius grows as
$(N\rho_k)^{-1/2}$. Thus, low ESS weakens the reliability guarantee for the IS
gradient, consistent with the motivating example in the introduction. We now
combine this radius with
Lemma~\ref{lem:gradient-error-progress} to obtain an ESS-dependent improvement
guarantee.

\begin{theorem}[ESS-dependent improvement for the IS estimator]
\label{thm:permissive-reliability}
Under the conditions of Lemma~\ref{lem:rho-gradient-reliability}, suppose that
$J$ is $L_k$-smooth along the update segment for $L_k>0$. For any
$0<\delta\le1$, define
\begin{equation}
 \varepsilon_k(\delta):=
 \sqrt{\frac{M_{2,k}}{\delta N\rho_k}},
 \qquad
 \gamma_k:=\frac1{L_k}
 \left(1-\frac{\varepsilon_k(\delta)}{\|\widehat g_k\|_2}\right)_+,
 \label{eq:is-safe-step}
\end{equation}
with $\gamma_k=0$ when $\widehat g_k=0$. Then, with probability at least
$1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}
 J(\theta_k+\gamma_k\widehat g_k)-J(\theta_k)
 \ge
 \frac{1}{2L_k}
 \left(
 \|\widehat g_k\|_2-\varepsilon_k(\delta)
 \right)_+^2.
 \label{eq:permissive-reliability}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Theorem~\ref{thm:permissive-reliability} makes the role of ESS explicit. Its
lower bound is positive when
\[
 \|\widehat g_k\|_2
 >
 \sqrt{\frac{M_{2,k}}{\delta N\rho_k}}.
\]
Thus, the IS update remains certifiably useful while effective support is
sufficiently large relative to the observed gradient signal. Holding the other
terms fixed, both the admissible step and the certified improvement shrink as
ESS falls. This complements population policy-deviation guarantees, which
characterize the consequences of changing the policy, by controlling the
finite-rollout direction used to make that change. Having established when the
IS update is reliable, we next ask when an alternative gradient estimator
provides the stronger one-step certificate.

\subsection{Choosing an alternative gradient estimator}
\label{sec:clipping-recovery}

The preceding result connects the reliability of the IS estimator to policy
improvement. When this guarantee weakens, one may instead use a controlled
coefficient rule, such as truncated IS or PPO-style masking. We compare a
general rule $u$ with IS in two steps: first as a question of gradient
estimation, and then as a question of policy improvement at a common fixed
step. Write
$\widehat g_k(u):=\widehat g_N(u;\theta_k)$. For a rule $u$, adding and
subtracting the estimator mean gives the MSE decomposition
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 m_k(u)&:=\E\|\widehat g_k(u)-g_k\|_2^2\\
 &=\|\E[\widehat g_k(u)]-g_k\|_2^2
 +\E\|\widehat g_k(u)-\E[\widehat g_k(u)]\|_2^2.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:clipping-risk-decomposition}
\end{equation}
The two terms are squared bias and sampling variation. Since the IS estimator
is unbiased, a controlled rule has lower MSE exactly when its reduction in
sampling variation exceeds the squared bias introduced by changing the
coefficients. We call this the estimator-level bias--variance crossover. The
next theorem gives the expected-improvement certificate used to compare such
estimators at a common fixed step.

\begin{theorem}[Improvement certificate]
\label{thm:fixed-step-certificate}
Suppose that $J$ is $L_k$-smooth on every update segment generated by a
coefficient rule $u$ almost surely, for a constant $L_k>0$, and
that $\E\|\widehat g_k(u)\|_2^2<\infty$. For any step size $\eta_k>0$,
\begin{equation}
 \E\left[J(\theta_k+\eta_k\widehat g_k(u))-J(\theta_k)\right]
 \ge
 \eta_k g_k^\top\E[\widehat g_k(u)]
 -\frac{L_k\eta_k^2}{2}\E\|\widehat g_k(u)\|_2^2.
 \label{eq:full-token-rule-progress}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Denote the right-hand side by
\begin{equation}
 B_k(u;\eta_k)
 :=\eta_k g_k^\top\E[\widehat g_k(u)]
 -\frac{L_k\eta_k^2}{2}\E\|\widehat g_k(u)\|_2^2.
 \label{eq:full-certificate}
\end{equation}
The certificate has two parts. The first rewards alignment with the population
gradient; the second penalizes the expected squared update under smoothness.
Coefficient control improves the certificate only when the penalty it removes
outweighs the useful alignment it removes. The next corollary states this
crossover directly.

\begin{corollary}[IS-to-controlled-estimator crossover]
\label{cor:certificate-crossover}
Let $u_{\mathrm{IS}}$ denote the IS coefficient rule and let $u$ be any
alternative rule. Suppose the same deterministic $L_k$ is valid almost surely
for both families of update segments. With a common step size
$0<\eta_k\le1/L_k$, the alternative rule has the stronger certificate if and
only if
\begin{equation}
 B_k(u;\eta_k)>B_k(u_{\mathrm{IS}};\eta_k)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 m_k(u_{\mathrm{IS}})-m_k(u)
 >(1-L_k\eta_k)
 \left\{
 \E\|\widehat g_k(u_{\mathrm{IS}})\|_2^2
 -\E\|\widehat g_k(u)\|_2^2
 \right\}.
 \label{eq:is-controlled-certificate-crossover}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

When the alternative lowers the update second moment, the right-hand side of
Equation~\eqref{eq:is-controlled-certificate-crossover} is its discounted
reduction. Its MSE gain must exceed this amount. Since the IS estimator is
unbiased, the same condition has the equivalent alignment form
\begin{equation}
 \|g_k\|_2^2-g_k^\top\E[\widehat g_k(u)]
 <\frac{L_k\eta_k}{2}
 \left\{
 \E\|\widehat g_k(u_{\mathrm{IS}})\|_2^2
 -\E\|\widehat g_k(u)\|_2^2
 \right\}.
 \label{eq:is-controlled-alignment-crossover}
\end{equation}
The left-hand side is the population-gradient alignment lost by changing the
estimator; the right-hand side is the reduction in the quadratic smoothness
penalty.

\begin{remark}[Truncated importance sampling]
Upper truncation, $u_{\mathrm{T},t}=\min\{r_t,c\}$, caps each selected ratio
without deleting its tail contribution. It is therefore a cheap variance
safeguard with comparatively mild bias intervention. Its control is also
limited: it acts only on the chosen upper tail, so the reduction in the update
second moment can be modest under sequence-level mismatch.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[PPO-style masking]
The PPO rule in Equation~\eqref{eq:ppo-coefficient-rule} removes saturated
positive-advantage terms and low-ratio negative-advantage terms. It can reduce
the update second moment more aggressively than upper truncation, but it also
discards more population-gradient alignment. Equations
\eqref{eq:is-controlled-certificate-crossover} and
\eqref{eq:is-controlled-alignment-crossover} state when that stronger
intervention is worthwhile.
\end{remark}

\section{ESS-conditioned policy optimization}

Section~5 identifies ESS as an observable coordinate for effective support. We
now ask whether acting on this signal resolves the central optimization
tradeoff: retain a permissive update while the rollout remains informative,
then activate control as effective support contracts. We first test this rule
in Qwen3-30B-A3B math RLVR and then change the model scale and training
objective.

\subsection{Update rule and protocol}

Let $\widehat g_{\mathrm{perm}}$ denote the permissive update and
$\widehat g_{\mathrm{safe}}$ a prespecified safeguard. Given a threshold
$\tau$, the ESS-conditioned update is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_{\mathrm{gate}}
  =\begin{cases}
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{perm}},&\widehat\rho\ge \tau,\\
    \widehat g_{\mathrm{safe}},&\widehat\rho< \tau.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:llm-gate}
\end{equation}
ESS determines when the update changes; the selected safeguard determines how
the low-support update is controlled. We fix $\tau=0.1$ for all primary
comparisons and vary it only in the threshold ablation. This value was
prescribed for the language-model runs and is not derived from the Gaussian
thresholds in Section~\ref{sec:gaussian-example}.
GRPO, DPPO, and the unclipped construction use the raw $\widehat\rho$ in
Equation~\eqref{eq:sample-ess}. Clip-Higher instead uses the analogous shaped
ESS computed from post-cap sequence coefficients.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}
The main experiment trains Qwen3-30B-A3B for up to 200 policy updates on the
boxed mathematical-reasoning data of \citet{yu2025dapo} and evaluates
AIME-2024 using mean@16. The primary permissive branch is TIS with ratio cap 3;
an explicit ablation instead uses the unclipped update. At low ESS, we test
three prespecified safeguards: conventional GRPO clipping
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}, Clip-Higher \citep{yu2025dapo}, and DPPO
\citep{qi2026dppo}.
The static 30B recipes use their native token-mean aggregation, whereas the
TIS-3 conditional recipes use sum normalization. Panels (a)--(c) of the main
figure therefore compare complete recipes; the TIS-3 pair in panel (d) and the
unclipped comparison isolate ESS conditioning.

\section{Conclusion}

We studied clipping as a finite-sample estimator choice rather than a default
property of policy optimization. The theory separates gradient-estimation
error from the useful update retained after control: reducing MSE improves the
fixed-step certificate only when that gain compensates for the signal removed
by clipping. This distinction makes IS preferable while effective support is
broad and explains why controlled estimators become attractive as mismatch
grows. The Gaussian construction instantiates these comparisons and shows why mild
truncation can dominate more aggressive masking. The reported language-model
trajectories then show that sequence
ESS can reveal support contraction and time a prespecified safeguard without
serving as the oracle itself. In the RLVR and RLHF examples, the resulting
conditional constructions retain permissive learning in the higher-support
regime and protect training more strongly as support contracts. ESS therefore
provides the operational link between importance-weight reliability and the
decision of when to control an update.

\subsection{Gaussian bandit}
\label{app:gaussian}

This section gives the calculations used in
Section~\ref{sec:gaussian-example}. 

\paragraph{Setup}



We compare IS, TIS with cap $c=3$, and the PPO-style rule in
Equation~\eqref{eq:ppo-coefficient-rule}, using the exact current-policy value
baseline.

Because the score contribution is polynomial and each mask
boundary is linear in $z$, the means, MSEs, and update second moments are finite
sums of truncated Gaussian moments.

We choose a quadratic reward because it makes the smoothness certificate equal
to the true expected gain. Any separation between the two oracles is therefore
genuine rather than an artifact of a loose bound.

Consider the current policy and rollout policy
\begin{equation}
 P_\theta=\mathcal N(\theta,1),
 \qquad
 Q_\Delta=\mathcal N(\theta-\Delta,1),
 \qquad \Delta\ge0,
 \label{eq:gaussian-policy-pair}
\end{equation}
with reward $R(a)=-\tfrac12(a-a^\star)^2$. We hold the current policy fixed and
vary only the rollout gap $\Delta$. Writing $g:=a^\star-\theta>0$, the
objective is
\begin{equation}
 J(\theta)=-\frac12\{(\theta-a^\star)^2+1\},
 \qquad \nabla J(\theta)=g,
 \qquad L=1.
 \label{eq:gaussian-objective}
\end{equation}
Here $\theta$ is the fixed pre-update mean and $Q_\Delta$ remains frozen during
the hypothetical one-step update. We vary $\Delta$ only across common-state
comparisons.
For $z=a-\theta$, the likelihood ratio and normalized ESS reduce to
\begin{equation}
 W_\Delta(z)=\exp\{\Delta z+\Delta^2/2\},
 \qquad
 \rho(\Delta)=\{\E_{Q_\Delta}[W_\Delta^2]\}^{-1}
 =\exp(-\Delta^2).
 \label{eq:gaussian-ratio-ess}
\end{equation}
Normalized ESS is therefore a one-to-one coordinate for this branch.

Write $\mu_u:=\E[\widehat g_u]$, $s_u:=\E[\widehat g_u^2]$, and
$B_u(\eta):=\eta g\mu_u-\eta^2s_u/2$. For this quadratic objective, the
fixed-step certificate is exact:
\begin{equation}
 \E[J(\theta+\eta\widehat g_u)-J(\theta)]
 =\eta g\mu_u-\frac{\eta^2}{2}s_u
 =B_u(\eta).
 \label{eq:gaussian-exact-gain}
\end{equation}
We use $N=32$, the standard PPO radius $\epsilon=0.2$, TIS cap $c=3$, and
choose $g=2$ and $\eta=0.4$ so that the reliability and estimator-crossover
regimes are visible on one monotone ESS path.

\paragraph{Exact Closed Form Computations}
Let $z=a-\theta$. Under the current policy,
$z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and the score is $z$. The exact current-policy value
baseline gives
\begin{equation}
 A_g(z)=gz+\frac{1-z^2}{2},
 \qquad
 f_g(z):=zA_g(z)=gz^2+\frac{z-z^3}{2}.
 \label{eq:gaussian-advantage-contribution}
\end{equation}
Although $f_g$ is unbounded, all polynomial Gaussian moments used below are
finite, so the finite-moment conditions of
Lemma~\ref{lem:rho-gradient-reliability} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:permissive-reliability} hold. IS uses $W_\Delta f_g$ and TIS
uses $\min\{W_\Delta,c\}f_g$. The PPO-style candidate uses
$W_\Delta f_gM_{\epsilon,\Delta}$, where
\begin{equation}
 M_{\epsilon,\Delta}(z)
 :=\mathbf 1\{A_g(z)\ge0,\ W_\Delta(z)\le1+\epsilon\}
 +\mathbf 1\{A_g(z)<0,\ W_\Delta(z)\ge1-\epsilon\}.
 \label{eq:gaussian-ppo-mask}
\end{equation}
Let $M_{\mathrm{IS}}\equiv1$ and
$M_{\mathrm{P}}=M_{\epsilon,\Delta}$. Change of measure gives the exact
per-sample moments
\begin{align}
 \mu_u(\Delta)
 &=\E_{U\sim\mathcal N(0,1)}[f_g(U)M_u(U)],
 \nonumber\\
 h_u(\Delta)
 &=e^{\Delta^2}
 \E_{V\sim\mathcal N(\Delta,1)}[f_g(V)^2M_u(V)],
 \qquad u\in\{\mathrm{IS},\mathrm{P}\}.
 \label{eq:gaussian-exact-moments}
\end{align}
For TIS, define
$b_c(\Delta):=(\log c-\Delta^2/2)/\Delta$. Its exact moments are
\begin{align}
 \mu_{\mathrm{T}}(\Delta)
 &=\E_{U\sim\mathcal N(0,1)}
 [f_g(U)\mathbf 1\{U\le b_c\}]
 +c\E_{U\sim\mathcal N(-\Delta,1)}
 [f_g(U)\mathbf 1\{U>b_c\}],
 \nonumber\\
 h_{\mathrm{T}}(\Delta)
 &=e^{\Delta^2}\E_{V\sim\mathcal N(\Delta,1)}
 [f_g(V)^2\mathbf 1\{V\le b_c\}]
 +c^2\E_{V\sim\mathcal N(-\Delta,1)}
 [f_g(V)^2\mathbf 1\{V>b_c\}].
 \label{eq:gaussian-tis-moments}
\end{align}
In particular, $\mu_{\mathrm{IS}}=g$. For an iid estimator with batch size $N$,
\begin{equation}
 m_u=(\mu_u-g)^2+\frac{h_u-\mu_u^2}{N},
 \qquad
 s_u=\mu_u^2+\frac{h_u-\mu_u^2}{N}.
 \label{eq:gaussian-risk-second-moment}
\end{equation}

For $g=2$, the IS weighted-contribution scale in
Equation~\eqref{eq:weighted-contribution-scale} is
\begin{align}
 M_2(\Delta)
 &=\rho(\Delta)h_{\mathrm{IS}}(\Delta)
 \nonumber\\
 &=\frac{\Delta^6}{4}-2\Delta^5+\frac{29\Delta^4}{4}
 -18\Delta^3+\frac{65\Delta^2}{2}-24\Delta+\frac{29}{2}.
 \label{eq:gaussian-weighted-contribution-scale}
\end{align}
Hence the exact IS MSE is
$\{e^{\Delta^2}M_2(\Delta)-4\}/32$ and its
Lemma~\ref{lem:rho-gradient-reliability} envelope is
$e^{\Delta^2}M_2(\Delta)/32$. Panel (b) uses $10^5$ independent batches at
each of 81 policy pairs and $\delta=0.1$ to evaluate the realized coverage of
Lemma~\ref{lem:rho-gradient-reliability} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:permissive-reliability}. Both $M_2(\Delta)$ and the adaptive
step are evaluated from population quantities in this controlled theory
validation; they are not proposed as an empirical gate.

The advantage changes sign at $g\pm\sqrt{g^2+1}$, and the PPO ratio boundaries
are $(\log(1\pm\epsilon)-\Delta^2/2)/\Delta$. Equations
\eqref{eq:gaussian-exact-moments} and \eqref{eq:gaussian-tis-moments} are
therefore finite sums of truncated normal polynomial moments. With
$I_j(a,b):=\int_a^bz^j\phi(z)\,dz$, integration by parts gives
\begin{equation}
 I_j(a,b)
 =a^{j-1}\phi(a)-b^{j-1}\phi(b)+(j-1)I_{j-2}(a,b),
 \qquad j\ge2,
 \label{eq:truncated-normal-recursion}
\end{equation}
starting from $I_0=\Phi(b)-\Phi(a)$ and
$I_1=\phi(a)-\phi(b)$. Boundary terms vanish at infinity. Substituting these
moments into Equation~\eqref{eq:gaussian-risk-second-moment} and the exact gain
in Equation~\eqref{eq:gaussian-exact-gain} gives the reported boundaries.

\paragraph{Results}
We evaluate
$\sqrt{-\log(0.9)}\le\Delta\le\sqrt{-\log(0.005)}$. For each
$u\in\{\mathrm{T},\mathrm{P}\}$, substitution into
Corollary~\ref{cor:certificate-crossover} gives
\begin{equation}
 C_u(\rho)
 =\{m_{\mathrm{IS}}-m_u\}
 -(1-\eta)\{s_{\mathrm{IS}}-s_u\}
 =\frac{2}{\eta}\{B_u-B_{\mathrm{IS}}\}.
 \label{eq:gaussian-crossover-calculation}
\end{equation}
Numerical evaluation of the closed-form moments gives
\begin{equation}
 \rho_{\mathrm{IS,TIS3}}=0.105,
 \qquad
 \rho_{\mathrm{IS,PPO}}=0.0392.
 \label{eq:gaussian-thresholds}
\end{equation}
At the TIS-3 root, the MSE reduction and discounted second-moment reduction
both equal $3.94$; at the PPO root, both equal $11.0$. TIS-3 has lower
MSE than both IS and PPO throughout the displayed branch, yet IS has the
larger certificate for $\rho>0.105$. Below that point TIS-3 forms the upper
certificate envelope. PPO crosses IS at $0.0392$ only pairwise: it remains
below TIS-3 throughout the branch. Equation~\eqref{eq:gaussian-exact-gain}
makes the same ordering exact for expected one-step policy improvement.

\section{Additional coefficient-rule details}
\label{app:coefficient-rules}

The main text treats coefficient control generically because different rules
alter different parts of the gradient. A factorwise detached upper truncation
uses $u_{\mathrm{T},t}(\mathbf r,A):=\min\{r_t,c\}$. Its contribution obeys
\begin{equation}
 \|\psi_k(u_{\mathrm{T}};Z)\|_2
 \le c|A(Z)|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\ell_{\theta_k,t}(Z)\|_2,
 \label{eq:token-truncation-envelope}
\end{equation}
so it has a direct second-moment envelope whenever the right-hand side has a
finite second moment.

PPO masking is different. For $A\ge0$ and $c=1+\epsilon$,
\begin{equation}
 0\le r-\min\{r,c\}=(r-c)_+
 \le r\mathbf 1\{r>c\}=r-rM_\epsilon(r,A),
 \label{eq:truncation-mask-distortion}
\end{equation}
where
$M_\epsilon(r,A)=\mathbf 1\{A\ge0,\ r\le1+\epsilon\}
+\mathbf 1\{A<0,\ r\ge1-\epsilon\}$. Truncation retains a bounded positive
coefficient above the cap, whereas PPO removes that contribution. On the
negative-advantage lower branch, PPO masks small ratios whereas upper
truncation does not. A complete-sample mask is another distinct rule: it sets
$u_t(\mathbf r,A)=W_kM_\epsilon(W_k,A)$ for every factor. We state which rule
is used in each experiment rather than treating these implementations as
interchangeable.

\section{Proofs for the theoretical results}
\label{app:proofs}

At update $k$, all expectations in this appendix are taken after conditioning on the preceding updates. The current policy is therefore fixed and the unused sampling units are independent draws from $Q$. We suppress this conditioning throughout.

\subsection{Policy-gradient identity and mean-square bound}

By definition, $f_k=(R-b(X))\nabla_\theta\log P_{\theta_k}(Y\mid X)$ and $W_k=dP_{\theta_k}/dQ$. Change of measure gives
\begin{align}
 \E_Q[W_kf_k]
 &=\E_{P_{\theta_k}}[(R-b(X))\nabla_\theta\log P_{\theta_k}(Y\mid X)]
 \nonumber\\
 &=\E_{P_{\theta_k}}[R\nabla_\theta\log P_{\theta_k}(Y\mid X)]
 \nonumber\\
 &\quad-
 \E_{P_{\theta_k}}[b(X)\nabla_\theta\log P_{\theta_k}(Y\mid X)].
 \label{eq:proof-baseline-split}
\end{align}
For the baseline term, condition further on $X$. Under the standard regularity
condition that permits differentiation under the conditional integral,
\begin{align}
 &\E_{P_{\theta_k}}[b(X)\nabla_\theta\log P_{\theta_k}(Y\mid X)\mid X]
 \nonumber\\
 &\qquad=b(X)\int P_{\theta_k}(y\mid X)
 \nabla_\theta\log P_{\theta_k}(y\mid X)\,d\lambda(y)
 \nonumber\\
 &\qquad=b(X)\nabla_\theta
 \int P_{\theta_k}(y\mid X)\,d\lambda(y)
 \nonumber\\
 &\qquad=b(X)\nabla_\theta1
 =0.
 \label{eq:proof-baseline-zero}
\end{align}
The remaining term is the score-function identity. Hence
\begin{equation}
 \E_Q[W_kf_k]=\nabla J(\theta_k)=g_k.
 \label{eq:conditional-gradient-identity}
\end{equation}
Averaging $N$ independent copies gives $\E[\widehat g_k]=g_k$.

For the mean-square calculation, define $\xi_{k,i}:=W_{k,i}f_{k,i}-g_k$. Equation~\eqref{eq:conditional-gradient-identity} implies $\E[\xi_{k,i}]=0$. Since $\widehat g_k-g_k=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N\xi_{k,i}$,
\begin{align}
 \E\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2
 &=\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i=1}^N\E\|\xi_{k,i}\|_2^2
 +\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i\ne j}\E[\xi_{k,i}^\top\xi_{k,j}].
 \label{eq:mse-expand-sums}
\end{align}
For $i\ne j$, independence and zero means give
\begin{equation}
 \E[\xi_{k,i}^\top\xi_{k,j}]
 =\E[\xi_{k,i}]^\top\E[\xi_{k,j}]
 =0.
 \label{eq:cross-terms-zero}
\end{equation}
The diagonal terms are identical, so
\begin{equation}
 \E\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2
 =\frac1N\E_Q\|W_kf_k-g_k\|_2^2.
 \label{eq:mse-single-unit}
\end{equation}
Expanding the remaining squared norm yields
\begin{align}
 \E_Q\|W_kf_k-g_k\|_2^2
 &=\E_Q[W_k^2\|f_k\|_2^2]
 -2g_k^\top\E_Q[W_kf_k]
 +\|g_k\|_2^2
 \nonumber\\
 &=\E_Q[W_k^2\|f_k\|_2^2]-\|g_k\|_2^2,
 \label{eq:mse-expand-unit}
\end{align}
where the last equality uses Equation~\eqref{eq:conditional-gradient-identity}. Combining Equations~\eqref{eq:mse-single-unit} and \eqref{eq:mse-expand-unit} gives
\begin{equation}
 \E\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2
 =\frac1N\left\{\E_Q[W_k^2\|f_k\|_2^2]-\|g_k\|_2^2\right\}.
 \label{eq:rho-gradient-mse-proof}
\end{equation}
Finally, Equation~\eqref{eq:weighted-contribution-scale} implies
\begin{align}
 \E\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2
 &\le\frac1N\E_Q[W_k^2\|f_k\|_2^2]
 \nonumber\\
 &=\frac{M_{2,k}}{N\rho_k},
 \label{eq:mse-rho-bound-proof}
\end{align}
which proves Equation~\eqref{eq:rho-gradient-mse-bound}.

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:rho-gradient-reliability}}

If $M_{2,k}=0$, Equation~\eqref{eq:rho-gradient-mse-bound} makes the claim
immediate. Otherwise, apply Markov's inequality to the nonnegative random
variable $\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2$:
\begin{align}
 &\Pr\left(
 \|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2
 >\sqrt{\frac{M_{2,k}}{\delta N\rho_k}}
 \right)
 \nonumber\\
 &\quad=
 \Pr\left(
 \|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2
 >\frac{M_{2,k}}{\delta N\rho_k}
 \right)
 \nonumber\\
 &\quad\le
 \frac{\E\|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2^2}{M_{2,k}/(\delta N\rho_k)}
 \nonumber\\
 &\quad\le\delta,
 \label{eq:markov-rho-proof}
\end{align}
where the last line uses Equation~\eqref{eq:rho-gradient-mse-bound}. Taking complements proves Equation~\eqref{eq:rho-gradient-concentration}.

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:gradient-error-progress}}

Let $\|\widehat g-g\|_2\le\varepsilon$. Smoothness gives, for every $\gamma\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 J(\theta+\gamma\widehat g)-J(\theta)
 \ge
 \gamma g^\top\widehat g
 -\frac{L\gamma^2}{2}\|\widehat g\|_2^2.
 \label{eq:smoothness-start}
\end{equation}
The inner product satisfies
\begin{align}
 g^\top\widehat g
 &=\|\widehat g\|_2^2+(g-\widehat g)^\top\widehat g
 \nonumber\\
 &\ge\|\widehat g\|_2^2-\|g-\widehat g\|_2\|\widehat g\|_2
 \nonumber\\
 &\ge\|\widehat g\|_2(\|\widehat g\|_2-\varepsilon).
 \label{eq:reliable-inner-product}
\end{align}
Substituting Equation~\eqref{eq:reliable-inner-product} into Equation~\eqref{eq:smoothness-start} gives
\begin{align}
 J(\theta+\gamma\widehat g)-J(\theta)
 &\ge
 \gamma\|\widehat g\|_2(\|\widehat g\|_2-\varepsilon)
 -\frac{L\gamma^2}{2}\|\widehat g\|_2^2.
 \label{eq:reliable-quadratic}
\end{align}
For $\widehat g\ne0$, the derivative of the right-hand side is
\begin{equation}
 \|\widehat g\|_2(\|\widehat g\|_2-\varepsilon)
 -L\gamma\|\widehat g\|_2^2.
 \label{eq:reliable-quadratic-derivative}
\end{equation}
Its nonnegative maximizer is
$\gamma=L^{-1}(1-\varepsilon/\|\widehat g\|_2)_+$. When $\widehat g=0$, both
the chosen step and the claimed lower bound are zero. Substituting the maximizer
into Equation~\eqref{eq:reliable-quadratic} proves
Equation~\eqref{eq:gradient-error-progress}.

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:permissive-reliability}}

Lemma~\ref{lem:rho-gradient-reliability} shows that the event
\begin{equation}
 \|\widehat g_k-g_k\|_2
 \le
 \sqrt{\frac{M_{2,k}}{\delta N\rho_k}}
 \label{eq:theorem-reliability-event}
\end{equation}
has probability at least $1-\delta$. On this event, apply
Lemma~\ref{lem:gradient-error-progress} with $\theta=\theta_k$,
$\widehat g=\widehat g_k$,
$\varepsilon=\sqrt{M_{2,k}/(\delta N\rho_k)}$, and $L=L_k$. The resulting
inequality is exactly Equation~\eqref{eq:permissive-reliability}.

\subsection{Risk and certificate comparisons for coefficient rules}

For a coefficient rule $u$, add and subtract the estimator mean:
\begin{equation}
 \widehat g_k(u)-g_k
 =\left\{\widehat g_k(u)-\E[\widehat g_k(u)]\right\}
 +\left\{\E[\widehat g_k(u)]-g_k\right\}.
 \label{eq:modified-bias-split}
\end{equation}
Squaring and taking expectation gives
\begin{align}
 m_k(u)
 &=\E\left\|\widehat g_k(u)-\E[\widehat g_k(u)]\right\|_2^2
 +\|\E[\widehat g_k(u)]-g_k\|_2^2
 \nonumber\\
 &\quad+2\{\E[\widehat g_k(u)]-g_k\}^\top
 \E\left[\widehat g_k(u)-\E[\widehat g_k(u)]\right]
 \nonumber\\
 &=\E\left\|\widehat g_k(u)-\E[\widehat g_k(u)]\right\|_2^2
 +\|\E[\widehat g_k(u)]-g_k\|_2^2,
 \label{eq:modified-risk-proof}
\end{align}
because the centered term has mean zero. This proves
Equation~\eqref{eq:clipping-risk-decomposition}. For an iid sample mean, the
first term in Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof} is the per-sample
variation divided by $N$.

It remains to connect total risk to the expected policy-improvement certificate. For any random estimate $\widehat g$ of $g_k$, smoothness gives
\begin{equation}
 J(\theta_k+\eta_k\widehat g)-J(\theta_k)
 \ge
 \eta_k g_k^\top\widehat g
 -\frac{L_k\eta_k^2}{2}\|\widehat g\|_2^2.
 \label{eq:fixed-step-smoothness}
\end{equation}
Taking expectation in Equation~\eqref{eq:fixed-step-smoothness}, with
$\E\|\widehat g\|_2^2<\infty$, gives
\begin{equation}
 \E[J(\theta_k+\eta_k\widehat g)-J(\theta_k)]
 \ge
 \eta_k g_k^\top\E[\widehat g]
 -\frac{L_k\eta_k^2}{2}\E\|\widehat g\|_2^2.
 \label{eq:fixed-step-expected}
\end{equation}
Setting $\widehat g=\widehat g_k(u)$ proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:fixed-step-certificate}. The polarization identity, followed
by expectation, gives
\begin{equation}
 2g_k^\top\E[\widehat g]
 =\|g_k\|_2^2+\E\|\widehat g\|_2^2
 -\E\|\widehat g-g_k\|_2^2.
 \label{eq:polarization}
\end{equation}
Substituting Equation~\eqref{eq:polarization} into the certificate and
subtracting the IS certificate gives
\begin{align}
 B_k(u;\eta_k)-B_k(u_{\mathrm{IS}};\eta_k)
 &=\frac{\eta_k}{2}
 \bigl[m_k(u_{\mathrm{IS}})-m_k(u)\bigr]
 \nonumber\\
 &\quad-\frac{\eta_k}{2}(1-L_k\eta_k)
 \left\{
 \E\|\widehat g_k(u_{\mathrm{IS}})\|_2^2
 -\E\|\widehat g_k(u)\|_2^2
 \right\}.
 \label{eq:certificate-crossover-proof}
\end{align}
Since $\eta_k>0$, the right-hand side is positive exactly under
Equation~\eqref{eq:is-controlled-certificate-crossover}, proving
Corollary~\ref{cor:certificate-crossover}. Since the IS estimator is unbiased,
subtracting the certificate definitions instead gives
\begin{align}
 B_k(u;\eta_k)-B_k(u_{\mathrm{IS}};\eta_k)
 &=-\eta_k\left\{
 \|g_k\|_2^2-g_k^\top\E[\widehat g_k(u)]
 \right\}
 \nonumber\\
 &\quad+\frac{L_k\eta_k^2}{2}
 \left\{
 \E\|\widehat g_k(u_{\mathrm{IS}})\|_2^2
 -\E\|\widehat g_k(u)\|_2^2
 \right\}.
 \label{eq:certificate-alignment-proof}
\end{align}
Its positivity is equivalent to
Equation~\eqref{eq:is-controlled-alignment-crossover}.

\subsection{Intact prompt groups}

Suppose $M$ prompts are independent and each prompt has $G$ conditionally independent responses. Keep each prompt group intact and define its contribution by $\Xi_{k,i}:=G^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^G W_{k,ij}f_{k,ij}$. A leave-one-out baseline is admissible because it excludes the focal response. Conditional on the prompt and the remaining responses, the focal score still has mean zero. Hence $\E[\Xi_{k,i}]=g_k$.

Jensen's inequality gives
\begin{align}
 \|\Xi_{k,i}\|_2^2
 &=\left\|\frac1G\sum_{j=1}^G W_{k,ij}f_{k,ij}\right\|_2^2
 \nonumber\\
 &\le\frac1G\sum_{j=1}^G W_{k,ij}^2\|f_{k,ij}\|_2^2.
 \label{eq:group-jensen}
\end{align}
Repeating the sample-mean calculation across the $M$ independent prompt groups gives
\begin{align}
 \E\left\|\frac1M\sum_{i=1}^M\Xi_{k,i}-g_k\right\|_2^2
 &\le\frac1M\E\|\Xi_{k,1}\|_2^2
 \nonumber\\
 &\le\frac{\|f_k\|_\infty^2}{M\rho_k}.
 \label{eq:group-rho-bound}
\end{align}
Markov's inequality then gives the bounded-contribution specialization of
Lemma~\ref{lem:rho-gradient-reliability} with the number of independent prompts
$M$ in place of the number of responses $N$.

\subsection{Bounded-contribution specialization}

If $\|f_k\|_\infty<\infty$, then
$M_{2,k}\le\|f_k\|_\infty^2$. Lemma~\ref{lem:rho-gradient-reliability}
therefore recovers the conventional radius
$\|f_k\|_\infty/\sqrt{\delta N\rho_k}$ as a direct special case.
