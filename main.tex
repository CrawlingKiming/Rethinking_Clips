\title{\bf Rethinking Clipping in RLVR:\\
Sequence ESS Governs the Reliability of Unclipped Updates}
\author{Anonymous authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards often reuses generated responses
for several updates.  Clipping is commonly treated as necessary because reuse
is assumed to make an unclipped update unstable.  We show that reuse alone does
not determine reliability.  What matters is whether the reused batch still
covers the current policy, and sequence effective sample size provides the
missing bridge from policy drift to update error.  This view also separates
diagnosis from intervention: clipping is useful only when the stability it gains
is worth the distortion it introduces.  Exact results in a controlled simulation
support this account.  Moderate upper caps reduce update error as coverage
deteriorates, although they do not reliably improve learning over several reused
updates.  These findings motivate an adaptive clipping rule while leaving its
validation in language-model training as open work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

RLVR improves data efficiency by taking several policy updates on each batch of
generated responses.  As the policy changes, however, the stored responses
become less representative of its current behavior.  The central practical
question is therefore not whether the batch has been reused, but whether it
still supports a reliable update.

Ratio clipping is the standard answer in PPO-style training
\citep{schulman2017ppo}.  Its usual justification treats an unclipped update as
increasingly unstable as the data become stale.  Recent work has questioned the
ratio itself as a measure of harmful policy change \citep{qi2026dppo}, while
adaptive methods track how concentrated reused data have become
\citep{zheng2025m2po,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}.  These methods expose a
logically prior question.  Before choosing how to regulate an update, one must
determine whether the unclipped update has actually become unreliable.

Batch age cannot answer that question.  Two batches reused for the same number
of updates can differ sharply in how well they represent the current policy.
One may remain broadly informative, while the other may be dominated by only a
few responses.  Sequence effective sample size (ESS) captures this difference
and provides a more meaningful notion of staleness than update count alone.

We show that ESS is the missing theoretical bridge.  For the exact
update studied here, it connects policy coverage directly to estimation error.
The result also identifies a necessary qualification: coverage alone is not
enough because a few responses may still exert unusually large influence.  An
old batch can therefore remain reliable, while a much newer batch can already
be poor, depending on these two factors.

This diagnosis changes the role of clipping.  Clipping is not what defines a
stable update.  It limits the influence of extreme responses, but also changes
the update in a systematic way.  We make this tradeoff explicit and show why a
moderately high upper cap can remove harmful extremes while otherwise leaving
the update largely unchanged.  Verifier rewards make this regime especially
relevant because their signal is naturally bounded.

We test the resulting account in a controlled contextual-bandit simulation where
all relevant quantities can be computed exactly.  The experiment confirms the
predicted link between ESS and update error and shows that upper caps of 3 and 5
become beneficial as coverage deteriorates.  It also reveals the boundary of
this evidence: lower estimation error does not reliably translate into better
reward after several updates on the same batch.  The controlled study therefore
validates the mechanism without claiming that it settles language-model
training.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item We identify sequence ESS as the bridge between policy coverage and the
reliability of an unclipped update.
\item We characterize when clipping is worth the distortion it introduces and
explain why moderate upper caps can reduce estimation error.
\item We validate the mechanism in a controlled contextual bandit and
state precisely what remains to be tested in language models.
\end{enumerate}

The next section develops the first link in this argument by separating the
correctness of batch reuse from the reliability of its finite-sample estimate.

\section{From batch reuse to sequence ESS}
\label{sec:ess}

Let $X\sim\nu$ be a prompt, $Y$ a complete response generated by $Q$, and
$R=R(X,Y)$ a terminal verifier reward.  The learner $P_\theta$ is absolutely
continuous with respect to $Q$, and both policies share the prompt distribution.
The sequence likelihood ratio is
\begin{equation}
  W_\theta(X,Y)
  :=\frac{P_\theta(Y\mid X)}{Q(Y\mid X)},
  \qquad
  \E_Q[W_\theta\mid X]=1.
  \label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}

Let $b(X)$ be a prompt-only baseline, detached from differentiation, and write
$A=R-b(X)$.  The next proposition shows that batch reuse is population-correct
before finite-sample error is considered.

\begin{proposition}[Exact centered improvement]
\label{prop:improvement}
If $P_\theta\ll Q$ and the displayed expectations are finite, then
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta)-J(Q)=\E_Q[(W_\theta-1)A],
  \qquad
  J(\theta):=\E_{P_\theta}[R],
  \quad
  J(Q):=\E_Q[R].
  \label{eq:improvement}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Expand the centered advantage and retain one transformation per line:
\begin{align}
  \E_Q[(W_\theta-1)A]
  &=\E_Q[(W_\theta-1)R]
    -\E_Q[(W_\theta-1)b(X)]
    \label{eq:improvement-proof-1}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta R]-\E_Q[R]
    -\E_X\!\left[
      b(X)\{\E_Q[W_\theta\mid X]-1\}
    \right]
    \label{eq:improvement-proof-2}\\
  &=\E_{P_\theta}[R]-\E_Q[R]
    \label{eq:improvement-proof-3}\\
  &=J(\theta)-J(Q).
    \label{eq:improvement-proof-4}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:improvement-proof-1} substitutes $A=R-b(X)$ and distributes
the product.  Equation~\eqref{eq:improvement-proof-2} expands the first term and
uses the tower property on the baseline term because $b(X)$ depends only on the
prompt.  Equation~\eqref{eq:improvement-proof-3} uses
$\E_Q[W_\theta\mid X]=1$ and the change-of-measure identity
$\E_Q[W_\theta R]=\E_{P_\theta}[R]$.  The last line substitutes the definitions
of $J(\theta)$ and $J(Q)$.
\end{proof}

The proposition shows that reuse itself introduces no population approximation.
The remaining difficulty must therefore arise from estimating this identity
with finitely many responses.

That finite-sample difficulty is encoded by the second moment of $W_\theta$.
Assume $\E_Q[W_\theta^2]<\infty$.  Define the order-two R\'enyi divergence and
normalized sequence ESS by
\begin{align}
  D_2(P_\theta\|Q)
  &:=\log\E_Q[W_\theta^2],
  \label{eq:renyi-two}\\
  \rho_\theta
  &:=\frac{1}{\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
    \label{eq:population-ess-definition}\\
  &=\exp\{-D_2(P_\theta\|Q)\}.
    \label{eq:population-ess}
\end{align}
The variance of the likelihood ratio then satisfies
\begin{align}
  \E_Q[(W_\theta-1)^2]&=\rho_\theta^{-1}-1.
  \label{eq:weight-variance}
\end{align}
Indeed,
\begin{align}
  \E_Q[(W_\theta-1)^2]
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta^2]-2\E_Q[W_\theta]+1
    \label{eq:weight-variance-proof-1}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta^2]-1
    \label{eq:weight-variance-proof-2}\\
  &=\rho_\theta^{-1}-1.
    \label{eq:weight-variance-proof-3}
\end{align}
The second line uses $\E_QW_\theta=1$, and the third uses the definition of
$\rho_\theta$.

Hence $N\rho_\theta$ is the population effective sequence count.  This is the
same second-moment coverage principle used in importance-sampling policy
optimization \citep{metelli2018pois,metelli2020is}.  The next section converts
this scalar coverage quantity into the error of the vector supplied to the
optimizer.

\section{ESS governs the ratio-driven error of the unclipped gradient}
\label{sec:raw}

Define the full-response score and the unweighted sequence contribution
\begin{equation}
  S_\theta:=\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(Y\mid X),
  \qquad
  H_\theta:=A S_\theta.
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
Assume differentiation and expectation can be interchanged and
$\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]<\infty$.  Differentiating
\eqref{eq:improvement} yields
\begin{equation}
  g(\theta):=\nabla J(\theta)=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta].
  \label{eq:true-gradient}
\end{equation}
For $N$ independent responses from $Q$, the raw estimator is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_W(\theta):=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N
  W_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}.
  \label{eq:raw-estimator}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Exact finite-sample error]
\label{thm:raw-mse}
For fixed $\theta$, $\widehat g_W$ is unbiased and
\begin{equation}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  =\frac1N\left\{
    \E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:raw-mse}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $Z_i=W_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}$ and let $g=g(\theta)$.  Unbiasedness follows
line by line from identical sampling:
\begin{align}
  \E[\widehat g_W]
  &=\E\left[\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N Z_i\right]
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-1}\\
  &=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\E[Z_i]
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-2}\\
  &=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta]
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-3}\\
  &=g.
    \label{eq:unbiased-proof-4}
\end{align}
For the MSE, center each contribution before expanding the squared norm:
\begin{align}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  &=\E\left\|
    \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N(Z_i-g)
    \right\|_2^2
    \label{eq:raw-proof-1}\\
  &=\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i=1}^N\E\|Z_i-g\|_2^2
    +\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i\ne j}
      \E[(Z_i-g)^\top(Z_j-g)]
    \label{eq:raw-proof-2}\\
  &=\frac1N\E\|Z_1-g\|_2^2
    \label{eq:raw-proof-3}\\
  &=\frac1N\left\{
    \E\|Z_1\|_2^2-2g^\top\E[Z_1]+\|g\|_2^2
    \right\}
    \label{eq:raw-proof-4}\\
  &=\frac1N\left\{
    \E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]-\|g\|_2^2
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:raw-proof-5}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:unbiased-proof-1} substitutes the estimator definition.
Equation~\eqref{eq:unbiased-proof-2} uses linearity of expectation, and
\eqref{eq:unbiased-proof-3} uses identical sampling.  The last line substitutes
\eqref{eq:true-gradient}.  For the MSE, \eqref{eq:raw-proof-1} uses
$\widehat g_W-g=N^{-1}\sum_i(Z_i-g)$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:raw-proof-2} expands
the squared norm.  Independence and $\E[Z_i-g]=0$ make every cross term vanish;
identical sampling then gives \eqref{eq:raw-proof-3}.  Equation
\eqref{eq:raw-proof-4} expands the remaining squared norm.  Finally,
\eqref{eq:raw-proof-5} substitutes $\E[Z_1]=g$ and
$\|Z_1\|_2^2=W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2$.
\end{proof}

To expose the role of ESS, define
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}
  :=\frac{\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]}
          {\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}.
  \label{eq:gradient-scale}
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq:population-ess} into
\eqref{eq:raw-mse} gives
\begin{equation}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  =\frac1N\left(
    \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{\rho_\theta}-\|g(\theta)\|_2^2
  \right)
  \le \frac{G_{2,\theta}}{N\rho_\theta}.
  \label{eq:ess-bridge}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:ess-bridge} is the main theoretical bridge.  Sequence ESS
governs the error contributed by ratio concentration, while $G_{2,\theta}$
records whether large ratios coincide with large gradient contributions.  When
$G_{2,\theta}$ is controlled and $N\rho_\theta$ is large, the upper bound remains
small.  Policy drift or update count alone therefore does not imply estimator
failure.  Falling ESS instead identifies the regime in which a
variance-reducing estimator may become worth considering.

The remaining qualifier is the weighted gradient scale.  Advantage bounds are
useful here, but they cannot control $G_{2,\theta}$ without a corresponding
condition on the score.

\begin{proposition}[A sufficient bound on $G_{2,\theta}$]
\label{prop:g2-bound}
If $|A|\le B_A$ and $\|S_\theta\|_2\le B_S$ almost surely under $Q$, then
\begin{equation}
  G_{2,\theta}\le B_A^2B_S^2.
  \label{eq:g2-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $H_\theta=A S_\theta$, substitute its squared norm into
\eqref{eq:gradient-scale} and apply one bound per line:
\begin{align}
  G_{2,\theta}
  &=\frac{\E_Q[W_\theta^2 A^2\|S_\theta\|_2^2]}
          {\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
    \label{eq:g2-proof-1}\\
  &\le B_A^2
    \frac{\E_Q[W_\theta^2\|S_\theta\|_2^2]}
         {\E_Q[W_\theta^2]}
    \label{eq:g2-proof-2}\\
  &\le B_A^2B_S^2.
    \label{eq:g2-proof-3}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:g2-proof-2} uses $A^2\le B_A^2$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:g2-proof-3} uses $\|S_\theta\|_2^2\le B_S^2$ under the same
nonnegative weight $W_\theta^2$, then cancels $\E_Q[W_\theta^2]$.
\end{proof}

The advantage side is favorable in common RLVR constructions.  If the verifier
reward is binary and a prompt-only baseline satisfies $b(X)\in[0,1]$, then
$|A|=|R-b(X)|\le1$.  Outcome-level GRPO instead standardizes rewards within a
group of $K$ responses \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}.  Its standardized
advantages also have a deterministic finite-group bound whenever their sample
variance is nonzero.

\begin{proposition}[Magnitude of a standardized group advantage]
\label{prop:grpo-bound}
Let $d_i=r_i-\bar r$, let $v=K^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^K d_j^2>0$, and let
$A_i=d_i/\sqrt v$.  Then $|A_i|\le\sqrt{K-1}$ for every $i$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The centered deviations satisfy $d_i=-\sum_{j\ne i}d_j$.  Therefore,
\begin{align}
  d_i^2
  &=\left(\sum_{j\ne i}d_j\right)^2
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-1}\\
  &\le (K-1)\sum_{j\ne i}d_j^2
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-2}\\
  &=(K-1)\left(\sum_{j=1}^K d_j^2-d_i^2\right).
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-3}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:grpo-proof-1} uses the zero-sum identity for centered
deviations.  Equation~\eqref{eq:grpo-proof-2} is Cauchy--Schwarz, and
\eqref{eq:grpo-proof-3} restores the omitted term.  Rearranging gives
\begin{align}
  Kd_i^2
  &\le (K-1)\sum_{j=1}^K d_j^2
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-4}\\
  \frac{d_i^2}{v}
  &\le K-1,
    \label{eq:grpo-proof-5}
\end{align}
where the second line uses $Kv=\sum_jd_j^2$.  Taking square roots proves the
claim.  Adding a positive stabilizer to the denominator can only decrease the
magnitude.
\end{proof}

These bounds isolate what advantage normalization can and cannot establish.
They control the advantage factor, while a bound or empirical estimate of the
score factor remains necessary.  Moreover, group normalization couples the $K$
responses, so \Cref{thm:raw-mse} does not apply response by response without a
group-level sampling analysis.  The binary-reward, prompt-baseline case obeys
the theorem directly; the GRPO bound supplies scale information rather than an
i.i.d. justification.

\begin{remark}[Pointwise scope]
The expectation is over a fresh batch at fixed $\theta$.  During same-batch
optimization, $\theta$ depends on the reused data.  Extending
\eqref{eq:ess-bridge} uniformly over that adaptive path requires additional
stability or complexity control.
\end{remark}

The raw risk is now explicit, so the remaining question is comparative.  The
next section asks whether a bounded coefficient reduces that risk enough to
justify the bias it introduces.

\section{When clipping becomes preferable}
\label{sec:clipping}

Let $U_\theta=U(W_\theta)$ be a bounded, detached sequence coefficient.  The
modified estimator
\begin{equation}
  \widehat g_U:=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N U_{\theta,i}H_{\theta,i}
  \label{eq:clipped-estimator}
\end{equation}
has gradient bias and one-sample covariances
\begin{equation}
  b_U:=\E_Q[(U_\theta-W_\theta)H_\theta],
  \quad
  \Sigma_W:=\Cov_Q(W_\theta H_\theta),
  \quad
  \Sigma_U:=\Cov_Q(U_\theta H_\theta).
  \label{eq:bias-covariance}
\end{equation}
Detachment matters: differentiating a scalar expression containing
$U(W_\theta)$ generally produces additional chain-rule terms.

\begin{theorem}[Exact clipping crossover]
\label{thm:crossover}
For fixed $\theta$ and finite second moments,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  =\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_U)
  =\|b_U\|_2^2+\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U).
  \label{eq:two-risks}
\end{equation}
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_U)
  <\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
  \iff
  \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U)
  >N\|b_U\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $\mu_U:=\E[\widehat g_U]$.  Its displacement from the target is
\begin{align}
  \mu_U-g
  &=\E_Q[U_\theta H_\theta]-\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta]
    \label{eq:modified-bias-proof-1}\\
  &=\E_Q[(U_\theta-W_\theta)H_\theta]
    \label{eq:modified-bias-proof-2}\\
  &=b_U.
    \label{eq:modified-bias-proof-3}
\end{align}
Centering the modified estimator at $\mu_U$ now yields
\begin{align}
  \E\|\widehat g_U-g\|_2^2
  &=\E\|\widehat g_U-\mu_U+b_U\|_2^2
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-1}\\
  &=\E\|\widehat g_U-\mu_U\|_2^2
    +2b_U^\top\E[\widehat g_U-\mu_U]
    +\|b_U\|_2^2
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-2}\\
  &=\E\|\widehat g_U-\mu_U\|_2^2+\|b_U\|_2^2
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-3}\\
  &=\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U)+\|b_U\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:modified-risk-proof-4}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:modified-bias-proof-1}--\eqref{eq:modified-bias-proof-3}
substitute $g=\E_Q[W_\theta H_\theta]$, use linearity, and invoke the definition
of $b_U$, respectively.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-1} adds and
subtracts $\mu_U$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-2} expands the
squared norm.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-3} uses
$\E[\widehat g_U-\mu_U]=0$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modified-risk-proof-4} uses
independence of the $N$ sampled responses, exactly as in
\eqref{eq:raw-proof-2}--\eqref{eq:raw-proof-3}.  The raw estimator is unbiased by
\Cref{thm:raw-mse}, so the same covariance calculation gives
\begin{equation}
  \E\|\widehat g_W-g\|_2^2
  =\frac1N\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W).
  \label{eq:raw-risk-crossover-proof}
\end{equation}
Subtracting the two risks gives
\begin{align}
  &\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_U)
    -\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_W)
    \label{eq:crossover-proof-1}\\
  &\quad=\|b_U\|_2^2
    -\frac1N\left\{
      \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_W)-\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_U)
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:crossover-proof-2}
\end{align}
The left side is negative exactly when the covariance reduction exceeds
$N\|b_U\|_2^2$, which is \eqref{eq:crossover}.
\end{proof}

The exact crossover still contains estimator-specific moments.  One-sided
upper truncation makes those moments interpretable.  For $c>0$, define
\begin{equation}
  U_c:=\min(W_\theta,c),
  \qquad
  T_c:=(W_\theta-c)_+,
  \qquad
  \tau_c:=\E_Q[T_c].
  \label{eq:upper-truncation}
\end{equation}
The cap changes only ratios above $c$, while $\tau_c$ measures how much target
mass those changes remove.

\begin{corollary}[Bias and MSE of upper ratio truncation]
\label{cor:upper-truncation}
Suppose $\|H_\theta\|_2\le H_{\max}$ almost surely.  The detached estimator
$\widehat g_c=N^{-1}\sum_i U_{c,i}H_{\theta,i}$ satisfies
\begin{align}
  b_c&=-\E_Q[T_cH_\theta],
  &\|b_c\|_2&\le H_{\max}\tau_c,
  \label{eq:cap-bias}\\
  \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat g_c)
  &\le H_{\max}^2\left(\tau_c^2+\frac{c}{N}\right).
  \label{eq:cap-mse-bound}
\end{align}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The coefficient difference is $U_c-W_\theta=-T_c$.  Substitution into
\eqref{eq:bias-covariance} gives
\begin{align}
  b_c
  &=\E_Q[(U_c-W_\theta)H_\theta]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-1}\\
  &=-\E_Q[T_cH_\theta].
    \label{eq:cap-proof-2}
\end{align}
The triangle inequality and the bound on $H_\theta$ then give
\begin{align}
  \|b_c\|_2
  &\le\E_Q[T_c\|H_\theta\|_2]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-3}\\
  &\le H_{\max}\E_Q[T_c]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-4}\\
  &=H_{\max}\tau_c.
    \label{eq:cap-proof-5}
\end{align}
For the covariance term, use the trace identity and remove its nonnegative
mean-squared term:
\begin{align}
  \operatorname{tr}\{\Cov_Q(U_cH_\theta)\}
  &=\E_Q[U_c^2\|H_\theta\|_2^2]
    -\|\E_Q[U_cH_\theta]\|_2^2
    \label{eq:cap-proof-6}\\
  &\le H_{\max}^2\E_Q[U_c^2]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-7}\\
  &\le cH_{\max}^2\E_Q[W_\theta]
    \label{eq:cap-proof-8}\\
  &=cH_{\max}^2.
    \label{eq:cap-proof-9}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cap-proof-7} uses the bound on $H_\theta$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:cap-proof-8} uses $U_c^2\le cW_\theta$, which holds both
when $W_\theta\le c$ and when $W_\theta>c$.  The final line uses
$\E_Q[W_\theta]=1$.  Combining \eqref{eq:two-risks},
\eqref{eq:cap-proof-5}, and \eqref{eq:cap-proof-9} proves
\eqref{eq:cap-mse-bound}.
\end{proof}

The bound explains why a moderately large cap can be useful.  It replaces the
raw second-moment dependence by $c/N$, while its bias is small when the tail
excess $\tau_c$ is small.  Caps such as 3 or 5 are therefore candidates to test,
not universal constants.  Truncated importance ratios also appear in ACER with
bias correction \citep{wang2017acer} and in the V-trace correction used by
IMPALA \citep{espeholt2018impala}; recent LLM methods likewise control extreme
weight moments \citep{zheng2025m2po,fakoor2026p3o}.  These precedents motivate
the intervention, while \Cref{cor:upper-truncation} states the bias quantity that
must be measured for this estimator.

The ESS identity and crossover produce a conditional conclusion.  When $G_{2,\theta}$ is
controlled, high ESS keeps the raw ratio-driven error small and leaves little
variance for clipping to remove.  Falling ESS enlarges the ratio second moment
and can make variance reduction worth its bias.  But ESS cannot determine the
sign of \eqref{eq:crossover}, because the decision also depends on
$G_{2,\theta}$ and on what the clipping rule removes.
The claim is therefore not ``clip below a universal ESS,'' but ``ESS locates
the regime in which the clipping MSE comparison becomes consequential.''

\subsection{Why MSE matters for optimization}

The crossover compares errors relative to the population gradient.  The next
result shows why this quantity matters for a standard first-order update.

\begin{proposition}[MSE-controlled stationarity]
\label{prop:stationarity}
Suppose $J$ is $L$-smooth and bounded above by $J_{\max}$.  For
$\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k+\eta\widehat g_k$ with $0<\eta\le 1/L$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac1T\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\E\|\nabla J(\theta_k)\|_2^2
  \le
  \frac{2\{J_{\max}-\E J(\theta_0)\}}{\eta T}
  +\frac1T\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}
   \E\|\widehat g_k-\nabla J(\theta_k)\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:stationarity}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $g_k:=\nabla J(\theta_k)$ and $e_k:=\widehat g_k-g_k$.  The ascent form of
$L$-smoothness gives
\begin{align}
  J(\theta_{k+1})-J(\theta_k)
  &\ge g_k^\top(\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k)
    -\frac L2\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k\|_2^2
    \label{eq:stationarity-proof-1}\\
  &=\eta g_k^\top\widehat g_k
    -\frac{L\eta^2}{2}\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2
    \label{eq:stationarity-proof-2}\\
  &=\frac\eta2\|g_k\|_2^2
    +\frac\eta2\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2
    -\frac\eta2\|e_k\|_2^2
    -\frac{L\eta^2}{2}\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2
    \label{eq:stationarity-proof-3}\\
  &=\frac\eta2\|g_k\|_2^2
    +\frac\eta2(1-L\eta)\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2
    -\frac\eta2\|e_k\|_2^2
    \label{eq:stationarity-proof-4}\\
  &\ge\frac\eta2\|g_k\|_2^2
    -\frac\eta2\|e_k\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:stationarity-proof-5}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:stationarity-proof-2} substitutes the update.  Equation
\eqref{eq:stationarity-proof-3} uses
$2g_k^\top\widehat g_k=\|g_k\|_2^2+\|\widehat g_k\|_2^2-
\|e_k\|_2^2$.  The last line uses $\eta\le1/L$.

Rearranging \eqref{eq:stationarity-proof-5}, taking expectations, and summing
over $k$ gives
\begin{align}
  \frac\eta2\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\E\|g_k\|_2^2
  &\le \E[J(\theta_T)-J(\theta_0)]
    +\frac\eta2\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\E\|e_k\|_2^2
    \label{eq:stationarity-proof-6}\\
  &\le J_{\max}-\E J(\theta_0)
    +\frac\eta2\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\E\|e_k\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:stationarity-proof-7}
\end{align}
The second line uses the upper bound on $J$.  Dividing by $\eta T/2$ yields
\eqref{eq:stationarity}.
\end{proof}

Thus clipping improves the statistical term in this stationarity guarantee
exactly when \eqref{eq:crossover} holds.  The proposition does not analyze Adam
or imply globally ordered RL trajectories; it supplies the limited optimization
bridge required by the estimator comparison.  This use of MSE is consistent
with broader analyses of biased stochastic gradients
\cite{driggs2022biased,tang2022biased}, while persistent clipping bias can still
create an error floor \cite{ajalloeian2020biased,koloskova2023clipping}.

The theoretical comparison is pointwise and uses population moments.  The next
section identifies what must be estimated before it can guide an online choice.

\section{From theory to a practical ESS gate}
\label{sec:practice}

Population ESS is unavailable during training.  Its plug-in estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\rho
  :=\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^N W_i)^2}{N\sum_{i=1}^N W_i^2}.
  \label{eq:sample-ess}
\end{equation}
It should be computed from raw, pre-clipping sequence ratios.  A candidate gate
uses the low-bias objective while $\widehat\rho$ is high and switches to a
bounded objective when it falls below a calibrated threshold.

This rule is a hypothesis, not a consequence of
\Cref{thm:crossover}.  First, sample ESS can miss unobserved tail mass.  Second,
the same batch usually determines both the gate and the gradient.  Third,
a token-local surrogate is not algebraically equal to
\eqref{eq:raw-estimator}.  Recent LLM RL methods
already adapt updates using ESS, weight moments, or sequence reshaping
\cite{zheng2025m2po,shen2026vespo,huang2026vcpo,fakoor2026p3o}; our distinct
empirical question is whether their relevant regime change coincides with the
gradient-MSE crossover in \eqref{eq:crossover}.

\paragraph{Candidate rule.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item Aggressive estimator: use the detached raw sequence ratio $W_i$ in
\eqref{eq:raw-estimator}.
\item Conservative estimator: replace $W_i$ by the detached coefficient
$\min(W_i,c)$, with $c\in\{3,5\}$ treated as a calibration choice.
\item Calibration: choose a gate on held-out runs by locating the measured MSE
crossover, then monitor raw $\widehat\rho$, estimated tail excess, and a
weighted score-scale proxy.  Freeze both the threshold and cap before transfer.
\end{itemize}

The rule makes the estimator choice reproducible without claiming a universal
ESS threshold.  The controlled study below tests the identities that motivate
it before considering an LLM-scale gate.

\section{Controlled contextual-bandit validation}
\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{Testbed and protocol.}
We use a linear contextual bandit, a canonical controlled testbed for contextual
learning \citep{chu2011contextual}.  The environment contains 96 fixed contexts,
8 actions, and 6 features.  Every context has norm at most one, and each
Bernoulli reward mean is $0.5+0.4x^\top\beta_a\in[0.1,0.9]$.  A softmax behavior
policy generates batches of $N=32$, while a target-policy path increases
coverage mismatch.  Because both sets are finite, we enumerate $\rho_\theta$,
$G_{2,\theta}$, the population gradient, bias, covariance, and MSE exactly.
We evaluate the raw estimator and upper caps 3 and 5 at 19 target policies.  The
released script fixes all seeds and regenerates every reported number and plot.

\paragraph{Exact estimator results.}
The raw-MSE expression in \eqref{eq:ess-bridge} matches direct covariance
enumeration to a maximum absolute error of $1.74\times10^{-18}$.  As coverage
falls, caps 3 and 5 become preferable at different points because their
variance reductions and bias costs differ.  At $\rho=0.273$, cap 3 reduces MSE
by $31.6\%$ and cap 5 reduces it by $10.6\%$.  The squared bias of cap 5 is only
$3.45\times10^{-7}$, consistent with the tail-excess argument in
\Cref{cor:upper-truncation}.  Raw MSE is not monotone in ESS along this path
because $G_{2,\theta}$ also decreases.  This behavior validates the paper's
central qualification: ESS governs ratio concentration, but $G_{2,\theta}$
determines how that concentration enters the gradient.

\paragraph{Matched-batch intervention.}
For each target policy, we also draw 2,000 paired batches, fork identical policy
states, and reuse each batch for eight raw, cap-3, or cap-5 updates.  At
$\rho=0.273$, the estimated probabilities of decreasing population reward are
$0.190$, $0.195$, and $0.192$, respectively, with overlapping intervals in
\Cref{fig:bandit}.  Mean reward improvements are $0.00800$, $0.00684$, and
$0.00758$.  Thus the exact MSE crossover is validated, but this multi-step reward
comparison does not establish that truncation improves the adaptive trajectory.
That distinction leads directly to the required LLM study.

\paragraph{What remains for LLMs.}
Exact full-gradient MSE is mathematically well defined for an LLM, but a
population reference requires an impractically large independent rollout and
backpropagation budget.  A feasible approximation can compare selected layers
or random gradient projections against a large fresh-rollout reference.  The
matched-state intervention is also feasible, but expensive: checkpoint the
model, optimizer, and batch, fork candidate updates, then evaluate fresh
rollouts.  These two LLM tests, followed by held-out threshold transfer, remain
